## A Large Cabinet in a Presidential System

The formation of the Merah Putih Cabinet in October 2024 brought together **presidential authority and the need for political support**. The president chooses ministers, while government also works with a multiparty parliament. A coalition may support effective government while creating a risk that appointments reward political connections.

This article examines the initial cabinet, not the current roster after reshuffles. Size is a starting point for questions about responsibilities, coordination, competence, and oversight. The number of appointments alone neither proves patronage nor establishes performance.

## Counting the Initial Composition Correctly

Prabowo announced the cabinet on **20 October 2024**. Ministers were inaugurated on 21 October. Announcement and inauguration are different events, so both the date and type of office need to be identified accurately.

| Category | Initial number | How to interpret it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coordinating ministers | 7 | Included in the total of 48 ministers |
| Other ministers | 41 | Together with coordinating ministers, head 48 ministries |
| All ministers | 48 | Does not mean 53 ministries |
| Ministerial-rank officials inaugurated on 21 October | 5 | Distinct from heads of ministries |
| Deputy-minister positions in the 20 October announcement | 56 | Should not be added to the ministry count |

These figures refer to the initial announcement and inauguration recorded by the Cabinet Secretariat. Comparisons need equivalent units, such as ministries against ministries. Adding ministers, deputies, and equivalent-rank officials, then comparing that sum with an earlier administration's ministry count, would mislead.

Classification requires the same care. Does “experienced” mean previously a minister, previously a deputy, or retained in the same position? Without a definition and a list of the people counted, a percentage may look precise while remaining impossible to verify.

## Presidential Authority and the Legal Amendment

Article 17 of the Constitution gives the president authority to **appoint and dismiss ministers**. Law No. 39 of 2008 also governs ministries and was amended by Law No. 61 of 2024. That amendment, dated 15 October 2024, belongs in an assessment of the new cabinet.

The amended framework gives the president greater flexibility to determine ministry numbers according to governmental needs. Authority to choose a structure, however, is not proof that each additional ministry is necessary. That need can still be assessed through responsibilities, workloads, costs, and service outcomes.

Party members serving as ministers do not automatically contradict presidential government. The constitutional appointment power remains with the president. A more precise criticism asks whether coalition considerations displace capability, integrity, and accountability.

## Coalition Support and Patronage Risks

In a multiparty parliament, **coalition support** can help with legislation and budgets. Political support may also come with demands for appointments. These possibilities require examination of actual conduct, rather than an inference from the number of parties alone.

| Consideration | Possible benefit | Risk to scrutinize |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Party support | Easier political coordination with parliament | Appointments treated as rewards for support |
| Splitting ministries | Focused responsibility for particular issues | Overlapping duties and higher coordination costs |
| Experienced officeholders | Retained knowledge of programs | Reappointment without performance evaluation |
| Professionals outside parties | Relevant expertise | A professional label concealing business conflicts |

“Politician” and “professional” are not mutually exclusive categories. A party member may possess expertise. A non-member may have commercial interests. Assessment needs to examine each officeholder's work and interests.

## Integrity and the Status of Allegations

In its October 2024 statement, Indonesia Corruption Watch criticized cabinet size, background scrutiny, and potential conflicts of interest. **ICW's criticism is an attributed watchdog assessment**. It should not be rewritten as a finding that every person discussed has been convicted of corruption.

A witness, a person mentioned in proceedings, a suspect, a defendant, and a convicted person have different statuses. Being named in a case is not a guilty verdict. Likewise, disputes about academic qualifications require decisions by competent institutions, rather than inferences from study duration or social-media attention.

These distinctions strengthen demands for integrity. The president can be asked to explain background checks, selection reasons, and conflict-management arrangements. Verifiable standards are more useful than an allegation list that merges different legal statuses.

## Expertise as a Basis for Cabinet Appointments

A **zaken cabinet** generally emphasizes relevant expertise in appointments. It is not a legal category automatically reached when a specified share of ministers comes from outside parties. Figures such as 60% or 70% should not be presented as a universal threshold without a clear definition and basis.

Historical examples also need their governmental context. The Natsir and Wilopo cabinets belonged to the parliamentary period and should not be described as presidential cabinets for a direct comparison. Selecting capable officeholders remains relevant, but their relationship with parliament was different.

Competence involves political coordination, priorities, technical knowledge, and public accountability.

| Dimension | Assessment question |
| --- | --- |
| Political | Can the minister build support while maintaining public duties? |
| Strategic | Can the minister set priorities and allocate responsibilities? |
| Technical and ethical | Is there relevant experience and a way to manage conflicts? |
| Public | Can citizens assess targets and results? |

The framework does not assume that one background is always superior. Ministers need to demonstrate that expertise and leadership produce usable public services.

## Performance as the Final Measure

A large cabinet risks **costs and coordination burdens disproportionate to results**. Splitting responsibilities may clarify accountability, but it can also add meetings, data transfers, and jurisdictional disputes. Assessment should compare cost changes with service times and program outcomes.

Oversight of [private interests in public office](/en/articles/politics/nepotism-in-political-governance) must continue after inauguration. Initial screening is insufficient if later budget, procurement, and appointment decisions remain opaque.

Forming a cabinet begins accountability. It does not demonstrate success. Government needs to explain why its structure is needed, who is responsible, and when results can be evaluated. Criticism of a broad coalition can then move from suspicion to measurable assessment.
