## Family Values and Public Power

Respect for family does not by itself justify **the inheritance of political power**. A problem arises when family connections close competition, direct state resources, or shield decisions from scrutiny. This article examines how “Asian values” can be used to justify political dynasties, particularly in the debate surrounding Indonesia's 2024 regional elections.

The analysis draws on literature and nomination rules. Several relatives holding office may indicate concentrated power, but it does not establish that every family member has committed a violation. How people obtain and exercise office still needs examination.

## Asian Values as a Political Argument

The term *Asian values* appears in debates about **family, harmony, and authority**. Asia, however, is not a single society with uniform values. Political traditions, religions, and colonial experiences differ across countries. A leader's claim about “Asian values” cannot therefore stand for the views of every Asian population.

Richard Robison's 1996 article *The politics of ‘Asian values’* examines how cultural claims can shield conservative and authoritarian political positions from criticism. Its analytical question remains useful: who defines those values, and whose interests receive protection?

| Invoked value | Application that supports shared life | Possible misuse to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family solidarity | Mutual care without taking away other people's rights | Favoring relatives for public appointments without fair selection |
| Harmony | Resolving disagreement through deliberation | Treating criticism as a disturbance to suppress |
| Respect for leaders | Respecting the duties of public office | Demanding obedience without accountability |
| Collective interests | Assessing policies by public benefit | Equating the ruling group's interests with everyone's interests |

The distinction is between a value and its use. Criticism of dynastic justifications need not reject family solidarity or suggest that a culture inevitably produces corruption.

## Indonesia and the New Order Legacy

Research on Soeharto's government links the language of stability, obedience, and harmony to **restrictions on criticism**. Hasanah (2019) examines the political use of Asian values and human rights norms in Indonesia from 1991 to 1999. That relationship needs to remain grounded in the institutions, policies, and periods being studied.

Calling nepotism a “natural” part of Indonesian culture obscures individual responsibility. Officials decide how to appoint people, parties organize recruitment, and oversight bodies can strengthen or weaken scrutiny. These are decisions that can change.

Personal trust may make coordination easier within a family. Applied to public appointments, the same reasoning raises additional questions: did other candidates have access, were qualifications assessed consistently, and can the decision be audited? Concrete questions reveal the boundary between private relationships and public office.

## Dynastic Politics in Regional Elections

The 2024 debate shows why **family relationships, nomination, and abuse of office** must be distinguished. Gibran Rakabuming Raka, Joko Widodo's son, stood as Prabowo Subianto's vice-presidential running mate. Before the regional elections, reporting also discussed figures from political families. Being discussed as a potential candidate did not make someone an officially registered contestant.

The debate about Kaesang Pangarep's possible candidacy, for example, must be read alongside the legal developments at the time. In its reasoning in Decision 70/PUU-XXII/2024 on 20 August 2024, the Constitutional Court emphasized that candidate requirements had to be met before candidate pairs were formally designated. Predictions made before that ruling should not be presented as the election's final outcome.

Family relationships can provide early access to networks, name recognition, and resources. A finding of unfair competition nevertheless requires evidence about how that access was used. A count of related candidates is not a count of corruption cases and cannot establish that all those candidates are corrupt.

## The Right to Run and Fair Competition

**Individual political rights** must also be protected. In Decision 33/PUU-XIII/2015, the Constitutional Court invalidated a candidacy restriction based on kinship with incumbents. A family relationship does not by itself prove shared political interests or misuse of power.

That principle does not remove the need to scrutinize incumbents. Two aims can coexist: assess relatives as individuals, and prevent state facilities, administrative authority, or budgets from being used to secure their election.

| Question | Relevant evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Is party recruitment open? | Selection criteria, candidates' records, and reasons for nomination |
| Does public office provide an improper advantage? | Use of facilities, instructions to officials, and financing records |
| Is there a conflict of interest? | Connections between decision-makers and beneficiaries, plus recusal arrangements |
| Can misconduct be addressed? | Oversight findings and decisions by competent authorities, rather than conclusions drawn from surnames |

Criticism of [nepotism in government](/en/articles/politics/nepotism-in-political-governance) becomes more precise when it identifies decisions that depart from public duties. Dynastic politics concerns the distribution of power. Nepotism and corruption require examination of particular conduct.

## What Voters and Parties Are Responsible For

Voters can demand **a record of performance and accountability** from every candidate. Family connections need not be an unexamined reason for acceptance or rejection. Programs, capability, integrity, and resource use provide a more testable basis for judgment.

Parties have an earlier responsibility: giving competent members and candidates a route to nomination. Published criteria, clear reasons for selection, and controls on conflicts of interest can reduce dependence on family networks. Election supervisors then assess misconduct through evidence and applicable procedures. Kinship does not automatically trigger a sanction.

Family values can coexist with democracy when office remains a public trust. The boundary is crossed when personal closeness replaces fair competition and the duty to explain decisions to citizens.
