The question asks for the pair in which **both words** are used figuratively. A literal sense is the word's basic meaning: a smell you can really smell, a load really on someone's shoulders. A figurative sense borrows the word for something that only resembles it. The test: see what the word applies to in its sentence, then ask whether that event really happens physically. Each pair has to pass the test twice, because one figurative word isn't enough.

Start with the seven words that appear in the options.

| Word and sentence | Applies to | Meaning in that sentence | Type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *harum* (1) | the name of Barus | famous and praised | figurative |
| *harum* (4) | camphor crystals | smelling fragrant | literal |
| *lesu* (16) | the camphor trade | slow, little buying and selling | figurative |
| *mengering* (13) | the source of camphor | the supply running out | figurative |
| *dipikul* (7) | camphor and benzoin | carried on the shoulder | literal |
| *hulu* (7) | the river | upper course of the river | literal |
| *mengeras* (4) | benzoin sap | turning solid | literal |

All three figurative senses are in KBBI. *Harum* ("fragrant") has the figurative sense "much praised, famous", as in *namanya semakin harum* ("his name grew ever more renowned"). A name can't smell, so *harum* in sentence (1) has to be figurative. *Lesu* ("weary") has the figurative sense "little buying and selling, weak and quiet (of trade)". A trade can't feel tired the way a body does. *Kering* ("dry") has the figurative sense "already used up or empty", and sentence (13) gives the cause: camphor trees grew scarce because they kept being felled.

The biggest trap is *harum*, which appears twice. In sentence (4), what's fragrant is the camphor crystal, and camphor really does have a strong smell. That's the literal sense. So you have to check the sentence number, not just the word.

Now test each pair:

- *Harum* (4) and *mengering* (13): only *mengering* is figurative. *Harum* in sentence (4) is the crystals' real smell.
- *Harum* (1) and *lesu* (16): both are figurative. This is the answer.
- *Mengering* (13) and *dipikul* (7): *dipikul* often shows up in the figure *memikul tanggung jawab* ("to shoulder a responsibility"), but in sentence (7) the load really is carried on the shoulders.
- *Lesu* (16) and *hulu* (7): *hulu* in sentence (7) is the upper part of the river, where the boats set off. That's literal.
- *Harum* (1) and *mengeras* (4): *mengeras* describes sap that really turns solid, not a figure like *sikapnya mengeras* ("his attitude hardened").

So the pair in which both words are figurative is *harum* in sentence (1) and *lesu* in sentence (16).
