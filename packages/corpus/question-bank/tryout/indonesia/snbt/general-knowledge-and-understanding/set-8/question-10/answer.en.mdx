Sentence (7) names the damp air, termites and fungi that damage palm-leaf pages, and sentence (8) mentions manuscripts that are already *rapuh* and hard to read. **Rapuh here describes material that breaks or crumbles easily**, and its opposite is material that bears load and use, *kuat* ("strong"). A palm-fibre rope that is still *kuat* after years of drawing water uses the same physical sense.

The choice *tegar* is a meaning trap. *Rapuh* is also used for a heart that wavers easily, and its opposite there is indeed tegar ("steadfast"), but sentence (8) is about manuscript pages, not feelings. A post that is *lapuk* ("rotten") points the same way as rapuh, since rot is one way things become brittle. Leaves cut *tipis* ("thin") and grass that is *kering* ("dry") only name conditions that often come with brittleness, not its opposite.
