This question has two steps. First, pin down what *membeku* ("to freeze") means in sentence (6). Second, pin down what the italic word means in each option sentence, then find the one with the opposite meaning. The same word can appear with a different sense, so don't pick an option just because the word looks right.

Step 1: the sense of *membeku* in sentence (6). The sentence reads *Tokoh-tokoh di dalam lukisan membeku dalam satu sikap, sehingga tugas menghidupkan cerita jatuh ke suara dalang* ("The figures in the painting are frozen in one *sikap*, so the job of bringing the story to life falls to the voice of the *dalang*, the storyteller").

- KBBI lists several senses of *sikap*, among them "body shape", "way of standing", and "conduct based on one's convictions", which English calls an attitude. A painted figure has no convictions that could change, so *sikap* here is the way the figure stands, its pose.
- KBBI gives *beku* the basic sense "solid or hard (of a liquid)", but also the figurative sense "not undergoing change, stiff, static". A painted figure isn't water, so the figurative sense fits. KBBI explains *statis* as "in a state of rest (not moving ...)".

So *membeku* in sentence (6) means **staying still in one pose, not changing position**. Its opposite is changing position: *bergerak* in the KBBI sense "to shift from a place or position, not just staying still".

Step 2: test each sentence.

- The dancer who only starts to *bergerak* after the drum is struck a third time: the dancer was still before, and then the body starts changing position. That's the sense "shift position, not stay still", the exact opposite of a figure held still in one pose.
- Residents who *bergerak* to clean the drains: here *bergerak* means "to start an effort, to take action", the second KBBI sense. Same word, but it's about joint action, not a body changing position. This is the strongest trap.
- Ice that *mencair* ("melts"): the opposite of the basic sense of *beku*, solid turning liquid. A painted figure isn't solid like ice.
- The village head's *sikap* that *melunak* ("softens"): here *sikap* means an attitude, and *melunak* means it's no longer harsh. This option traps you if you read *sikap* in sentence (6) as an attitude.
- Funds that are *dicairkan* ("released"): the opposite of the sense of *beku* for savings, "not yet allowed to be withdrawn". That sense is about money, not a figure's pose.

So the word whose meaning is opposite to *membeku* in sentence (6) is *bergerak* in the sentence about the dancer who only starts moving after the drum is struck a third time.
