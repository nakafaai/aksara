# Given

Helmetless riders at **e-ticket camera intersections** fell from <InlineMath math="25\%" /> to <InlineMath math="5\%" />, while police patrols also ran.

# Asked

The statement that, if true, **most strengthens** the conclusion that e-ticketing caused the drop.

# Solution

> To separate the effect of cameras from patrols, inspect intersections without cameras that still had the very same patrols.

The head of the transport office is making a cause-and-effect claim. The share did fall from <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6{,}000}{24{,}000}=25\%" /> to <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1{,}050}{21{,}000}=5\%" />, but the text hides two other possible causes. The best strengthener is the statement that **rules those other causes out**.

- The first rival cause is the police checks from <InlineMath math="9" /> to 22 March. Maybe the checks, not the cameras, got people to wear helmets.
- The second rival cause is a change of route. The number of motorcycles passing fell by <InlineMath math="24{,}000-21{,}000=3{,}000" /> a day. Maybe riders without helmets are simply avoiding the camera junctions.

Now look at the statement about junctions without cameras. Those junctions also had the police checks, yet the share of riders without helmets there stayed at <InlineMath math="25\%" />. So the checks alone didn't change the habit. The number of motorcycles there didn't rise either, so riders without helmets didn't just move over. Both rival causes are gone at once, and what's left is the electronic ticket.

The other statements don't do that.

- The figure of <InlineMath math="1{,}050" /> riders a day only repeats the table. It doesn't explain why the share fell.
- Checks that ticketed thousands of people actually support the rival cause, the checks.
- Riders who only learned about the cameras after getting a ticket fit the head's story. But that statement rules out neither the checks nor the change of route.
- Riders without helmets choosing other roads weakens the conclusion, because they still aren't wearing helmets.

# Answer

So the strongest support is the statement that at junctions without cameras that also had the checks, the share of riders without helmets stayed at about **<InlineMath math="25\%" />** and the number of motorcycles didn't rise.
