Start by naming the flaw in the developer's reasoning. Two features have to show up together.

1. The people who answer **choose for themselves to take part**. Users who exercise a lot are keener to fill in a survey about exercise, so they're over-represented.
2. The result from those respondents is **carried over to the whole group** that was invited, meaning all the app's users.

The radio station's reasoning has both features. Listeners decide for themselves whether to phone in, and people who like the show are more likely to call. Then the <InlineMath math="85\%" /> of callers is carried over to all listeners.

The other arguments have only one feature, or neither.

- The library picks <InlineMath math="100" /> members at random. Nobody selects themselves, so the conclusion is reasonable.
- The canteen also uses a sample that doesn't represent the group, but for a different reason. The manager does the choosing, through the time of asking, and the buyers don't volunteer. The canteen also swaps _buying_ for _liking_.
- The cooperative's survey is voluntary, and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{56}{70}=80\%" />. But the board only reports on the people who answered. It doesn't carry the result over to all members, so there's no leap.
- The clinic picks patients at random, then jumps to a different group altogether, the city's residents. Clinic patients aren't a random sample of the city, but that isn't about volunteering.

So the most similar reasoning is the radio station's, which infers the taste of all its listeners from the callers.
