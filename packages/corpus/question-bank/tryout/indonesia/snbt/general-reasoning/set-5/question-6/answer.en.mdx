#### Comparing totals

Compare the **three-month totals** by adding January, February, and March. The unit remains one hundred thousand users.

| Company | January | February | March | Three-month total |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| A | <InlineMath math="27" /> | <InlineMath math="31.8" /> | <InlineMath math="26.9" /> | <InlineMath math="27+31.8+26.9=85.7" /> |
| B | <InlineMath math="19.7" /> | <InlineMath math="23.2" /> | <InlineMath math="21.5" /> | <InlineMath math="19.7+23.2+21.5=64.4" /> |
| C | <InlineMath math="11.8" /> | <InlineMath math="14.9" /> | <InlineMath math="17" /> | <InlineMath math="11.8+14.9+17=43.7" /> |
| D | <InlineMath math="20.7" /> | <InlineMath math="10.8" /> | <InlineMath math="16.8" /> | <InlineMath math="20.7+10.8+16.8=48.3" /> |

The totals give the unambiguous ordering

<BlockMath math="43.7 < 48.3 < 64.4 < 85.7." />

Company C also rises every month, so the claim that every company fluctuates is false. The chart therefore supports the statement that Company C has the **lowest three-month total**.

#### Checking percentage changes

Check the **percentage-change** options as well. Company B's January-to-February increase is <InlineMath math="\frac{23.2-19.7}{19.7}\times100\%\approx17.8\%" />, and its February-to-March decrease is <InlineMath math="\frac{23.2-21.5}{23.2}\times100\%\approx7.3\%" />. Company D directly disproves both claims about B:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\text{D increase}&amp;=\frac{16.8-10.8}{10.8}\times100\%\approx55.6\%\\\text{D decrease}&amp;=\frac{20.7-10.8}{20.7}\times100\%\approx47.8\%\end{aligned}" />

D has both a larger percentage increase and a larger percentage decrease than B.
