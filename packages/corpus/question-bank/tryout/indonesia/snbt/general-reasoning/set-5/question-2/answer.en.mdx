The small-boat owners are making a cause-and-effect claim: the rafts are the cause, the falling price is the effect. A claim like that is weakest when there's evidence that **the effect still shows up without the cause**. So look for a place the rafts never touched, and see whether the price fell there too.

The price at the village fish auction (TPI) fell from <InlineMath math="20" /> to <InlineMath math="15" /> thousand rupiah per kg, a drop of <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{20}=25\%" />, or a quarter. If prices at auctions in other villages also fell by about a quarter, even though the fishers there don't use rafts and don't receive tuna from this group, the drop must have another cause that works in many places. For example, tuna may be plentiful along the whole coast. You no longer need the rafts to explain the lower price.

The other statements don't cut the link between cause and effect.

- If the auction takes no tuna from other villages, one rival cause is ruled out. That makes the owners' view stronger.
- Smaller, cheaper tuna from the rafts still means the rafts pull the average price down. Only the route is different.
- You can already work out the rise from <InlineMath math="320" /> tonnes to <InlineMath math="376" /> tonnes from the table. More supply fits the owners' view.
- An unchanged number of buyers rules out the idea that the price fell because buyers stayed away. That also strengthens the view.

So the statement that weakens it most is that tuna prices also fell by about a quarter at auctions in other villages that have nothing to do with the rafts.
