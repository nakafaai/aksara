# Given

The table compares **hourly patient arrivals and wait times** before (March) and after online queuing launched (May).

# Asked

The conclusion **most strongly supported** by the March and May data.

# Solution

> Notice how the early-morning pileup spreads across later hours in May, bringing down wait times during peak slots.

Compare **how patients spread out** and **the waiting times** in the two months. In March, patients piled up early (<InlineMath math="40" /> and <InlineMath math="35" />), then very few came near noon. In May, the patients per hour were more balanced, between <InlineMath math="13" /> and <InlineMath math="32" />. Waiting at the formerly busiest hours dropped sharply, for example from <InlineMath math="70" /> to <InlineMath math="30" /> minutes at 08.00 to 09.00.

The chart below draws how patients spread over the hours in both months. **The May line is much flatter**, so patients no longer pile up early in the morning.

The other options are not supported:

- Patients per day rose only from <InlineMath math="120" /> to <InlineMath math="130" />, far from double.
- Waiting at 11.00 to 12.00 actually rose from <InlineMath math="15" /> to <InlineMath math="20" /> minutes.

# Answer

So the best supported conclusion is **that, after online queuing began, arrivals were spread more evenly and waiting at the busiest hours fell**.
