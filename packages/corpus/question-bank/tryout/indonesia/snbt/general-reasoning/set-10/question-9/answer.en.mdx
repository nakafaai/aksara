# Given

The table shows **ice melting time dropping from <InlineMath math="60" /> to <InlineMath math="48, 40, 34, 31" /> minutes** for each <InlineMath math="5^\circ\text{C}" /> rise in temperature.

# Asked

The statement **most strongly supported** by the table data.

# Solution

> Check the step-by-step differences to see that ice melts faster at higher heat, yet each five-degree jump shaves off fewer minutes than the last.

Work out **the drop in time** for each <InlineMath math="5^\circ\text{C}" /> rise.

<BlockMath math="60-48=12,\quad 48-40=8,\quad 40-34=6,\quad 34-31=3" />

The chart below draws the melting time for all five temperatures. **The line keeps falling but flattens out**, because the drops shrink from <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="3" /> minutes.

The melting time keeps falling, so the ice melts faster. But the drops get smaller, so each rise in temperature matters less.

The other options are not supported:

- The drops are <InlineMath math="12" />, <InlineMath math="8" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, and <InlineMath math="3" />, so they are not equal.
- The data stop at <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />, so the time at <InlineMath math="45^\circ\text{C}" /> cannot be certain.
- Half of <InlineMath math="60" /> is <InlineMath math="30" />, but the data show <InlineMath math="31" />.
- The melting time clearly changes with temperature.

# Answer

So the best supported statement is that the higher the temperature, the faster the ice melts, but each **<InlineMath math="5^\circ\text{C}" />** rise has a smaller effect.
