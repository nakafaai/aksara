# Given

Serving the same neighborhood, **Cendana Market gained <InlineMath math="600" /> visitors** while **Kenari Market lost <InlineMath math="300" /> visitors**.

# Asked

The **most reasonable conclusion** supported by data from both markets.

# Solution

> When one market surges while the neighboring market drops at the same time, part of the gain likely came from shoppers switching over.

Look at **both markets together**, not only Pasar Cendana. Visitors to Cendana rose by <InlineMath math="600" />, but visitors to Kenari fell by <InlineMath math="300" />. Both markets serve the same neighborhood, so some shoppers likely moved to the renovated one.

<BlockMath math="\text{before: }1{,}200+1{,}500=2{,}700,\qquad \text{after: }1{,}800+1{,}200=3{,}000" />

The net rise is only <InlineMath math="300" />, half of the <InlineMath math="600" /> the official claimed.

The chart below puts both markets and their total side by side. **The total bar rises by only <InlineMath math="300" />**, because Kenari lost <InlineMath math="300" /> visitors while Cendana gained <InlineMath math="600" />.

The other options are not supported:

- The figure <InlineMath math="600" /> ignores the drop at Kenari.
- The data say nothing about prices at Kenari.
- Going from <InlineMath math="2{,}700" /> to <InlineMath math="3{,}000" /> is far from double.
- The total still rose by <InlineMath math="300" />, so "no effect at all" does not fit.

# Answer

So the best supported conclusion is that part of Cendana's rise may come from Kenari.
