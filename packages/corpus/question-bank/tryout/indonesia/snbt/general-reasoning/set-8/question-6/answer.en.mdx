# Given

The principal concludes **more self-study hours raise exam scores**, while the table also tracks **tutoring enrollment** across classes.

# Asked

The detail in the table that most shows **the causal claim is not yet certain**.

# Solution

> When a second factor like tutoring rises hand in hand with self-study hours, you cannot tell which one actually boosted the scores.

The principal's conclusion states **a cause**: self-study raises scores. To cast doubt on it, look for another factor in the table that **rises together** with study hours and could also raise scores.

The share of students in tutoring rises with self-study hours: <InlineMath math="20\%" /> in the class with <InlineMath math="4" /> hours up to <InlineMath math="80\%" /> in the class with <InlineMath math="10" /> hours. Higher scores could come from tutoring, from self-study, or from both. The table cannot separate them.

The chart below draws two columns of the table after sorting the classes by self-study hours. **Both lines rise together**, so the table can't separate the effect of self-study from that of tutoring.

The other options do not cast doubt:

- The gap in hours between XII-4 and XII-2 is just part of the same pattern.
- XII-1's lowest score fits its fewest study hours.
- Writing the scores as whole numbers does not change the pattern in the table.
- The unit of measurement does not change the link between study hours and scores.

# Answer

So the information that best shows the conclusion is not settled is that classes with many self-study hours also have many students in tutoring.
