The question wants an argument with the same mistake, not the same topic. So the first step is to name the researcher's mistake precisely.

The researcher only looks at old houses that are **still standing**. But whether a house lasts a hundred years depends on its wood. Teak (*jati*) is durable and resists termites, while houses made of less durable wood have rotted away. The houses left today have been filtered, and the filter favors teak houses.

Try some example numbers. Say there were <InlineMath math="100" /> houses a hundred years ago, and only <InlineMath math="30" /> were teak. If <InlineMath math="24" /> teak houses and <InlineMath math="2" /> other houses are still standing, then:

<BlockMath math="\frac{24}{24 + 2} = \frac{24}{26} \approx 92\%" />

Almost every house left really is teak, even though only <InlineMath math="30\%" /> of the houses back then were. This mistake is called **survivorship bias**: drawing a conclusion about everything from the past using only the things that survived, when the feature being studied helps decide what survives.

Now match each option against that pattern.

- The old regional songs follow exactly the same pattern. Only songs that are still sung get looked at. Simple, catchy songs are easier to pass on by word of mouth, while complicated ones get forgotten faster. So the songs left today don't represent all the songs of that time. Same mistake.
- The port records look similar because they're also about wood and the past. The difference is that the records list every ship that docked, not just ships that still exist. There's no filter, so the conclusion fits the data.
- Neighborhood watch posts and theft get the direction of cause and effect wrong. Residents may have built lots of watch posts precisely because theft in their area was high. That's a different mistake.
- Compost for mangoes and then chilies uses a weak analogy. Mangoes and chilies are different plants with different needs, so what worked for one may not work for the other. That's a different mistake.
- Sugary drinks and exam scores treat two things that happened one after the other as cause and effect. Scores could have risen for some other reason that same year. That's a different mistake too.

So the argument whose mistake is most similar is the conclusion that regional songs from before independence were mostly simple, drawn only from the songs that are still sung today.
