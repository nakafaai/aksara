The department head is making a cause-and-effect claim: the concrete barriers made crashes on road segments drop. A claim like that is weakest when **a drop just as large also happens without barriers**. So you first need to know how big the drop in the four corridors really was, then compare each option with it.

First, measure the drop on the four corridors' road segments as risk per trip, because trips rose from <InlineMath math="400{,}000" /> to <InlineMath math="1{,}000{,}000" />. Count per <InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> trips.

The count did fall by half, but the risk per trip **fell to one fifth**. That's the number that needs explaining.

Second, compare each option with that one-fifth figure.

- Unprotected road segments whose risk also fell to about one fifth show that a drop that large happened without barriers too. Then the barriers aren't needed to explain the drop in the four corridors, and the cause-and-effect link the department head claims is cut.
- Unprotected road segments whose crash count halved with trips unchanged only had their risk halved. The four corridors fell much more sharply, to one fifth. That extra drop could still come from the barriers, so this option only weakens the claim a little. It's tempting because "half" matches the department head's own words.
- The old trend from <InlineMath math="60" /> to <InlineMath math="30" /> with roughly the same trips means the risk halved over two years, from <InlineMath math="1.5" /> to <InlineMath math="0.75" />. Even if the risk halved again within a single year, the 2025 risk would be about <InlineMath math="0.375" />, still far above <InlineMath math="0.15" />. The trend only explains a small part of the drop.
- The new speed limit only applies at intersections. The claim is about road segments, so this change offers no other cause for them. Besides, the risk at the four corridors' intersections actually went up.
- New cyclists who aren't used to traffic tend to crash more easily. If the risk still fell with many beginners on the road, the department head's claim actually looks stronger.

So the statement that most weakens the department head's view is that the risk per trip on unprotected road segments also fell to about one fifth.
