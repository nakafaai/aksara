# Given

**The text and table** cover cycling trips and crashes solely inside the four concrete-barrier corridors.

# Asked

Which statements can and cannot be concluded from the four-corridor data alone.

# Solution

> Data from four corridors alone can never prove claims about the whole city or unmeasured roads without barriers.

A statement can be concluded (*dapat disimpulkan*) when the text and the table are enough to prove it in every possible case. A statement that needs data from outside the four corridors, such as figures for the whole city or for streets without barriers, **can't be concluded** (*tidak dapat disimpulkan*), even if part of it is correct.

For the serious-injury figures, remember that the last row is part of the crashes on road segments and at intersections, but the table doesn't split it by location.

First statement: can't be concluded. Its first part is right, because trips rose from <InlineMath math="400{,}000" /> to <InlineMath math="1{,}000{,}000" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{1{,}000{,}000 - 400{,}000}{400{,}000} \times 100\% = 150\%" />

But its second part compares with the whole city, and the table has no figures at all for cycling outside the four corridors. The correct number at the start makes the statement sound true, yet the comparison has nothing to compare against.

Second statement: can be concluded. In 2025 there were <InlineMath math="12" /> serious-injury crashes, but you don't know how many happened at intersections. Take the worst case, where all <InlineMath math="12" /> happened at intersections.

<BlockMath math="60 - 12 = 48" />

Even in the worst case there are still <InlineMath math="48" /> intersection crashes without serious injuries. In any other case there are more. So at least <InlineMath math="48" /> is **certain**, even though the statement sounds as if it needs data you don't have.

Third statement: can be concluded. Count per <InlineMath math="100{,}000" /> trips, which gives <InlineMath math="4" /> blocks in 2024 and <InlineMath math="10" /> blocks in 2025.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{2024: } \frac{10}{4} &= 2.5 \\ \text{2025: } \frac{12}{10} &= 1.2 \\ \text{half of the 2024 risk: } \frac{2.5}{2} &= 1.25 \end{aligned}" />

The 2025 risk is <InlineMath math="1.2" />, just under <InlineMath math="1.25" />. Someone who only sees <InlineMath math="10" /> becoming <InlineMath math="12" /> would think the risk went up.

The chart below compares the risk of a serious-injury crash in both years. **The bar for the latest year falls just below the line at half the earlier risk**, even though the count rose from <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="12" />.

Fourth statement: can't be concluded. You can work out the risk in the four corridors, but the table has no data for streets without barriers, so this comparison has nothing to compare against.

# Answer

So **the second and third statements can be concluded, while the first and fourth can't, because they need a comparison group that the table doesn't have**.
