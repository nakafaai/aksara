You're looking for the conclusion that must be true in every situation that fits all three premises. Two words decide everything: *hanya* ("only") in premise 1 and *sebagian* ("some", meaning at least one) in premise 3.

Start with premise 1. "Only students who have passed the swimming test may join the rafting trip" means anyone allowed to join **must have passed the swimming test**. It runs from "allowed to join" to "passed", not the other way. Passing is a requirement, but it isn't necessarily enough.

Now build the chain.

- The contrapositive of premise 1: a student who hasn't passed the swimming test may not join the rafting trip. A contrapositive flips the direction and negates both sides, and it always means the same as the original.
- Premise 2: no class X (grade 10) student has passed the swimming test. Combined with the step above, no class X student may join the rafting trip.
- Premise 3: at least one member of the nature club is also a class X student. That member may not join the rafting trip.

<BlockMath math="\text{club member in class X} \Rightarrow \text{hasn't passed} \Rightarrow \text{may not join}" />

So at least one person is both a nature club member and not allowed to join the rafting trip. A sentence "some <InlineMath math="A" /> are <InlineMath math="B" />" only says someone belongs to both groups, so you can swap the two groups. That gives: some students who may not join the rafting trip are members of the nature club.

The other options aren't guaranteed, and each one fails in a situation that still fits all three premises.

- "No club member may join" turns "some" into "all". A club member who isn't in class X may well have passed the swimming test and been allowed to join.
- "Everyone who passed the swimming test may join" reverses the direction of "only". A student could pass and still not be allowed, say for another reason.
- "Some club members may join" reads "some" as "some yes, some no". Every club member could be in class X, and then nobody in the club may join.
- "Every club member not in class X may join" negates premise 2 and reverses premise 1 at once. Not being in class X doesn't mean passing the swimming test, and passing still doesn't guarantee permission.

So the valid conclusion is that some students who may not join the rafting trip are members of the nature club.
