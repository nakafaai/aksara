# Given

**The clinic head compares** full immunization rates of <InlineMath math="85\%" /> for SMS-enrolled parents against <InlineMath math="60\%" /> for unenrolled parents.

# Asked

Which finding most strengthens the conclusion that the SMS reminders caused the jump.

# Solution

> Showing that the same enrolled parents only reached <InlineMath math="60\%" /> before SMS existed rules out the excuse that they were always more diligent.

The health center head compares two groups in the same year: babies of parents who got the text messages, <InlineMath math="255 : 300 = 85\%" /> fully immunized, and babies of parents who didn't register, <InlineMath math="120 : 200 = 60\%" /> fully immunized. From that gap, the head concludes that the reminders raised coverage.

First, look for another cause that could produce the same gap. The text says registering a phone number was voluntary, so parents **chose their own group**. Parents who bothered to register might simply care more about immunization from the start. If so, they'd reach higher coverage even without ever getting a text. The best strengthener is the statement that rules this out.

Second, work out one number that several options depend on: coverage for all babies in 2025.

<BlockMath math="\frac{255 + 120}{300 + 200} = \frac{375}{500} = 75\%" />

Third, test the options that look strongest.

- The older children of registered parents, before any texts, were only about <InlineMath math="60\%" /> fully immunized. That matches the group that didn't register. So registered parents **weren't more diligent to begin with**, and their children's coverage only jumped to <InlineMath math="85\%" /> once the texts started. The explanation "they just care more" drops out.
- The older children of parents who didn't register were also about <InlineMath math="60\%" />. This looks similar but measures the wrong group. It only shows the group without texts stayed stable, and says nothing about whether the registered group was already higher before the texts.
- Coverage for all babies of <InlineMath math="75\%" /> in 2024 sounds supportive, since <InlineMath math="85\%" /> is higher. But coverage for all babies in 2025 is also <InlineMath math="75\%" />. If the subdistrict's coverage didn't change, the gap between <InlineMath math="85\%" /> and <InlineMath math="60\%" /> could just come from parents sorting themselves into two groups. This statement actually weakens the conclusion.
- Living the same distance away rules out only one other cause, distance. Registered parents could still care more for other reasons.
- Parents reading the texts only shows the message arrived. Reading a message doesn't mean it changed what anyone did.

The chart below puts the older children's coverage next to the two 2025 groups. **Before any texts, the families who later registered were also only about <InlineMath math="60\%" />**, the same as the families who didn't register.

# Answer

So the statement that most strengthens the conclusion is that the older children of registered parents, immunized before any texts, were only about <InlineMath math="60\%" /> fully immunized.
