# Given

**Three contestants have distinct positive integer scores** averaging <InlineMath math="6" />, accompanied by two clues on the lowest score and the range.

# Asked

Whether statement (1) and statement (2) alone or together suffice to answer uniquely.

# Solution

> In data sufficiency, always test each clue on its own first to see whether exactly one valid score triple survives.

A data sufficiency question doesn't ask you to answer the question. It asks whether the information **leads to exactly one answer**. Check each statement on its own first, and only combine them if you need to.

The average of the three scores is <InlineMath math="6" />, so their sum is <InlineMath math="18" />. Name the scores from smallest to largest: <InlineMath math="a<b<c" />. They're different positive whole numbers with sum to <InlineMath math="18" />. The question asks for <InlineMath math="c" />.

Check statement (1). If <InlineMath math="a=5" />, the rest adds up to <InlineMath math="b+c=13" />. At first glance that looks like too little, since two numbers are still unknown. But the order locks the answer: <InlineMath math="b" /> has to be more than <InlineMath math="5" />, and <InlineMath math="c" /> has to be more than <InlineMath math="b" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
b=6 &\quad\Rightarrow\quad c=13-6=7, \text{ and } 7>6 \text{ (works)} \\
b\ge7 &\quad\Rightarrow\quad c\le13-7=6<b \text{ (fails)}
\end{aligned}" />

The only possibility is <InlineMath math="5,\ 6,\ 7" />, so <InlineMath math="c=7" />. Statement (1) alone is enough.

Check statement (2). Since the scores are different, the gap <InlineMath math="c-a" /> is at least <InlineMath math="2" />. So you only need to check gaps of <InlineMath math="2" />, <InlineMath math="3" />, and <InlineMath math="4" />, with every possible place for <InlineMath math="b" /> between <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="c" />.

| Scores | Sum | Sum equal to <InlineMath math="18" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+1,\ a+2" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+3" /> | <InlineMath math="a=5" />, scores <InlineMath math="5,\ 6,\ 7" /> |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+1,\ a+3" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+4" /> | no whole <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+2,\ a+3" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+5" /> | no whole <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+1,\ a+4" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+5" /> | no whole <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+2,\ a+4" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+6" /> | <InlineMath math="a=4" />, scores <InlineMath math="4,\ 6,\ 8" /> |
| <InlineMath math="a,\ a+3,\ a+4" /> | <InlineMath math="3a+7" /> | no whole <InlineMath math="a" /> |

There are two possibilities, <InlineMath math="5,\ 6,\ 7" /> and <InlineMath math="4,\ 6,\ 8" />. The top score could be <InlineMath math="7" /> or <InlineMath math="8" />, so statement (2) alone isn't enough.

The other options come from stopping the check too early:

- "Both statements together are enough" is what you'd pick if you treat <InlineMath math="b+c=13" /> as having many answers and forget that <InlineMath math="5<b<c" /> leaves only <InlineMath math="6" /> and <InlineMath math="7" />.
- "Each statement alone is enough" is what you'd pick if you find <InlineMath math="5,\ 6,\ 7" /> for statement (2) and stop, although <InlineMath math="4,\ 6,\ 8" /> works too.
- "Statement (2) alone is enough" swaps both of the judgements above.
- "Neither statement is enough" ignores that different whole numbers in a fixed order can pin down the answer.

# Answer

So statement (1) alone is enough to answer the question, but statement (2) alone isn't.
