The two kinds of hamper compete for the stock in opposite ways. The *Parsel Sirup* (syrup hamper) uses little cake but a lot of syrup, while the *Parsel Kue* (cake hamper) uses little syrup but a lot of cake. So making mostly one kind uses up one item and leaves the other behind. The largest total usually sits at **a mix that nearly uses up both items**, and since hamper counts must be whole numbers, that mix has to be checked carefully.

Let <InlineMath math="x" /> be the number of syrup hampers and <InlineMath math="y" /> the number of cake hampers. The conditions are:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{jars of cake} &: x+4y\le44 \\
\text{bottles of syrup} &: 2x+y\le28 \\
\text{minimum} &: x\ge3,\ y\ge3
\end{aligned}" />

The clearest method is to test a target total <InlineMath math="T=x+y" />. Replace <InlineMath math="y" /> with <InlineMath math="T-x" /> in both stock conditions.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{syrup} &: 2x+(T-x)\le28 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x\le28-T \\
\text{cake} &: x+4(T-x)\le44 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x\ge\frac{4T-44}{3}
\end{aligned}" />

Try <InlineMath math="T=19" />. The syrup condition needs <InlineMath math="x\le9" />, but the cake condition needs <InlineMath math="x\ge\frac{32}{3}\approx10.7" />. No number fits both, so <InlineMath math="19" /> hampers is impossible. For any larger <InlineMath math="T" />, the upper limit drops and the lower limit rises, so those are impossible too.

Try <InlineMath math="T=18" />. The syrup condition needs <InlineMath math="x\le10" />, and the cake condition needs <InlineMath math="x\ge\frac{28}{3}\approx9.3" />. The only whole number in between is <InlineMath math="x=10" />, so <InlineMath math="y=8" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{jars of cake} &: 10+4(8) = 42 \le 44 \\
\text{bottles of syrup} &: 2(10)+8 = 28 \le 28
\end{aligned}" />

Both kinds are also at least <InlineMath math="3" />. So <InlineMath math="10" /> syrup hampers and <InlineMath math="8" /> cake hampers give <InlineMath math="18" />, and no arrangement gives more.

The other options come from strategies that move too fast:

- <InlineMath math="13" /> comes from making <InlineMath math="3" /> syrup hampers and then as many cake hampers as possible. The cake only covers <InlineMath math="10" /> cake hampers, while plenty of syrup is left over.
- <InlineMath math="15" /> comes from making <InlineMath math="3" /> cake hampers and then as many syrup hampers as possible because they hold fewer items. The syrup only covers <InlineMath math="12" /> syrup hampers, while <InlineMath math="20" /> jars of cake are left over.
- <InlineMath math="17" /> comes from the crossing point of <InlineMath math="x+4y=44" /> and <InlineMath math="2x+y=28" />, which is <InlineMath math="x\approx9.7" /> and <InlineMath math="y\approx8.6" />, rounded down to <InlineMath math="9+8" /> without checking the whole-number arrangements nearby.
- <InlineMath math="19" /> rounds that crossing point to the nearest whole numbers, <InlineMath math="10+9" />, but that arrangement needs <InlineMath math="29" /> bottles of syrup and <InlineMath math="46" /> jars of cake.

So the shop can make at most <InlineMath math="18" /> hampers.
