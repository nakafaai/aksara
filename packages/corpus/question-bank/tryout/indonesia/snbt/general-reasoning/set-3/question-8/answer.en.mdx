# Given

The head librarian divides <InlineMath math="120" /> returning debtors by <InlineMath math="300" /> **reading-room visitors** to claim <InlineMath math="40\%" /> of past debtors are active again.

# Asked

The statement that **most accurately** pinpoints the flaw in that reasoning.

# Solution

> Surveying people inside the reading room tells you their share among visitors, not the share of all past debtors who actually came back.

The head librarian works out <InlineMath math="120\div 300=40\%" /> and then calls that <InlineMath math="40\%" /> the share of debtors who have come back. To judge this reasoning, check the denominator and who could get into the sample.

The denominator is the <InlineMath math="300" /> reading-room visitors. So <InlineMath math="40\%" /> means "out of every <InlineMath math="100" /> visitors, <InlineMath math="40" /> are returning debtors". The librarian's question runs the other way: out of all debtors, what share came back? For that, the denominator has to be all the debtors, the <InlineMath math="1{,}500" /> members.

The deeper problem is the sample. The survey happened in the reading room, so **only people who already came to the library could be asked**. Debtors who haven't come back can't be in the reading room, so the survey could never catch them. A sample like this doesn't represent all debtors, and it can't measure what share of them returned.

Here's why the other options fail.

- The size of <InlineMath math="300" /> isn't the core problem. A survey of <InlineMath math="3{,}000" /> visitors would still reach only people who already came. What's wrong is who was surveyed, not how many.
- The <InlineMath math="40\%" /> conclusion says nothing about why debtors came back, only how many did. Criticizing a reason it never claims attacks a claim that wasn't made.
- Dividing <InlineMath math="120" /> by <InlineMath math="1{,}500" /> does use the right denominator, <InlineMath math="120\div 1{,}500=8\%" />. But the <InlineMath math="120" /> are only the debtors who happened to be in the reading room during the survey. Other debtors who borrow again but weren't there aren't counted, so <InlineMath math="8\%" /> is only a lower bound, not the exact share.
- Embarrassment makes people hide that they owed fines, so <InlineMath math="120" /> could be too small, not too big. The direction of the error is reversed.

# Answer

So the main flaw is that <InlineMath math="40\%" /> is the share of debtors among visitors, while a reading-room survey can never reach debtors who haven't come back.
