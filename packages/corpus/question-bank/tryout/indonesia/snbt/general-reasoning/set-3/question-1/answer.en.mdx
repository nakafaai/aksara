# Given

The coffee yield table separates shaded and unshaded farms on **gentle and steep slopes** along with how many farms lie on each slope.

# Asked

The conclusion **most strongly supported** by the table data.

# Solution

> When one group sits mostly on harder ground, the overall average can fool you, so always compare farms on the same slope first.

The question asks for the conclusion the table truly guarantees. The table splits the farms by slope, so you need two moves: compare shaded and unshaded farms **on the same slope**, then see how the way farms are spread over the two slopes shapes the overall averages.

Start inside each slope. On the gentle slope (*lereng landai*), shaded farms average <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> kilograms per hectare and unshaded farms <InlineMath math="900" />. On the steep slope (*lereng curam*), it's <InlineMath math="700" /> against <InlineMath math="600" />. On both slopes, shaded farms come out <InlineMath math="100" /> kilograms per hectare ahead.

Now work out the overall averages. Every farm has the same area, so the overall average is a weighted average: multiply each group's average by its number of farms, add, then divide by the number of farms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{shaded} &= \frac{10\cdot 1{,}000+40\cdot 700}{50}=\frac{38{,}000}{50}=760 \\ \text{unshaded} &= \frac{40\cdot 900+10\cdot 600}{50}=\frac{42{,}000}{50}=840 \end{aligned}" />

Overall, shaded farms lose, even though they win on each slope. The reason is how the farms are spread out. Of the <InlineMath math="50" /> shaded farms, <InlineMath math="40" /> sit on the low-yield steep slope, while <InlineMath math="40" /> of the <InlineMath math="50" /> unshaded farms sit on the high-yield gentle slope.

The "what if" conclusion tests exactly that. Spread the shaded farms like the unshaded ones, <InlineMath math="40" /> on the gentle slope and <InlineMath math="10" /> on the steep slope, and keep each group's average:

<BlockMath math="\frac{40\cdot 1{,}000+10\cdot 700}{50}=\frac{47{,}000}{50}=940>840" />

This conclusion is certain, because it uses only the table's numbers and the assumption stated inside the option itself. Check it: <InlineMath math="940-840=100" />, the same lead as on each slope. That makes sense, since once the spread is equal, the <InlineMath math="100" /> lead on each slope carries straight into the overall average.

Here's why the other options fail.

- The option saying unshaded farms do better on both slopes flips the table. On each slope, the shaded farms are ahead. The slip is pushing the overall result (<InlineMath math="760<840" />) down into every group.
- The figure <InlineMath math="850" /> comes from <InlineMath math="(1{,}000+700)\div 2" />, a plain average that ignores how many farms each group has. There are four times as many shaded farms on the steep slope, so the correct average is <InlineMath math="760" />, below <InlineMath math="840" />.
- The option claiming "every farm gains <InlineMath math="100" /> kilograms" goes beyond the data. That <InlineMath math="100" /> is a gap between averages, not a gain on each farm, and observational data like this doesn't prove cause and effect.
- The option blaming shade trees for the low steep-slope yields contradicts the table. On the steep slope, the unshaded farms are the lowest (<InlineMath math="600" />), so shade can't explain why yields there are low.

# Answer

So the best-supported conclusion is that if the shaded farms had the same slope mix as the unshaded ones, their average (**<InlineMath math="940" />**) would beat the unshaded average (**<InlineMath math="840" />**).
