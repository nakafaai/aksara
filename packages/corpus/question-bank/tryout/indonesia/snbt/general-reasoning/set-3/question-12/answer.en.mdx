Let <InlineMath math="p" />: the price of fresh chili rises, <InlineMath math="q" />: Bu Sari cuts her fresh chili purchases, and <InlineMath math="r" />: Bu Sari switches to dried chili. The first premise is <InlineMath math="p \Rightarrow (q \vee r)" />.

The second premise says <InlineMath math="\neg q \wedge \neg r" />, which is equivalent to <InlineMath math="\neg(q \vee r)" />. So **the "then" part of the first premise is false**. By modus tollens:

<BlockMath math="p \Rightarrow (q \vee r),\quad \neg(q \vee r) \;\vdash\; \neg p" />

So the price of fresh chili did not rise this week.

The other options are wrong:

- "The price rose" contradicts the conclusion just derived.
- Not cutting purchases does not mean buying more than usual. Bu Sari may buy the same amount.
- Not cutting fresh chili purchases means Bu Sari still buys, so "buys no chili at all" contradicts the premise.
- The premises say nothing about the price of dried chili.

So the conclusion that must be true is that the price of fresh chili did not rise this week.
