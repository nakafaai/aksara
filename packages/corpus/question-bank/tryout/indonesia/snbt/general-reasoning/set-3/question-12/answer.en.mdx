This question asks what must be true about last night, using four rules and two facts: the pump wasn't switched on, and the west plot had already been fertilised that week. The key is **reading rules 1 and 4 correctly**, because they use *kecuali jika* ("unless") and *hanya jika* ("only if").

A sentence of the form "P unless Q" means "if not Q, then P". So rule 1 says: if the tank isn't full yet, the pump is switched on. Every if-then statement is equivalent to its contrapositive, the form that swaps the two parts and negates both. The contrapositive of rule 1 says: if the pump isn't switched on, the tank is full.

Now chain the steps.

1. The pump wasn't switched on, so by the contrapositive of rule 1, the tank was full.
2. The tank was full, so by rule 2, the dividing sluice gate was opened.
3. The gate was opened, so by rule 3, the east plot or the west plot was watered. At least one of them was watered.
4. A sentence of the form "P only if Q" means "if P, then Q". So rule 4 says: if the west plot is watered, it hasn't been fertilised that week. Its contrapositive says: if the west plot was fertilised, it isn't watered. It was fertilised, so the west plot wasn't watered.
5. From step 3, at least one plot was watered. The west plot wasn't, so the east plot was.

Here's the whole chain at a glance.

<BlockMath math="\text{pump off} \Rightarrow \text{tank full} \Rightarrow \text{gate open} \Rightarrow (\text{east} \vee \text{west})" />

Since rule 4 rules out the west plot, the east plot is what's left. Check it backwards: a full tank, an open gate, a watered east plot and a dry west plot satisfy all four rules at once.

Each other conclusion fails because of one wrong step.

- "The tank was full, but we can't tell which plot was watered" comes from reading *hanya jika* backwards, as "if the west plot isn't fertilised, it's watered". Read that way, rule 4 says nothing about a fertilised plot, so the reasoning stalls at step 3.
- "The tank was full and the west plot was watered" contradicts rule 4, because the west plot had been fertilised.
- "The tank wasn't full and no plot was watered" comes from reading rule 1 backwards, as if a full tank switched the pump on. It then reverses rule 2 as well, but a tank that isn't full tells you nothing about the gate.
- "Both plots were watered" reads *atau* ("or") as *dan* ("and") and then ignores rule 4.

So what must be true is that the tank was full and the east plot was watered last night.
