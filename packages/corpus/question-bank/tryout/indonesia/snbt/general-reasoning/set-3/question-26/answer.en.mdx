# Given

Wulan buys **positive integer counts of package A and package B**, with two extra statements about paying <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}61{,}000" /> and swapping the two quantities.

# Asked

Whether statements **(1)** and **(2)** are sufficient to determine how many package A items Wulan bought.

# Solution

> Information is sufficient only when it leaves a single unique answer, so test whether more than one whole-number pair still fits.

The question asks how many A packs Wulan bought. A statement is sufficient when it leaves only one possible number of A packs. Let Wulan buy <InlineMath math="a" /> A packs and <InlineMath math="b" /> B packs, where <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are whole numbers of at least <InlineMath math="1" />. Test each statement on its own first, then combine them.

Dividing both sides by <InlineMath math="1{,}000" />, statement (1) gives the equation:

<BlockMath math="7a + 4b = 61" />

Since <InlineMath math="4b" /> is a multiple of <InlineMath math="4" />, the remainder <InlineMath math="61 - 7a" /> has to be divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> too. The value of <InlineMath math="a" /> is at most <InlineMath math="8" />, because <InlineMath math="7 \times 9 = 63" /> is already more than <InlineMath math="61" />. Try every value.

| <InlineMath math="a" /> | <InlineMath math="61 - 7a" /> | Divisible by <InlineMath math="4" />? | <InlineMath math="b" /> |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="54" /> | no | not whole |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="47" /> | no | not whole |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="40" /> | yes | <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="33" /> | no | not whole |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="26" /> | no | not whole |
| <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="19" /> | no | not whole |
| <InlineMath math="7" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | yes | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | no | not whole |

There are **two possible pairs**: <InlineMath math="(a, b) = (3, 10)" /> and <InlineMath math="(7, 3)" />. The number of A packs could be <InlineMath math="3" /> or <InlineMath math="7" />, so statement (1) alone isn't sufficient.

Statement (2) says the swapped purchase would cost more. Write that as an inequality and simplify.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
7b + 4a &> 7a + 4b \\
3b &> 3a \\
b &> a
\end{aligned}" />

Statement (2) only tells you that Wulan bought more B packs than A packs. Lots of pairs fit, such as <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" /> or <InlineMath math="(5, 9)" />, so statement (2) alone isn't sufficient.

Now combine them. Of the two pairs from statement (1), only <InlineMath math="(3, 10)" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="b > a" />. The pair <InlineMath math="(7, 3)" /> drops out because <InlineMath math="3 < 7" />. Check: the original bill is <InlineMath math="61" /> thousand, and after swapping the price is <InlineMath math="82" /> thousand, which really is more. The number of A packs must be <InlineMath math="3" />.

Why the other options are wrong:

- Statement (1) alone looks sufficient if you stop at the first pair you find, <InlineMath math="(3, 10)" />, and never check <InlineMath math="(7, 3)" />.
- Statement (2) alone only gives the order <InlineMath math="b > a" />, not a specific value.
- Each statement alone being sufficient means making both of those mistakes at once.
- Neither being sufficient is what you conclude if you don't turn statement (2) into <InlineMath math="b > a" />, so you miss that it removes the pair <InlineMath math="(7, 3)" />.

# Answer

So the two statements together are **sufficient to answer the question, but neither statement alone is**.
