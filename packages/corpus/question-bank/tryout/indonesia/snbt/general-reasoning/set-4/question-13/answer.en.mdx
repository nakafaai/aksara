The question asks for the statement that **has the same truth value in every situation** as the original sentence. Two statements are equivalent when no situation makes one true and the other false.

Step one: turn the form *tidak ada ... kecuali ...* ("no one ... except ...") into "if ..., then ...". The original says that the only students allowed to borrow lab equipment are students who meet both requirements at once. So it has this form.

<BlockMath math="\text{may borrow} \Rightarrow (\text{did training} \wedge \text{has card})" />

The sentence sets a requirement, not a promise. A student who meets both requirements isn't guaranteed a loan, for example when someone else is using the equipment, and that situation doesn't contradict the original.

Step two: take the contrapositive, which means flipping the arrow and negating both sides. The negation of "did the training and has the card" is "didn't do the training or doesn't have the card", because one failed requirement is already enough to sink the pair.

<BlockMath math="(\neg\text{did training} \vee \neg\text{has card}) \Rightarrow \neg\text{may borrow}" />

That form is exactly the statement "if a student hasn't done the safety training or doesn't bring a lab card, they may not borrow lab equipment".

You can test every other option with one situation where its truth value differs from the original.

- The statement that every student who did the training and has the card may borrow flips the arrow. Picture a student who meets both requirements but is turned away. The original stays true, while this statement is false.
- The statement that some student may borrow even without the training or without the card is the negation of the original, not an equivalent form. Its truth value is always the opposite of the original.
- The statement that mentions only the lab card drops the training requirement. Picture a student who has the card, hasn't done the training, and borrows equipment anyway. The original is broken, but this statement isn't.
- The statement with "hasn't done the training **and** doesn't have the card" uses "and" where "or" belongs. It only forbids students who fail both requirements. Picture a student who did the training, doesn't have the card, and borrows equipment. The original is broken, but this statement isn't.

So the equivalent statement is: if a student hasn't done the safety training or doesn't bring a lab card, they may not borrow lab equipment.
