# Given

Registered entrants and attendance in **District R stay unchanged**, while the office targets at least an <InlineMath math="80\%" /> pass rate among exam takers.

# Asked

The **minimum extra passing candidates** needed in District R compared with this year.

# Solution

> Find eighty percent of the exam takers first and round up to a whole person before subtracting this year passing count.

The office's target is a pass rate of **at least** <InlineMath math="80\%" />, and its denominator is the participants who sit the exam, not everyone registered.

Step 1. Find the exam takers in District R. Registration and the attendance share stay the same, so the exam takers stay the same too.

<BlockMath math="80\% \times 160 = 128" />

Step 2. Find how many passes are needed.

<BlockMath math="80\% \times 128 = 102.4" />

A number of people has to be a whole number. <InlineMath math="102" /> people isn't enough, because <InlineMath math="102 : 128 \approx 79.7\%" /> is still below <InlineMath math="80\%" />. So at least <InlineMath math="103" /> people must pass.

The chart below draws the pass rate for several numbers of passes. **The pass-rate line only reaches the target once <InlineMath math="103" /> people pass**.

Step 3. Compare with this year, when <InlineMath math="96" /> people passed.

<BlockMath math="103 - 96 = 7" />

Check: <InlineMath math="103 : 128 \approx 80.5\%" />, which reaches the target.

Common wrong answers:

- <InlineMath math="6" /> comes from rounding <InlineMath math="102.4" /> down to <InlineMath math="102" />.
- <InlineMath math="32" /> comes from taking <InlineMath math="80\%" /> of the <InlineMath math="160" /> registered participants, which makes the pass target <InlineMath math="128" />.
- <InlineMath math="103" /> is the number of passes needed, not the increase over this year.

# Answer

So at least <InlineMath math="7" /> more people must pass in District R.
