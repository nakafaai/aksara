This question asks for an assumption the estimate **needs**, not one that merely helps it. A needed assumption is a condition that has to be true for the reasoning to stand. You test it with the negation test: flip the option, then check whether the market head's reasoning falls apart. If it falls apart, the option is needed. If the reasoning can still stand, it isn't.

First lay out the market head's reasoning. He sees a gap of <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}0.8\text{ million}" /> between users and non-users and treats it as extra turnover caused by the e-wallet. Then he multiplies it by the <InlineMath math="60" /> traders who don't use the e-wallet yet:

<BlockMath math="60 \times \text{Rp}0.8\text{ million} = \text{Rp}48\text{ million}" />

Notice that he's estimating the extra turnover of the **whole market**, not just the users' turnover. That's where the hidden condition sits. A user might be doing better simply by taking buyers away from other traders in the same market. If so, the money spent in the market doesn't grow, it just changes hands.

Now run the negation test on the key. Suppose all of the users' extra turnover came from buyers who switched away from other traders in that market. Once every trader has to use the e-wallet, there's no one left to take buyers from, and spending in the market stays the same. The whole market's turnover doesn't rise at all, so the <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}48\text{ million}" /> estimate collapses. Because the negation brings the reasoning down, the statement that the extra turnover didn't come entirely from switching buyers is a needed assumption.

The other options fail the negation test:

- Traders being willing without pressure isn't needed, because the plan makes the e-wallet compulsory for everyone. Even unwilling traders would have to use it.
- The service fee is about profit, but the estimate is about turnover, which is total sales. Even if the fee were bigger than the extra turnover, turnover could still go up.
- Saying that not a single buyer switched is too strong. Its negation, that some buyers did switch, doesn't break the reasoning as long as part of the extra turnover comes from new spending.
- An identical rise for every user is also too strong. The estimate only needs an average rise of about <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}0.8\text{ million}" />, so individual traders can gain different amounts.

So the needed assumption is that the users' extra turnover didn't come entirely from buyers who switched away from other traders in that market.
