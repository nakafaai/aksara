# Given

The passage and table track **rice plots with and without ducks** and how many needed spraying, while the group leader sorted plots by harvest status.

# Asked

The conclusion **most strongly supported** by the table data.

# Solution

> To judge whether a method truly works, sort plots by what happened at the start of the season rather than by how they looked at harvest.

This question asks for the conclusion the data really support, not a repeat of the group leader's claim. The trap is the way the leader split the plots. He grouped them by their state at harvest, but the fair comparison groups the plots by their **state at the start of the season**.

Why is the leader's split misleading? The group's rule says ducks must be taken out before a plot is sprayed, and they never go back afterwards. So every sprayed plot loses its ducks and moves into the "no ducks" group. That leaves the "duck plots" group holding only plots that were never sprayed. The zero in the table comes from how the plots were grouped, not from anything the ducks did.

Now regroup the plots. Ducks were released on <InlineMath math="20" /> plots at the start, but only <InlineMath math="16" /> plots still had ducks at harvest. That means <InlineMath math="20-16=4" /> plots had their ducks taken out. Those four plots sit in the "no ducks" group, so that group holds <InlineMath math="20+4=24" /> plots, which matches the table.

Ducks were only ever taken out for spraying, so those four plots were all sprayed. The twelve sprayed plots in the "no ducks" group are those four plus plots that never had ducks. Work out the share of sprayed plots in each starting group:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Ducks from the start: } & \frac{4}{20} = 20\% \\ \text{No ducks from the start: } & \frac{12-4}{20} = \frac{8}{20} = 40\% \end{aligned}" />

The chart below compares the two groups as they stood at the start of the season. **The bar for plots with ducks from the start is only half as tall as the bar for plots without ducks**.

So <InlineMath math="20\%" /> is half of <InlineMath math="40\%" />. Check the total: <InlineMath math="4+8=12" /> sprayed plots, the same as the table.

Here's why the other options don't hold:

- The claim that no plot that ever had ducks was sprayed takes the leader's groups at face value. Four plots that had ducks were in fact sprayed, and the share of never-duck plots that were sprayed is <InlineMath math="40\%" />, not half.
- One third comes from treating all twelve sprayed plots as never-duck plots and computing <InlineMath math="60\%" />. That counts the four plots that lost their ducks twice.
- The gap of <InlineMath math="1" /> tonne per hectare is the gap between the leader's two groups. By starting group, the averages are <InlineMath math="5.7" /> and <InlineMath math="5.1" />, so the gap is only <InlineMath math="0.6" /> tonnes per hectare.
- The estimate of <InlineMath math="8" /> plots is <InlineMath math="20\%" /> of <InlineMath math="40" />. It treats the gap as a sure effect of the ducks, but the owners chose for themselves which plots got ducks, so the two groups may differ in other ways.

# Answer

So the best supported conclusion is that among plots with ducks from the start, the share that had to be sprayed is only half the share among plots without ducks from the start.
