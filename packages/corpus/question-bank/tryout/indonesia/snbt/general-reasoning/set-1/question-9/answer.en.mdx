Pak Harun looks only at houses that were burgled: <InlineMath math="15" /> of those <InlineMath math="20" /> houses, or <InlineMath math="75\%" />, had double locks. From that he concludes that double locks don't help. The flaw is that he **doesn't know what share of all houses** in the neighborhood have double locks.

Why does that number matter? Suppose the neighborhood has <InlineMath math="200" /> houses and <InlineMath math="180" /> of them have double locks. Work out the burglary rate for each kind of house:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{With double locks: } & \frac{15}{180} \approx 8.3\% \\ \text{Without double locks: } & \frac{5}{20} = 25\% \end{aligned}" />

In this example, houses without double locks are actually burgled three times as often. The <InlineMath math="75\%" /> only means something once you compare it with how common double locks are among all houses.

So the pattern of the flaw is this: most bad cases share some feature, and the arguer concludes that the feature doesn't lower the risk, without checking how common that feature is in the whole group.

The argument about hikers has exactly the same pattern. Of the <InlineMath math="40" /> hikers who got lost, <InlineMath math="36" /> carried a map, and the arguer concludes that maps don't make hikers get lost less often. If nearly every hiker on that mountain carries a map, it's no surprise that most lost hikers carried one too.

The other options don't share this flaw:

- The motorcycle argument looks similar, but it includes the comparison number: among the broken-down motorcycles, <InlineMath math="\frac{30}{50} = 60\%" /> were over <InlineMath math="10" /> years old, while across the whole city only <InlineMath math="20\%" /> are. That reasoning holds up.
- The bookstore survey goes wrong because its sample isn't representative: bookstore visitors stand in for all residents.
- The market argument goes wrong because it treats two events that happen one after the other as cause and effect, though other things could change at the same time.
- The security camera argument shares Pak Harun's topic, but it already compares two groups of houses. Its weakness is different: houses with cameras may differ from houses without them in other ways.

So the reasoning with the most similar flaw is the claim that carrying a map doesn't make hikers get lost less often, because <InlineMath math="36" /> of the <InlineMath math="40" /> lost hikers carried one.
