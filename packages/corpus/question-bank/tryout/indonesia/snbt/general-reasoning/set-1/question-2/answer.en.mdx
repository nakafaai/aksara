# Given

**Bu Laras's conclusion** credits aerators with curbing mortality in high-density ponds, while weak fry from a new supplier entered some ponds.

# Asked

Which finding about where the weak fry went most weakens Bu Laras's conclusion.

# Solution

> A cause-and-effect claim weakens most when an outside culprit like weak fry lands specifically in the group that performed worse.

Bu Laras bases her conclusion on the comparison in the high-density row: aerated ponds rose <InlineMath math="30 - 22 = 8" /> points, while ponds without aerators rose <InlineMath math="42 - 26 = 16" /> points. Her conclusion only weakens if the weak fry can explain this gap, which means the weak fry must **hit only the high-density ponds without aerators**, not the aerated ponds in the same row.

So each option takes two steps. First, read the table to find which group the description points to. Second, ask what that does to the comparison in the high-density row.

The rises are <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="8" />, and <InlineMath math="16" /> points for low aerated, low without, high aerated, and high without.

The chart below draws those four rises. **Bu Laras's conclusion rests on the gap between the last two bars**, so only weak fry hitting just the high-density ponds without aerators can explain that gap.

- The highest average in the earlier cycle is <InlineMath math="26\%" />, which belongs to the high-density ponds without aerators. If only those ponds got weak fry, their <InlineMath math="16" />-point rise could come from the fry rather than from the missing aerator. That alternative cuts the link between aerators and the gap, so the conclusion weakens.
- Averages above <InlineMath math="25\%" /> in the latest cycle belong to two groups, <InlineMath math="30\%" /> and <InlineMath math="42\%" />, so both high-density groups got weak fry. Both sides of the comparison are hit, and the gap is still unexplained.
- Averages below <InlineMath math="25\%" /> in the earlier cycle belong to both low-density groups (<InlineMath math="10\%" />) and the high-density aerated group (<InlineMath math="22\%" />). In the high row, the aerated ponds are the ones handicapped, yet they rose less. That actually strengthens the conclusion.
- The smallest rise, <InlineMath math="4" /> points, belongs to both low-density groups. Bu Laras's conclusion is only about high density, so this falls outside it.
- Weak fry spread equally over all ponds hit every group the same way, so it explains no gap at all.

# Answer

So the statement that weakens the conclusion most is that **weak fry went only to the ponds whose group had the highest average death rate in the earlier cycle, which are the high-density ponds without aerators**.
