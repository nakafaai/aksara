# Given

**An isosceles triangle** has perimeter <InlineMath math="20" /> cm, accompanied by two clues that one side is <InlineMath math="8" /> cm and the base is shorter than each leg.

# Asked

Whether statements (1) and (2) alone or together suffice to determine the triangle's side lengths uniquely.

# Solution

> A side of <InlineMath math="8" /> cm could be either the base or a leg, so you need the extra inequality to lock down one unique valid triangle.

Let each leg (*kaki*, one of the two equal sides) be <InlineMath math="k" /> and the base (*alas*) be <InlineMath math="a" />. From the perimeter, <InlineMath math="2k+a=20" />. The three sides also have to form a real triangle: the two legs together must be longer than the base, so <InlineMath math="2k>a" />. A statement is sufficient if it leaves exactly one value of <InlineMath math="a" />.

**Statement (1) alone**. "One side is <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" />" doesn't say which side, so **there are two cases to check**:

| Case | Sides | Triangle condition | Base |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| leg <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="8, 8, 4" /> | <InlineMath math="16>4" />, holds | <InlineMath math="4" /> |
| base <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="6, 6, 8" /> | <InlineMath math="12>8" />, holds | <InlineMath math="8" /> |

Both cases give a valid triangle with a different base. Statement (1) alone isn't sufficient. An equilateral triangle isn't possible either, because <InlineMath math="20" /> isn't divisible by <InlineMath math="3" />.

**Statement (2) alone**. The condition is <InlineMath math="a<k" />. With <InlineMath math="a=20-2k" />, it becomes <InlineMath math="20-2k<k" />, so <InlineMath math="k>6.67" />. The base must also be at least <InlineMath math="1" />, so <InlineMath math="k\le9" />. The options are <InlineMath math="(7, 7, 6)" />, <InlineMath math="(8, 8, 4)" />, and <InlineMath math="(9, 9, 2)" />. Three bases are possible, so statement (2) alone isn't sufficient.

**Both statements together**. Of the two cases from statement (1), only <InlineMath math="(8, 8, 4)" /> has a base shorter than the legs. The case <InlineMath math="(6, 6, 8)" /> is ruled out because its base is longer. There's exactly one answer, a base of <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" />, so the two statements together are sufficient.

The other options come from a missed case:

- "Statement (1) alone is sufficient" comes from taking the <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> side to be a leg straight away. The case with an <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> base and <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> legs gets missed, even though it's a valid triangle.
- "Neither statement is sufficient, even together" comes from listing the cases for statement (2) without using statement (1), so three possibilities seem to remain.
- Statement (2) alone still leaves three triangles and statement (1) alone leaves two, so the options claiming statement (2) alone is sufficient, or that each statement alone is sufficient, can't be right.

# Answer

So the two statements together are **sufficient to answer the question, but neither statement alone is**.
