The principal compares two groups of applicants: participants rose <InlineMath math="78 - 70 = 8" /> points, and applicants who didn't get a place rose <InlineMath math="73 - 70 = 3" /> points. The difference is <InlineMath math="8 - 3 = 5" /> points. This comparison already removes two other explanations: both groups wanted to join, and both started at the same average, <InlineMath math="70" />.

One gap is left. The text only says the committee chose <InlineMath math="60" /> participants, not how. If the choice depended on something about the students, such as who seemed keenest or most organized, the participants could still differ from the other applicants in ways the table doesn't show. The best strengthener is the statement that **closes the gap in how places were assigned**.

A lottery closes it. A lottery looks at no trait at all, so participants and the applicants who weren't drawn are, on average, alike in every way except one: one group attended the class and the other didn't. Put that together with the data: their starting scores really were equal, and only the group that attended rose more. The <InlineMath math="5" />-point difference is then most plausibly due to the class.

The other options don't close that gap:

- Participants who attended most rose most, but the students who attend most may also be the most diligent. Diligence can still explain this pattern.
- The comparison with all <InlineMath math="120" /> students who didn't attend does give a bigger gap, since their average rise is only <InlineMath math="\frac{30(3) + 90(1)}{120} = 1.5" /> points. But that group includes <InlineMath math="90" /> students who never applied and started lower. That comparison is more mixed up, not stronger.
- Paid tutoring for some of the applicants without a place means their <InlineMath math="3" />-point rise is partly caused by something else, so the <InlineMath math="5" />-point estimate becomes less reliable.
- Giving places to the fastest applicants makes participants differ from the other applicants, for example by being keener, so the conclusion gets weaker.

So the statement that strengthens the conclusion most is that the committee chose the <InlineMath math="60" /> participants by drawing names from all the applicants.
