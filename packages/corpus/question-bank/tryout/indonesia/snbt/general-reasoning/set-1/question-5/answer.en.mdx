All four statements test one habit: **settle the base and the group first**, then calculate. The school has <InlineMath math="500" /> students, <InlineMath math="400" /> of them bought drinks, and the buyers of high-sugar drinks in the two weeks aren't exactly the same students.

Statement 1 is false (*Salah*), even though it sounds true. The base is all students at the school:

<BlockMath math="\frac{180}{500} = 36\% < 40\%" />

You get <InlineMath math="\frac{180}{400} = 45\%" /> only if you divide by the drink buyers, but the statement talks about all students.

Statement 2 is true (*Benar*), even though it sounds false. Of the <InlineMath math="180" /> buyers of high-sugar drinks after the labels, <InlineMath math="150" /> had already bought them before. The rest are new buyers:

<BlockMath math="180 - 150 = 30 \qquad \frac{30}{180} = \frac{1}{6}" />

So even though there were fewer buyers of high-sugar drinks, <InlineMath math="30" /> students started buying them.

Statement 3 is true, even though both the packs and the buyers went down. Work out the packs per buyer:

<BlockMath math="\text{Before: } \frac{480}{240} = 2 \qquad \text{After: } \frac{360}{180} = 2" />

Packs and buyers both fell by a quarter, so the ratio stays at <InlineMath math="2" />.

Statement 4 is false, even though both changes are <InlineMath math="120" /> packs. Packs that aren't high in sugar are all packs minus high-sugar packs, <InlineMath math="800 - 480 = 320" /> before the labels and <InlineMath math="800 - 360 = 440" /> after. The percentage changes use different bases:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Not high in sugar, up: } & \frac{120}{320} = 37.5\% \\ \text{High in sugar, down: } & \frac{120}{480} = 25\% \end{aligned}" />

The same difference divided by different bases gives different percentages.

So statements 2 and 3 are true, while statements 1 and 4 are false.
