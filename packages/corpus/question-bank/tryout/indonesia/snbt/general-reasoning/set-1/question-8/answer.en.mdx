Start by writing down the frame of the council member's reasoning. They observe **one place** that improved after an action and then confidently conclude that the same action will give the same result **everywhere**:

<BlockMath math="\text{one case improves after action } T \;\Rightarrow\; T \text{ everywhere will improve the whole area}" />

The argument about Gang Melati has the same frame. Thefts dropped in one alley after lamps were installed, and the arguer concludes that lamps in every alley will reduce thefts across the whole neighborhood. It even shares the same weakness: thieves might simply move to another alley.

The other options use different patterns:

- The Gang Kenanga argument is a syllogism. A general premise about all lit alleys is applied to one alley, which runs in the opposite direction.
- The Gang Mawar argument uses modus tollens: from "if lamps are installed, thefts fall" and "thefts did not fall" it concludes "lamps were not installed".
- The argument about every lit alley starts from many cases and ends with a cautious "may". The council member starts from one case and concludes with certainty.
- The night-patrol argument compares two actions, although the evidence covers only one.

So the most similar reasoning is the argument that lamps in all alleys will cut thefts across the neighborhood based on the experience of Gang Melati.
