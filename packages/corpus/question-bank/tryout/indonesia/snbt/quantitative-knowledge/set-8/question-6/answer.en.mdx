The **order of members does not matter** in a set, so we use the concept of combinations.

The total number of subsets of a set with <InlineMath math="n" /> members is <InlineMath math="2^n" />. Given <InlineMath math="n = 10" />, the total number of subsets is:

<BlockMath math="2^{10} = 1024" />

We are asked to find the number of subsets with **more than <InlineMath math="3" />** members. This means we are looking for the sum of subsets with <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, ..., <InlineMath math="10" /> members.

An easier way is to use the complement principle: Total subsets minus subsets with **<InlineMath math="0" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="2" />, or <InlineMath math="3" />** members.

The combination formula is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}C(n, r)&=\binom{n}{r}\\&=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\end{aligned}" />

| Members selected | Calculation | Number of subsets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | <InlineMath math="\frac{10!}{0!10!}=1" /> | 1 |
| 1 | <InlineMath math="\frac{10!}{1!9!}=10" /> | 10 |
| 2 | <InlineMath math="\frac{10\times9}{2\times1}=45" /> | 45 |
| 3 | <InlineMath math="\frac{10\times9\times8}{3\times2\times1}=120" /> | 120 |

The one subset with zero members is the **empty set**, which must still be counted.

The number of subsets with <InlineMath math="\leq 3" /> members is:

<BlockMath math="1 + 10 + 45 + 120 = 176" />

Thus, the number of subsets with **more than <InlineMath math="3" />** members is:

<BlockMath math="1024-176=848" />

So, the number of subsets of <InlineMath math="A" /> that have more than <InlineMath math="3" /> members is <InlineMath math="848" />.
