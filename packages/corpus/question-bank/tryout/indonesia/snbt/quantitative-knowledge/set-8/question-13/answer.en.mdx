You need the distance from the vertex <InlineMath math="A" /> to the point of tangency <InlineMath math="T" />. Here's the plan: find both diagonals from their sum and the side length, locate the centre of the inscribed circle, then **use the right triangle formed by the radius to the point of tangency**.

# The lengths of both diagonals

Let the diagonals meet at <InlineMath math="O" />. The diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles, so triangle <InlineMath math="AOB" /> has a right angle at <InlineMath math="O" />, with <InlineMath math="AO=\tfrac12AC" /> and <InlineMath math="BO=\tfrac12BD" />. Pythagoras gives <InlineMath math="AC^2+BD^2=4\cdot15^2=900" />. Combine this with the sum of the diagonals.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(AC+BD)^2&=AC^2+BD^2+2\,AC\cdot BD\\
42^2&=900+2\,AC\cdot BD\\
AC\cdot BD&=\tfrac{1764-900}{2}=432
\end{aligned}" />

Two numbers with sum <InlineMath math="42" /> and product <InlineMath math="432" /> are the roots of <InlineMath math="t^2-42t+432=0" />, that is, <InlineMath math="(t-24)(t-18)=0" />. Since **<InlineMath math="AC" /> is the longer diagonal**, you get <InlineMath math="AC=24\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="BD=18\text{ cm}" />. So <InlineMath math="AO=12\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="BO=9\text{ cm}" />. Check: <InlineMath math="12^2+9^2=225=15^2" />.

# The point of tangency on the side

A rhombus is symmetric about both diagonals, so <InlineMath math="O" /> is the same distance from all four sides and is the centre of the inscribed circle. A radius to a point of tangency is always perpendicular to the tangent line, so **<InlineMath math="OT" /> is the altitude from the right angle at <InlineMath math="O" /> to the hypotenuse <InlineMath math="AB" />**.

Triangles <InlineMath math="ATO" /> and <InlineMath math="AOB" /> are similar, because both have a right angle and both contain the angle at <InlineMath math="A" />. The ratio of matching sides gives <InlineMath math="AT" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{AT}{AO}&=\frac{AO}{AB}\\
AT&=\frac{AO^2}{AB}=\frac{144}{15}=9.6\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

In the same way, <InlineMath math="BT=\tfrac{BO^2}{AB}=\tfrac{81}{15}=5.4\text{ cm}" /> and the radius is <InlineMath math="OT=\tfrac{AO\cdot BO}{AB}=\tfrac{108}{15}=7.2\text{ cm}" />. Check: <InlineMath math="AT+BT=9.6+5.4=15" />, the side length, and <InlineMath math="AT^2+OT^2=92.16+51.84=144=AO^2" />.

# The wrong options

- <InlineMath math="5.4\text{ cm}" /> is <InlineMath math="BT" />, which comes from **using the vertex on the shorter diagonal** as <InlineMath math="A" />.
- <InlineMath math="7.2\text{ cm}" /> is the radius <InlineMath math="OT" />, which comes from stopping one step too early.
- <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> comes from dividing <InlineMath math="AO^2" /> by the diagonal <InlineMath math="BD" /> instead of the side <InlineMath math="AB" />: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{144}{18}" />.
- <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm}" /> comes from using **the inscribed circle of triangle <InlineMath math="AOB" />** instead of the inscribed circle of the rhombus: its tangent length from <InlineMath math="A" /> is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{12+15+9}{2}-9=9" />.

So the length of <InlineMath math="AT" /> is <InlineMath math="9.6\text{ cm}" />.
