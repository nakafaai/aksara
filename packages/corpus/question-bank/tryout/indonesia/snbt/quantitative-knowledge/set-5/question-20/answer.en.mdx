This fraction equation is hard to count directly, so the plan is to turn it into **a product of two factors with a fixed value**. After that, each factor pair gives one solution, and you just count the pairs with <InlineMath math="x<y" />.

# Turning it into a product

Multiply both sides by <InlineMath math="24xy" />, then **add <InlineMath math="576=24^2" /> to both sides** so the left side factors.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
24y+24x&=xy\\
xy-24x-24y&=0\\
xy-24x-24y+576&=576\\
(x-24)(y-24)&=576
\end{aligned}" />

# Both factors are positive

Since <InlineMath math="\tfrac1y" /> is positive, <InlineMath math="\tfrac1x" /> is smaller than <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{24}" />, so <InlineMath math="x>24" />. For the same reason, <InlineMath math="y>24" />. So **both factors <InlineMath math="x-24" /> and <InlineMath math="y-24" /> are positive**, and you don't need to check negative factor pairs.

# Counting the pairs

The prime factorisation is <InlineMath math="576=2^6\cdot3^2" />, so it has <InlineMath math="(6+1)(2+1)=21" /> positive divisors. Each divisor <InlineMath math="d" /> gives one ordered pair <InlineMath math="(x,y)=\left(24+d,\ 24+\tfrac{576}{d}\right)" />. The divisor <InlineMath math="d=24" /> gives <InlineMath math="x=y=48" />, which doesn't satisfy <InlineMath math="x<y" />. The other <InlineMath math="20" /> ordered pairs come in swapped twins, and **exactly half of them** have <InlineMath math="x<y" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{21-1}{2}=10" />

Check with the list: <InlineMath math="x<y" /> happens exactly when <InlineMath math="d<24" />, which means <InlineMath math="d=1,2,3,4,6,8,9,12,16,18" />, so <InlineMath math="10" /> values. For example, <InlineMath math="d=1" /> gives <InlineMath math="(25,600)" />, and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{25}+\tfrac{1}{600}=\tfrac{24+1}{600}=\tfrac{1}{24}" />.

# Common mistakes

**The pair with <InlineMath math="x=y" /> has to be removed before you halve**. If you don't, you get <InlineMath math="11" />. The answer <InlineMath math="21" /> counts ordered pairs without the condition <InlineMath math="x<y" />, and the answer <InlineMath math="4" /> comes from counting the divisors of <InlineMath math="24" /> instead of the divisors of <InlineMath math="24^2" />.

So the number of positive integer pairs <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> with <InlineMath math="x<y" /> that satisfy the equation is <InlineMath math="10" />.
