The plan: find <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log b" /> from the first equation, use the condition <InlineMath math="1<b<a" /> to **throw out one possibility**, chain the bases to get <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log c" />, and then change the base <InlineMath math="ab" /> to base <InlineMath math="a" />.

# Two possibilities for the first logarithm

Let <InlineMath math="m={}^{a}\log b" />. Swapping the base and the argument **flips the logarithm upside down**, so <InlineMath math="{}^{b}\log a=\tfrac{1}{m}" />. Multiply the equation by <InlineMath math="2m" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m+\frac{1}{m}&=\frac{5}{2}\\
2m^2-5m+2&=0\\
(2m-1)(m-2)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="m=\tfrac{1}{2}" /> or <InlineMath math="m=2" />.

# The condition that removes one possibility

The base <InlineMath math="a" /> is greater than <InlineMath math="1" />, so <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log" /> is increasing. From <InlineMath math="1<b<a" /> you get <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log 1<{}^{a}\log b<{}^{a}\log a" />, that is <InlineMath math="0<m<1" />. That's why **<InlineMath math="m=2" /> is thrown out** and <InlineMath math="m=\tfrac{1}{2}" />.

# Chaining the bases

The chain rule <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log b\cdot{}^{b}\log c={}^{a}\log c" /> works because the middle base <InlineMath math="b" /> cancels. It gives **the logarithm of <InlineMath math="c" /> with base <InlineMath math="a" />**.

<BlockMath math="{}^{a}\log c=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4=2" />

# Changing to base a

By the change of base, <InlineMath math="{}^{ab}\log (bc)=\dfrac{{}^{a}\log (bc)}{{}^{a}\log (ab)}" />. The logarithm of a product is **the sum of the logarithms of its factors**, including <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log a=1" /> in the denominator.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{a}\log (bc)&=\frac{1}{2}+2=\frac{5}{2}\\
{}^{a}\log (ab)&=1+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{2}\\
{}^{ab}\log (bc)&=\frac{5/2}{3/2}=\frac{5}{3}
\end{aligned}" />

# Checking with powers of a

From <InlineMath math="m=\tfrac{1}{2}" />, <InlineMath math="b=a^{1/2}" />, and from <InlineMath math="{}^{b}\log c=4" />, <InlineMath math="c=b^4=a^2" />. Then <InlineMath math="ab=a^{3/2}" /> and <InlineMath math="bc=a^{5/2}" />, so the logarithm is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5/2}{3/2}=\tfrac{5}{3}" />. A real example: <InlineMath math="a=16" />, <InlineMath math="b=4" />, and <InlineMath math="c=256" /> meet every condition, and **the example agrees**, because <InlineMath math="64^{5/3}=1024=bc" />.

# Checking the other options

The option <InlineMath math="\tfrac{10}{3}" /> uses <InlineMath math="m=2" />, which **forgets the condition <InlineMath math="b<a" />**. The option <InlineMath math="3" /> skips the chain and uses <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log c=4" />. The option <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{2}" /> stops at <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log (bc)" /> without changing the base. The option <InlineMath math="5" /> divides by <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log b" /> only, but the denominator is <InlineMath math="{}^{a}\log (ab)" />.

So <InlineMath math="{}^{ab}\log (bc)=\tfrac{5}{3}" />.
