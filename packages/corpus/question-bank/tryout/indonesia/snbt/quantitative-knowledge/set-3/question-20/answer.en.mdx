The divisor is built from a few primes, so first break it into prime powers. Then count how many times each prime appears in <InlineMath math="n!" />. The answer is decided by **the prime that is satisfied last**, which isn't necessarily the largest prime.

# The prime factorization of the divisor

Use <InlineMath math="6=2\cdot 3" /> and <InlineMath math="10=2\cdot 5" />.

<BlockMath math="6^{12}\cdot 10^{6}=2^{12}\cdot 3^{12}\cdot 2^{6}\cdot 5^{6}=2^{18}\cdot 3^{12}\cdot 5^{6}" />

So <InlineMath math="n!" /> needs **at least <InlineMath math="18" /> factors of <InlineMath math="2" />, <InlineMath math="12" /> factors of <InlineMath math="3" />, and <InlineMath math="6" /> factors of <InlineMath math="5" />**.

# Counting prime factors in a factorial

Every multiple of <InlineMath math="p" /> from <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="n" /> gives one factor of <InlineMath math="p" />. Every multiple of <InlineMath math="p^2" /> gives one extra factor, every multiple of <InlineMath math="p^3" /> one more, and so on. The symbol <InlineMath math="\lfloor y\rfloor" /> means the greatest integer not exceeding <InlineMath math="y" />. With it, the number of factors of <InlineMath math="p" /> in <InlineMath math="n!" /> is

<BlockMath math="\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^3}\right\rfloor+\cdots" />

This shows that numbers like <InlineMath math="9" /> or <InlineMath math="27" /> **give more than one factor of <InlineMath math="3" />**.

# The condition for each prime

Find the smallest <InlineMath math="n" /> that works for each prime on its own.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{prime } 5&:\quad 24!\ \text{has } 4,\quad 25!\ \text{has } 5+1=6\\
\text{prime } 3&:\quad 26!\ \text{has } 8+2=10,\quad 27!\ \text{has } 9+3+1=13\\
\text{prime } 2&:\quad 19!\ \text{has } 9+4+2+1=16,\quad 20!\ \text{has } 10+5+2+1=18
\end{aligned}" />

So the prime <InlineMath math="5" /> needs <InlineMath math="n\ge 25" />, the prime <InlineMath math="3" /> needs <InlineMath math="n\ge 27" />, and the prime <InlineMath math="2" /> needs <InlineMath math="n\ge 20" />. The count of factors of <InlineMath math="3" /> **jumps from <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="13" />** at <InlineMath math="n=27" />, because <InlineMath math="27=3^3" /> brings three factors at once. All three conditions hold together exactly when <InlineMath math="n\ge 27" />.

# Checking the result

For <InlineMath math="n=27" />, the number of factors of <InlineMath math="2" /> is <InlineMath math="13+6+3+1=23\ge 18" />, there are <InlineMath math="13\ge 12" /> factors of <InlineMath math="3" />, and <InlineMath math="5+1=6" /> factors of <InlineMath math="5" />. For <InlineMath math="n=26" />, there are **only <InlineMath math="10" /> factors of <InlineMath math="3" />**, so <InlineMath math="26!" /> isn't divisible yet.

Common traps: answering <InlineMath math="25" /> because the largest prime <InlineMath math="5" /> seems to decide, answering <InlineMath math="20" /> because <InlineMath math="2^{18}" /> has the biggest exponent, or answering <InlineMath math="36" /> because you think you need <InlineMath math="12" /> multiples of <InlineMath math="3" /> and forget that <InlineMath math="9" />, <InlineMath math="18" />, and <InlineMath math="27" /> give extra factors.

So the smallest natural number is <InlineMath math="n=27" />.
