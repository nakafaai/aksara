A product is even when **at least one number is even**. That event covers two cases, so it is quicker to count the complement: the product is odd only when both numbers are odd.

# The sample space and the complement

There are 9 numbers: 5 odd (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) and 4 even. Two numbers are taken at once, so order does not matter and every pair **is equally likely**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(S)&=\binom92=36\\
n(\text{both odd})&=\binom52=10
\end{aligned}" />

# Computing the probability

<BlockMath math="P(\text{even product})=1-\frac{10}{36}=\frac{26}{36}=\frac{13}{18}" />

**A direct count** gives the same result: there are <InlineMath math="4\times5=20" /> even-odd pairs and <InlineMath math="\binom42=6" /> even-even pairs, 26 in total.

The value <InlineMath math="\tfrac5{18}" /> is the probability of the complement, not yet subtracted from 1. The value <InlineMath math="\tfrac59" /> counts only the 20 even-odd pairs and forgets the 6 even-even pairs. The value <InlineMath math="\tfrac16" /> counts only the even-even pairs. The value <InlineMath math="\tfrac{56}{81}" /> lets a number be drawn twice, <InlineMath math="1-\left(\tfrac59\right)^2" />.

So the probability of an even product is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{13}{18}" />.
