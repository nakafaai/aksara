You need to sort the triangles by where their vertices lie, write the area in terms of a single distance, and then count the pairs of points that work. The key is that **the area depends only on the length of the base** on one of the lines.

# Which triangles are possible

Three points on the same line are collinear, so they don't form a triangle. That means every triangle has **two vertices on one line and one vertex on the other line**. The total number of triangles is <InlineMath math="\binom{7}{2}\cdot 5+\binom{5}{2}\cdot 7=105+70=175" />.

# The area of each triangle

Take the two vertices on the same line as the base, with length <InlineMath math="d\text{ cm}" />. Since neighbouring points are <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" /> apart, <InlineMath math="d" /> is a whole number. The third vertex sits on the parallel line, so the height is always the distance between the lines, <InlineMath math="3\text{ cm}" />, wherever that vertex is.

<BlockMath math="L=\frac{1}{2}\cdot d\cdot 3=\frac{3d}{2}" />

The number <InlineMath math="3" /> is odd, so <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3d}{2}" /> is a whole number exactly when **the base length <InlineMath math="d" /> is even**.

# Counting pairs at an even distance

On a line with <InlineMath math="k" /> points spaced <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" /> apart, the number of pairs at distance <InlineMath math="d" /> is <InlineMath math="k-d" />. Line <InlineMath math="g" /> has <InlineMath math="7" /> points, so its even distances are <InlineMath math="2" />, <InlineMath math="4" />, and <InlineMath math="6" />. Line <InlineMath math="h" /> has <InlineMath math="5" /> points, so its even distances are <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="4" />. Each base then pairs with **every point on the opposite line**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{base on } g&: (5+3+1)\cdot 5=9\cdot 5=45\\
\text{base on } h&: (3+1)\cdot 7=4\cdot 7=28\\
\text{total}&: 45+28=73
\end{aligned}" />

# Checking with the odd distances

Also count the triangles whose area isn't a whole number, the ones with an odd base. On <InlineMath math="g" /> there are <InlineMath math="6+4+2=12" /> pairs at distance <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="3" />, or <InlineMath math="5" />, and on <InlineMath math="h" /> there are <InlineMath math="4+2=6" /> pairs at distance <InlineMath math="1" /> or <InlineMath math="3" />. That gives <InlineMath math="12\cdot 5+6\cdot 7=102" />, and **<InlineMath math="73+102=175" /> matches the total number of triangles**.

Common traps: forgetting the factor <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2}" /> makes the area <InlineMath math="3d" /> always a whole number, which leads to <InlineMath math="175" />, and forgetting the triangles with their base on <InlineMath math="h" /> gives <InlineMath math="45" />.

So <InlineMath math="73" /> triangles have a whole-number area.
