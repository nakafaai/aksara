You're looking for the length of the angle bisector <InlineMath math="AD" />, but the sides <InlineMath math="AB" /> and <InlineMath math="AC" /> aren't given. The plan has four steps: use the **angle bisector theorem** for the ratio of the sides, use the perimeter for their lengths, find <InlineMath math="\cos B" /> from the big triangle, and then use that <InlineMath math="\cos B" /> in triangle <InlineMath math="ABD" />.

# The ratio from the bisector

The angle bisector theorem says: the bisector of angle <InlineMath math="A" /> splits the opposite side **in the same ratio as the two sides that form the angle**.

<BlockMath math="\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{3}{4}" />

# Side lengths from the perimeter

The perimeter is the sum of all three sides, and **side <InlineMath math="BC" /> counts too**. The length is <InlineMath math="BC=3+4=7\text{ cm}" />, so the rest goes to <InlineMath math="AB" /> and <InlineMath math="AC" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
AB+AC &= 21-7=14 \\
AB &= \tfrac{3}{7}\cdot 14=6\text{ cm} \\
AC &= \tfrac{4}{7}\cdot 14=8\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

# The shared angle

Angle <InlineMath math="B" /> belongs to both triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" /> and triangle <InlineMath math="ABD" />. Find its cosine from triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" />, because there **all three sides are known**. The cosine rule links the side opposite an angle with the other two sides.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
AC^2 &= AB^2+BC^2-2\cdot AB\cdot BC\cos B \\
64 &= 36+49-84\cos B \\
\cos B &= \frac{21}{84}=\frac{1}{4}
\end{aligned}" />

# The length of the bisector

Now use the cosine rule in triangle <InlineMath math="ABD" />, with sides <InlineMath math="AB=6" /> and <InlineMath math="BD=3" /> and **the same angle <InlineMath math="B" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
AD^2 &= AB^2+BD^2-2\cdot AB\cdot BD\cos B \\
&= 36+9-2\cdot 6\cdot 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{4} \\
&= 45-9=36
\end{aligned}" />

A length can't be negative, so <InlineMath math="AD=6\text{ cm}" />.

# Checking through the other angle

Work it out from the other side. In triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" />, the cosine of angle <InlineMath math="C" /> is <InlineMath math="\cos C=\dfrac{64+49-36}{2\cdot 8\cdot 7}=\dfrac{11}{16}" />. In triangle <InlineMath math="ADC" />, with <InlineMath math="AC=8" /> and <InlineMath math="DC=4" />, you get the same result.

<BlockMath math="AD^2=64+16-2\cdot 8\cdot 4\cdot\tfrac{11}{16}=80-44=36" />

Two routes give **the same length**, so the result holds up.

# The wrong options

- <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{10}\text{ cm}" /> shows up if the ratio is flipped, with <InlineMath math="AB=8" /> and <InlineMath math="AC=6" />. That gives <InlineMath math="AD^2=40" />.

- <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{15}\text{ cm}" /> comes from the same shortcut with the wrong sign, <InlineMath math="AD^2=48+12=60" />.
- <InlineMath math="4\sqrt{6}\text{ cm}" /> shows up if the whole perimeter <InlineMath math="21" /> is shared between <InlineMath math="AB" /> and <InlineMath math="AC" />, so <InlineMath math="AB=9" />, <InlineMath math="AC=12" /> and <InlineMath math="AD^2=96" />.

So the length of <InlineMath math="AD" /> is <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" />.
