A statement is sufficient when **every polynomial that satisfies it gives the same value of <InlineMath math="P(2)" />**. The plan is to turn each statement into a general form of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />, find two examples with different values of <InlineMath math="P(2)" /> when a statement isn't enough, and then combine the two.

# Statement one alone

Polynomial division can always be written as divisor times quotient, plus remainder.

<BlockMath math="P(x)=(x-1)(x-3)\,H(x)+2x+1" />

Plug in <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=3" />, because the first part is zero there. You get <InlineMath math="P(1)=3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(3)=7" />. But <InlineMath math="x=2" /> **isn't a root of the divisor**, so <InlineMath math="P(2)=-H(2)+5" /> still depends on <InlineMath math="H" />.

- If <InlineMath math="H(x)=0" />, then <InlineMath math="P(x)=2x+1" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2)=5" />.
- If <InlineMath math="H(x)=1" />, then <InlineMath math="P(x)=x^2-2x+4" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2)=4" />.

Two different answers, so statement (1) alone isn't sufficient.

# Statement two alone

A parabola that's symmetric about the line <InlineMath math="x=1" /> has its vertex at <InlineMath math="x=1" />, so it has the form <InlineMath math="P(x)=a(x-1)^2+c" /> with <InlineMath math="a\ne 0" />. That gives <InlineMath math="P(2)=a+c" />, and **the values of <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> are still unknown**.

- If <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="c=0" />, then <InlineMath math="P(2)=1" />.
- If <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="c=1" />, then <InlineMath math="P(2)=2" />.

Two different answers, so statement (2) alone isn't sufficient either.

# Both statements together

Use the form from statement (2), then plug in the two values from statement (1). **Two conditions are enough for two unknowns**, <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="c" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(1) &= a\cdot 0+c=3 &&\Longrightarrow\; c=3 \\
P(3) &= 4a+c=7 &&\Longrightarrow\; a=1 \\
P(2) &= a+c=1+3=4
\end{aligned}" />

The polynomial must be <InlineMath math="P(x)=(x-1)^2+3=x^2-2x+4" />, so <InlineMath math="P(2)=4" />.

# Checking

Divide <InlineMath math="x^2-2x+4" /> by <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x-3)=x^2-4x+3" />. The quotient is <InlineMath math="1" /> and the remainder is <InlineMath math="(x^2-2x+4)-(x^2-4x+3)=2x+1" />, as statement (1) says. The graph has its vertex at <InlineMath math="x=1" />, as statement (2) says. So **this polynomial meets both conditions**.

# The wrong options

Each other option comes from **a shortcut that isn't allowed** or from counting too few conditions.

- The option "statement (1) alone is sufficient" usually comes from plugging <InlineMath math="x=2" /> into the remainder <InlineMath math="2x+1" /> to get <InlineMath math="5" />. That shortcut only works at the roots of the divisor, <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />.
- The option "statement (2) alone is sufficient" assumes the symmetry fixes the parabola, but the symmetry only gives <InlineMath math="P(2)=P(0)" />.
- The option "each alone is sufficient" combines both of those mistakes.
- The option "not sufficient" forgets that degree <InlineMath math="2" /> plus the symmetry leaves only two unknowns, and statement (1) gives exactly two conditions.

So the two statements together are sufficient, with <InlineMath math="P(2)=4" />, while neither statement alone is.
