The positions are different, so the order matters and this is **a permutation** problem. The restriction is easiest to handle by counting all arrangements and then **removing those that contain both Ani and Budi**.

# All arrangements without the condition

<BlockMath math="P(7,3)=7\cdot6\cdot5=210" />

# Forbidden arrangements

If Ani and Budi are both chosen, the third officer comes from the other 5 candidates. These three people are then assigned to the three positions in <InlineMath math="3!" /> ways.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{forbidden}&=5\cdot3!=30\\
\text{ways}&=210-30=180
\end{aligned}" />

Direct check: without Ani and Budi there are <InlineMath math="P(5,3)=60" /> ways, and with exactly one of them there are <InlineMath math="2\cdot\binom52\cdot3!=120" /> ways. The total is <InlineMath math="60+120=180" />.

The value 210 ignores the restriction. The value 150 removes 60 arrangements, counting the pair Ani and Budi twice.

So the number of ways to choose the three officers is **180**.
