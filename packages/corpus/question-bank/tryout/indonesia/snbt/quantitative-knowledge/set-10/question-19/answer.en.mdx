All four statements rest on the corner points of region <InlineMath math="D" />. The plan is to find the corners first and then use the main fact of linear programming: **the maximum of a linear function on a polygon region is always reached at a corner**. After that, check what this fact doesn't cover: whole-number conditions, an edge parallel to the function, and a constraint that changes.

# The corners of the region

Find where the two slanted lines meet by elimination. Multiply <InlineMath math="2x+y=10" /> by <InlineMath math="3" />, then subtract <InlineMath math="x+3y=14" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
6x+3y-(x+3y) &= 30-14 \\
5x &= 16 \\
x=\tfrac{16}{5},\quad y &= 10-\tfrac{32}{5}=\tfrac{18}{5}
\end{aligned}" />

The line <InlineMath math="2x+y=10" /> meets the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at <InlineMath math="(5,0)" />, and the line <InlineMath math="x+3y=14" /> meets the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis at <InlineMath math="\left(0,\tfrac{14}{3}\right)" />. The other intercepts, <InlineMath math="(0,10)" /> and <InlineMath math="(14,0)" />, break the other inequality. So the corners of <InlineMath math="D" /> are <InlineMath math="(0,0)" />, <InlineMath math="(5,0)" />, <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{16}{5},\tfrac{18}{5}\right)" /> and <InlineMath math="\left(0,\tfrac{14}{3}\right)" />.

# The first statement

Work out <InlineMath math="3x+4y" /> at every corner.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(0,0)&:\ 0, \qquad (5,0):\ 15 \\
\left(\tfrac{16}{5},\tfrac{18}{5}\right)&:\ \tfrac{48}{5}+\tfrac{72}{5}=24, \qquad \left(0,\tfrac{14}{3}\right):\ \tfrac{56}{3}
\end{aligned}" />

The largest value is <InlineMath math="24" />, and **that corner counts** because <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> are real numbers here. This statement is true.

# The second statement

The value <InlineMath math="24" /> happens at a point with fractional coordinates. For whole numbers the value is at most <InlineMath math="24" />, so you only need to check every whole-number point that gives <InlineMath math="24" /> or <InlineMath math="23" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
3x+4y=24&:\ (0,6),\ (4,3),\ (8,0) \\
3x+4y=23&:\ (1,5),\ (5,2)
\end{aligned}" />

Every one of them breaks a constraint: <InlineMath math="(0,6)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,5)" /> push <InlineMath math="x+3y" /> above <InlineMath math="14" />, while <InlineMath math="(4,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(8,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(5,2)" /> push <InlineMath math="2x+y" /> above <InlineMath math="10" />. The point <InlineMath math="(2,4)" /> meets <InlineMath math="2\cdot 2+4=8\le 10" /> and <InlineMath math="2+12=14\le 14" />, with value <InlineMath math="6+16=22" />. So **the whole-number maximum is <InlineMath math="22" />**, not <InlineMath math="23" />. Rounding the corner to <InlineMath math="(3,4)" /> fails too, because <InlineMath math="3+12=15>14" />. This statement is false.

# The third statement

The constraint <InlineMath math="2x+y\le 10" /> already makes sure <InlineMath math="2x+y" /> is at most <InlineMath math="10" />. The value <InlineMath math="10" /> is reached along the whole edge from <InlineMath math="(5,0)" /> to <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{16}{5},\tfrac{18}{5}\right)" />, for example at <InlineMath math="(5,0)" /> and at <InlineMath math="(4,2)" />. Because **the function runs parallel to one edge of the region**, the maximum is reached at infinitely many points. This statement is false.

# The fourth statement

The new constraint moves the meeting point of the two slanted lines.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
6x+3y-(x+3y) &= 30-17 \\
x=\tfrac{13}{5},\quad y &= 10-\tfrac{26}{5}=\tfrac{24}{5} \\
3x+4y &= \tfrac{39}{5}+\tfrac{96}{5}=27
\end{aligned}" />

The other corners give <InlineMath math="15" /> at <InlineMath math="(5,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{68}{3}" /> at <InlineMath math="\left(0,\tfrac{17}{3}\right)" />, both less than <InlineMath math="27" />. So the maximum rises from <InlineMath math="24" /> to <InlineMath math="27" />, which is <InlineMath math="3" />. The number <InlineMath math="4" /> shows up if you think <InlineMath math="y" /> rises by <InlineMath math="1" /> while <InlineMath math="x" /> stays put, but **the corner slides along the line <InlineMath math="2x+y=10" />**: <InlineMath math="x" /> drops by <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3}{5}" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> rises by <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6}{5}" />. This statement is false.

So only the first statement is true, and the second, third and fourth statements are false.
