The number of solutions of two linear equations depends on **whether the two lines intersect, are parallel, or coincide**. Compare the coefficients of the two equations.

# The case a equals one

For <InlineMath math="a=1" />, multiply the first equation by 3.

<BlockMath math="3x+6y=12" />

Its left side matches the second equation. If <InlineMath math="b=12" />, both equations **describe the same line**, so there are infinitely many solutions. The first statement is true. If <InlineMath math="b=10" />, the same left side would have to equal both 12 and 10, which is impossible. The lines are parallel with no solution, so the second statement is false.

# The case a equals two

The coefficient ratios <InlineMath math="2:3" /> and <InlineMath math="2:6" /> differ, so the lines **intersect at exactly one point**. The fourth statement is true. Find that point.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x+2y=4&\;\Longrightarrow\;x=2-y\\
3(2-y)+6y=12&\;\Longrightarrow\;y=2,\quad x=0
\end{aligned}" />

The solution is <InlineMath math="x=0" /> and <InlineMath math="y=2" />. The pair <InlineMath math="x=2" />, <InlineMath math="y=0" /> satisfies the first equation but gives <InlineMath math="3\cdot2+0=6\ne12" />. The third statement is false.

So the first statement is **true**, the second is **false**, the third is **false**, and the fourth is **true**.
