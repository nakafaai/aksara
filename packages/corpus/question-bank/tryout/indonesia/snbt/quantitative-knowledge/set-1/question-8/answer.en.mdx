Both <InlineMath math="4^x" /> and <InlineMath math="2^{x+3}" /> can be written in terms of <InlineMath math="2^x" />. So **let <InlineMath math="t=2^x" />**, which turns the equation into a quadratic in <InlineMath math="t" />.

# Turning it into a quadratic

Use <InlineMath math="4^x=\left(2^x\right)^2=t^2" /> and <InlineMath math="2^{x+3}=2^3\cdot2^x=8t" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
t^2-8t+12&=0\\
(t-2)(t-6)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

The roots are <InlineMath math="t=2" /> and <InlineMath math="t=6" />. Both are positive, so **both are valid values of <InlineMath math="2^x" />**, which is never zero or negative.

# Adding the solutions

Connect the product of the <InlineMath math="t" /> values with the sum of the exponents.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2^{x_1}\cdot2^{x_2}&=2\cdot6\\
2^{x_1+x_2}&=12\\
x_1+x_2&={}^{2}\log12={}^{2}\log4+{}^{2}\log3=2+{}^{2}\log3
\end{aligned}" />

The same result follows from <InlineMath math="x_1=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x_2={}^{2}\log6=1+{}^{2}\log3" />.

The value 3 comes from <InlineMath math="2^{x_1}+2^{x_2}=8=2^3" />, although **the sum of exponents comes from the product**, not from the sum. The values 6 and 8 are values of <InlineMath math="t" />, not of <InlineMath math="x" />. The value <InlineMath math="{}^{2}\log6" /> is only one of the solutions.

So <InlineMath math="x_1+x_2=2+{}^{2}\log3" />.
