Two chords that cross inside a circle satisfy **the intersecting chords theorem**: the product of the two parts of one chord equals the product of the two parts of the other.

<BlockMath math="AE\cdot EB=CE\cdot ED" />

# Why the products are equal

Triangles <InlineMath math="AEC" /> and <InlineMath math="DEB" /> are similar. Angles <InlineMath math="AEC" /> and <InlineMath math="DEB" /> are vertical angles, and angles <InlineMath math="CAB" /> and <InlineMath math="CDB" /> stand on the same arc <InlineMath math="CB" />. Similarity gives <InlineMath math="\frac{AE}{DE}=\frac{CE}{BE}" />, which **is equivalent to <InlineMath math="AE\cdot EB=CE\cdot ED" />**.

# Computing the length of CD

Write <InlineMath math="CE=3k" /> and <InlineMath math="ED=2k" /> according to the ratio <InlineMath math="3:2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
8\cdot3&=3k\cdot2k\\
24&=6k^2\\
k&=2
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="CE=6" />, <InlineMath math="ED=4" />, and <InlineMath math="CD=6+4=10\text{ cm}" />. **Check the products**: <InlineMath math="6\cdot4=24=8\cdot3" />.

The values 6 and 4 are only the parts <InlineMath math="CE" /> and <InlineMath math="ED" />. The value 5 stops at <InlineMath math="5k" /> with <InlineMath math="k=1" />, without using the theorem. The value 11 assumes the two chords are equal, although <InlineMath math="AB=11" /> and <InlineMath math="CD" /> need not match it.

So the length of chord <InlineMath math="CD" /> is **<InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />**.
