Since <InlineMath math="55=5\times11" /> and 5 and 11 share no factor other than 1, the remainder of <InlineMath math="n^2+n" /> on division by 55 **is fixed by its remainders on division by 5 and by 11**. Compute both remainders, then combine them.

# The remainder for each divisor

The remainder of an expression can be computed from the remainder of the number itself.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n^2+n&\equiv 2^2+2=6\equiv1 \pmod 5\\
n^2+n&\equiv 4^2+4=20\equiv9 \pmod{11}
\end{aligned}" />

So we need the number <InlineMath math="r" /> with <InlineMath math="0\le r<55" /> that **leaves 9 on division by 11 and 1 on division by 5**.

# Combining the two remainders

The candidates for <InlineMath math="r" /> are 9, 20, 31, 42, and 53. Their remainders on division by 5 are 4, 0, 1, 2, and 3. **Only 31** leaves remainder 1, so <InlineMath math="r=31" />.

Check with an actual number. The number <InlineMath math="n=37" /> satisfies both conditions, because <InlineMath math="37=7(5)+2" /> and <InlineMath math="37=3(11)+4" />. It gives <InlineMath math="37^2+37=1406=25(55)+31" />.

The value 37 is the remainder of <InlineMath math="n" /> itself. The value 49 is the remainder of <InlineMath math="n^2" /> without the term <InlineMath math="n" />. The values 6 and 20 use only one condition each, namely <InlineMath math="2^2+2" /> and <InlineMath math="4^2+4" />, without combining them.

So the remainder when <InlineMath math="n^2+n" /> is divided by 55 is **31**.
