# Finding the possible sides

In an isosceles triangle, the third side equals one of the two given sides. So the sides are <InlineMath math="4, 4, 7" /> or <InlineMath math="4, 7, 7" />. A triangle exists when **the two shorter sides add up to more than the longest side**. For <InlineMath math="4, 4, 7" />, <InlineMath math="4+4=8>7" />. For <InlineMath math="4, 7, 7" />, <InlineMath math="4+7=11>7" />. Both triangles exist.

# Computing the area in the first case

In the triangle <InlineMath math="4, 4, 7" />, the base is <InlineMath math="7" /> and both legs are <InlineMath math="4" />. The height from the top vertex **cuts the base into two equal parts**, each <InlineMath math="\frac72" />. Call that height <InlineMath math="t" /> and the area <InlineMath math="L_1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}" />

# Computing the area in the second case

In the triangle <InlineMath math="4, 7, 7" />, the base is <InlineMath math="4" /> and both legs are <InlineMath math="7" />. The height cuts the base into <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />. Call the area <InlineMath math="L_2" />.
