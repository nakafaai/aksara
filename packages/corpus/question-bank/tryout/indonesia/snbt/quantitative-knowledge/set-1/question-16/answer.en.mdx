Order the five numbers as <InlineMath math="a<b<c<d<e" />. The median of five values is the third value, so <InlineMath math="c=10" />. A mean of 12 means that **the five numbers always add up to 60**.

<BlockMath math="a+b+10+d+e=60\quad\Rightarrow\quad e=50-a-b-d" />

# Making the other numbers small

To make <InlineMath math="e" /> as large as possible, the other three numbers must be as small as the conditions allow. The smallest distinct positive integers below 10 are <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="b=2" />. The number <InlineMath math="d" /> must be **greater than the median** and different from it, so its smallest value is <InlineMath math="d=11" />.

<BlockMath math="e=50-1-2-11=36" />

Check: the data <InlineMath math="1,2,10,11,36" /> are all different, the median is 10, and the mean is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{60}{5}=12" />.

# The tempting wrong values

The value 37 uses <InlineMath math="d=10" />, although the numbers must be different. The value 38 uses <InlineMath math="1,1,10,10" />, which breaks the distinctness condition twice. The value 34 starts from <InlineMath math="a=2" /> and <InlineMath math="b=3" />, although 1 is also a positive integer. The value 46 leaves the median 10 out of the sum, as in <InlineMath math="60-1-2-11" />.

So the largest possible value of the greatest number is **36**.
