To build the new quadratic equation, you only need **the sum and the product of its two new roots**. The plan has three steps: find <InlineMath math="p" /> first, simplify the cubes with the help of the original equation, and then compute the sum and product of the new roots.

# Finding the missing coefficient

Vieta's formulas link the roots to the coefficients. For <InlineMath math="x^2+px+1=0" />, the sum of the roots is <InlineMath math="\alpha+\beta=-p" /> and their product is <InlineMath math="\alpha\beta=1" />. You can write the sum of the squares of the roots with these two.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\alpha^2+\beta^2&=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta\\
7&=p^2-2\\
p^2&=9
\end{aligned}" />

There are two candidates, <InlineMath math="p=3" /> or <InlineMath math="p=-3" />. The problem says **both roots are positive**, so their sum is positive: <InlineMath math="-p>0" />. The candidate <InlineMath math="p=3" /> is rejected because it gives a root sum of <InlineMath math="-3" />. So <InlineMath math="p=-3" />, the equation is <InlineMath math="x^2-3x+1=0" />, and <InlineMath math="\alpha+\beta=3" /> and <InlineMath math="\alpha\beta=1" />. The discriminant is <InlineMath math="9-4=5>0" />, so both roots really are real.

# Simplifying the cubes

Since <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> is a root, <InlineMath math="\alpha^2-3\alpha+1=0" />, or <InlineMath math="\alpha^2=3\alpha-1" />. That lets you **replace every <InlineMath math="\alpha^2" /> with <InlineMath math="3\alpha-1" />**. For <InlineMath math="\alpha^3" />, first multiply <InlineMath math="\alpha^2" /> by <InlineMath math="\alpha" />, then replace the <InlineMath math="\alpha^2" /> that shows up again.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\alpha^3&=\alpha\cdot\alpha^2=3\alpha^2-\alpha=3(3\alpha-1)-\alpha=8\alpha-3\\
\alpha^3-2\alpha^2&=(8\alpha-3)-2(3\alpha-1)=2\alpha-1
\end{aligned}" />

In the same way, <InlineMath math="\beta^3-2\beta^2=2\beta-1" />. So the two new roots are <InlineMath math="2\alpha-1" /> and <InlineMath math="2\beta-1" />.

# Building the new equation

Compute the sum and product of the new roots from <InlineMath math="\alpha+\beta=3" /> and <InlineMath math="\alpha\beta=1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(2\alpha-1)+(2\beta-1)&=2(\alpha+\beta)-2=6-2=4\\
(2\alpha-1)(2\beta-1)&=4\alpha\beta-2(\alpha+\beta)+1=4-6+1=-1
\end{aligned}" />

A quadratic equation whose roots have sum <InlineMath math="4" /> and product <InlineMath math="-1" /> follows **the sum and product pattern**, <InlineMath math="x^2-(\text{sum})x+(\text{product})=0" />. That gives <InlineMath math="x^2-4x-1=0" />.

# Checking the result

The roots of <InlineMath math="x^2-3x+1=0" /> are <InlineMath math="\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}" />, and both are positive. The new root <InlineMath math="2\alpha-1" /> becomes <InlineMath math="2+\sqrt5" />, and **substituting it into the new equation gives zero**.

<BlockMath math="(2+\sqrt5)^2-4(2+\sqrt5)-1=(9+4\sqrt5)-(8+4\sqrt5)-1=0" />

# The wrong options

The option <InlineMath math="x^2+32x+11=0" /> comes from **not rejecting the candidate <InlineMath math="p=3" />**, even though its roots are negative. The option <InlineMath math="x^2+4x-1=0" /> uses the sum of the roots with the wrong sign when the equation is built. The option <InlineMath math="x^2-4x+11=0" /> comes from a sign slip in the product, writing <InlineMath math="4\alpha\beta+2(\alpha+\beta)+1=11" />. The option <InlineMath math="x^2-5x-5=0" /> comes from writing <InlineMath math="\alpha^3" /> as <InlineMath math="3\alpha^2-1" />, which multiplies only the first term by <InlineMath math="\alpha" />.

So the quadratic equation whose roots are <InlineMath math="\alpha^3-2\alpha^2" /> and <InlineMath math="\beta^3-2\beta^2" /> is <InlineMath math="x^2-4x-1=0" />.
