You can get the radius from the chord <InlineMath math="TB" />, because that chord passes through the centre, so it's a **diameter**. The plan: use the tangent and secant relation to find <InlineMath math="PB" />, spot the right triangle <InlineMath math="PTB" />, then compute <InlineMath math="TB" /> and halve it.

# Finding the length of the secant

For a tangent and a secant drawn from one point outside a circle, **the power of a point** gives <InlineMath math="PT^2=PA\cdot PB" />. Let <InlineMath math="PA=x" />, so <InlineMath math="PB=x+7" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x(x+7)&=12^2\\
x^2+7x-144&=0\\
(x-9)(x+16)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

The candidates are <InlineMath math="x=9" /> or <InlineMath math="x=-16" />. A length can't be negative, so <InlineMath math="x=-16" /> is rejected. That gives <InlineMath math="PA=9\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="PB=16\text{ cm}" />.

# Spotting the right triangle

A tangent is always **perpendicular to the radius at the point where it touches**, so <InlineMath math="PT\perp OT" />. The chord <InlineMath math="TB" /> passes through <InlineMath math="O" />, so the line <InlineMath math="TB" /> is the same as the line <InlineMath math="TO" />. That makes angle <InlineMath math="PTB" /> a right angle, and the hypotenuse of triangle <InlineMath math="PTB" /> is <InlineMath math="PB" />.

# Computing the radius

Use the Pythagorean theorem in triangle <InlineMath math="PTB" />, and remember that <InlineMath math="TB" /> is a diameter, so **the radius is half of <InlineMath math="TB" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
TB^2&=PB^2-PT^2=256-144=112\\
TB&=\sqrt{112}=4\sqrt7\\
r&=\frac{TB}{2}=2\sqrt7
\end{aligned}" />

# Checking the result

Put the centre <InlineMath math="O" /> at the origin with <InlineMath math="r^2=28" />, <InlineMath math="T=(0,2\sqrt7)" />, <InlineMath math="B=(0,-2\sqrt7)" />, and <InlineMath math="P=(12,2\sqrt7)" />. The distance <InlineMath math="PB" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="PB^2=12^2+(4\sqrt7)^2=144+112=256" />, so <InlineMath math="PB=16" />. Then <InlineMath math="PA=\frac{PT^2}{PB}=\frac{144}{16}=9" />, so <InlineMath math="AB=16-9=7" />. Every given fact **checks out with this radius**.

# The wrong options

The option <InlineMath math="2\text{ cm}" /> comes from subtracting the lengths directly, <InlineMath math="TB=16-12=4" />, when the Pythagorean theorem works with squares. The option <InlineMath math="\frac72\text{ cm}" /> comes from treating <InlineMath math="AB" /> as a diameter. The option <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" /> comes from adding the squares, <InlineMath math="TB^2=256+144=400" />, as if <InlineMath math="PB" /> were a leg. The option <InlineMath math="4\sqrt7\text{ cm}" /> is the diameter <InlineMath math="TB" /> itself, which means **forgetting to halve it**.

So the radius of the circle is <InlineMath math="2\sqrt7\text{ cm}" />.
