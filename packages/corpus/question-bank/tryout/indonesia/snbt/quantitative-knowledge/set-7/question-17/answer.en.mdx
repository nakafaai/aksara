This is a yes-or-no question, so a piece of information is sufficient when the answer is **definitely "yes" or definitely "no"**. Here's the plan: write the final price as a multiple of the starting price, find the condition for the final price to be higher, then test each statement with examples.

# When the final price is higher

A rise of <InlineMath math="a\%" /> multiplies the price by <InlineMath math="1+\frac{a}{100}" />, and the drop of <InlineMath math="b\%" /> is taken from the new price, so it multiplies by <InlineMath math="1-\frac{b}{100}" />. Remember, **percentage changes multiply, they don't add**.

<BlockMath math="\left(1+\tfrac{a}{100}\right)\left(1-\tfrac{b}{100}\right)=1+\frac{a-b}{100}-\frac{ab}{10{,}000}" />

The final price is higher exactly when that factor is more than <InlineMath math="1" />, that is when <InlineMath math="100(a-b)>ab" />.

# Statement one alone

If <InlineMath math="a=b+5" />, the condition becomes <InlineMath math="500>ab" />, and you don't know <InlineMath math="ab" /> yet. For <InlineMath math="a=15" /> and <InlineMath math="b=10" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="1.15\times0.9=1.035" />, so the price goes up. For <InlineMath math="a=35" /> and <InlineMath math="b=30" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="1.35\times0.7=0.945" />, so the price goes down. **A positive gap between the percentages doesn't guarantee a rise**, so statement (1) alone isn't sufficient.

# Statement two alone

If <InlineMath math="ab=500" />, the condition becomes <InlineMath math="100(a-b)>500" />, that is <InlineMath math="a-b>5" />, and you don't know that gap yet. For <InlineMath math="a=50" /> and <InlineMath math="b=10" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="1.5\times0.9=1.35" />, so the price goes up. For <InlineMath math="a=10" /> and <InlineMath math="b=50" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="1.1\times0.5=0.55" />, so the price goes down. **A fixed product of the percentages doesn't fix the direction either**, so statement (2) alone isn't sufficient.

# Both statements together

Substitute <InlineMath math="a=b+5" /> into <InlineMath math="ab=500" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(b+5)b&=500\\
b^2+5b-500&=0\\
(b+25)(b-20)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

Since <InlineMath math="b>0" />, the value <InlineMath math="b=-25" /> is rejected, so <InlineMath math="b=20" /> and <InlineMath math="a=25" />. The factor is <InlineMath math="1.25\times0.8=1" />, which matches the formula: <InlineMath math="100(a-b)=500=ab" />. The final price is **exactly equal to the starting price**, so it isn't higher. The answer is definitely "no", and a definite answer means the information is sufficient.

There are two traps here. Seeing <InlineMath math="a>b" /> and concluding the price went up is wrong, and treating "the price stayed the same" as something that doesn't answer the question is wrong too.

So both statements together are sufficient to answer the question, but neither statement alone is.
