Factor the expression: <InlineMath math="n^3-n=(n-1)n(n+1)" />. So <InlineMath math="n^3-n" /> is **the product of three consecutive integers**, and its divisibility can be read from that.

# Divisibility by 6 and by 4

Among three consecutive integers there is always an even number and always a multiple of 3. Their product is **always divisible by <InlineMath math="2\cdot3=6" />**, so the first statement is true.

For <InlineMath math="n=2" />, the value is <InlineMath math="1\cdot2\cdot3=6" />, which is not divisible by 4. One counterexample is enough, so the second statement is false.

# The case of odd n

If <InlineMath math="n" /> is odd, then <InlineMath math="n-1" /> and <InlineMath math="n+1" /> are **two consecutive even numbers**. One of them is a multiple of 4, so their product is divisible by <InlineMath math="2\cdot4=8" />. A multiple of 3 is still among the three numbers, and 8 and 3 share no factor other than 1, so the product is divisible by 24. Example: <InlineMath math="n=5" /> gives <InlineMath math="4\cdot5\cdot6=120=5\cdot24" />. The third statement is true.

# Divisibility by 5

The example <InlineMath math="n=2" /> gives 6, which is not divisible by 5. Three consecutive integers **do not always contain a multiple of 5**, so the fourth statement is false.

So the first statement is **true**, the second is **false**, the third is **true**, and the fourth is **false**.
