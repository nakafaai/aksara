Let the craftsperson make <InlineMath math="x" /> bags of type A and <InlineMath math="y" /> bags of type B. This is **linear programming**: the profit is maximised over the region bounded by working hours and material.

# Model and corner points

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{hours}&:\quad 2x+3y\le60\\
\text{material}&:\quad x+y\le25\\
\text{profit}&:\quad K=40{,}000x+50{,}000y
\end{aligned}" />

Both variables are also non-negative, <InlineMath math="x\ge0" /> and <InlineMath math="y\ge0" />. A linear function reaches its maximum **at one of the corner points of the region**. The intersection of the two boundary lines comes from subtracting twice the material equation from the hours equation.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(2x+3y)-2(x+y)&=60-50\\
y&=10,\quad x=15
\end{aligned}" />

# Comparing the profits

| Corner point | Profit |
| :---: | :---: |
| <InlineMath math="(25,0)" /> | Rp1,000,000 |
| <InlineMath math="(15,10)" /> | Rp1,100,000 |
| <InlineMath math="(0,20)" /> | Rp1,000,000 |

The largest profit is at <InlineMath math="(15,10)" />, which uses exactly 60 hours and exactly 25 bags. **Both constraints are used up** at this point.

A profit of Rp1,250,000 makes 25 bags of type B, which would need 75 hours. A profit of Rp1,200,000 makes 30 bags of type A and breaks the material limit. A profit of Rp1,150,000 appears if the working hours of bags A and B are swapped.

So the maximum profit is **Rp1,100,000**.
