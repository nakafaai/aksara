The data doesn't fix every region of the Venn diagram, so each statement has to be tested against **every possible way of splitting the students**. A "must" statement needs a proof that works for every split, while a "possible" statement is settled by one example, or ruled out by showing that no split can do it.

# Naming each region of the diagram

Write <InlineMath math="F" />, <InlineMath math="M" />, and <InlineMath math="P" /> for the sets of students in Futsal, Music, and Pramuka (scouting). A Venn diagram of three sets has seven regions, and each region gets a letter as in the table.

| Region | Letter |
| :--- | :---: |
| Only Futsal, only Music, only Pramuka | <InlineMath math="a,\ b,\ c" /> |
| Futsal and Music only, Futsal and Pramuka only, Music and Pramuka only | <InlineMath math="x,\ y,\ z" /> |
| All three activities | <InlineMath math="t" /> |

There are <InlineMath math="40-5=35" /> students who take at least one activity. **The eight Futsal and Music students include those who also take Pramuka**, so the second fact means <InlineMath math="x+t=8" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a+x+y+t&=20\\
b+x+z+t&=18\\
c+y+z+t&=15\\
x+t&=8\\
a+b+c+x+y+z+t&=35
\end{aligned}" />

# Students who take only Pramuka

First count the students who take Futsal or Music. **The union rule for two sets** subtracts the overlap so the Futsal and Music students aren't counted twice.

<BlockMath math="n(F\cup M)=20+18-8=30" />

Of the <InlineMath math="35" /> students who take something, <InlineMath math="30" /> take Futsal or Music. The rest can only be in Pramuka alone, so <InlineMath math="c=35-30=5" /> in every split. The first statement is **true**.

# Students in Music and Pramuka

The students who take both Music and Pramuka number <InlineMath math="z+t" />. Try to build a split with <InlineMath math="z=0" /> and <InlineMath math="t=0" />. From <InlineMath math="x+t=8" /> you get <InlineMath math="x=8" />, the Pramuka equation gives <InlineMath math="5+y=15" />, so <InlineMath math="y=10" />, and then the Futsal and Music equations give <InlineMath math="a=2" /> and <InlineMath math="b=10" />.

| <InlineMath math="a" /> | <InlineMath math="b" /> | <InlineMath math="c" /> | <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="y" /> | <InlineMath math="z" /> | <InlineMath math="t" /> |

Check: <InlineMath math="2+10+5+8+10+0+0=35" />, and the three set sizes are still <InlineMath math="20" />, <InlineMath math="18" />, and <InlineMath math="15" />. This split is valid, yet **not a single student takes both Music and Pramuka**. The second statement is **false**.

# Students who take exactly two activities

Let <InlineMath math="e_1" />, <InlineMath math="e_2" />, and <InlineMath math="e_3" /> be the numbers of students who take exactly one, exactly two, and exactly three activities. The sum <InlineMath math="20+18+15" /> **counts each student once for every activity they take**, while <InlineMath math="35" /> counts each student once.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
e_1+2e_2+3e_3&=53\\
e_1+e_2+e_3&=35\\
e_2+2e_3&=18
\end{aligned}" />

The third line is the difference of the first two. So <InlineMath math="e_2=18-2e_3" />, an even number minus an even number. The result is always even, so the third statement is **true**.

# Students who take all three activities

From <InlineMath math="e_2+2e_3=18" /> you only get <InlineMath math="e_3\le9" />, and that bound is tempting. But every student who takes all three activities also takes Futsal and Music, so **they're among the eight Futsal and Music students**.

<BlockMath math="t=8-x\le8" />

So at most <InlineMath math="8" /> students take all three activities, and exactly <InlineMath math="9" /> is impossible. The fourth statement is **false**.

So the first statement is true, the second is false, the third is true, and the fourth is false.
