The number of positive divisors depends on the exponents in the prime factorisation. If <InlineMath math="n=p^{a}q^{b}\cdots" />, then **the number of divisors is <InlineMath math="(a+1)(b+1)\cdots" />**.

# Forms that give 10 divisors

The product <InlineMath math="(a+1)(b+1)\cdots" /> must be 10. The number 10 can only be written as <InlineMath math="10" /> or <InlineMath math="2\times5" />, so there are **two possible factorisation forms**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n&=p^{9}\\
n&=p^{4}q^{1}
\end{aligned}" />

# The smallest value of each form

The smallest number comes from giving **the largest exponent to the smallest prime**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
p^{9}&:\quad 2^{9}=512\\
p^{4}q&:\quad 2^{4}\cdot3=48
\end{aligned}" />

Check: the positive divisors of 48 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24 and 48, exactly 10 of them.

The numbers 80 and 162 also have 10 divisors, but they are larger than 48 because they use the prime 5 or give the exponent 4 to the prime 3. The number 36 has 9 divisors and the number 24 has 8.

So the smallest positive integer with exactly 10 positive divisors is **48**.
