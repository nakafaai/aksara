There are two routes: compute the new data from scratch, or use **how the standard deviation reacts to multiplying and adding**. The second is faster, but first we compute the standard deviation of the original data.

# Standard deviation of the original data

The mean of the original data is 6. **The deviations from the mean** are <InlineMath math="-4,-2,0,2,4" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
s^2&=\frac{16+4+0+4+16}{5}=\frac{40}{5}=8\\
s&=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2
\end{aligned}" />

# Effect of the transformation

Adding 5 shifts every value and the mean by 5, so **the deviations from the mean do not change**. Multiplying by 3 makes every deviation 3 times larger, so the standard deviation is also multiplied by 3.

<BlockMath math="s_{\text{new}}=3\cdot2\sqrt2=6\sqrt2" />

Check: the new data 11, 17, 23, 29, 35 have mean 23 and deviations <InlineMath math="-12,-6,0,6,12" />, so the variance is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{360}{5}=72" /> and the standard deviation is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt2" />.

The value <InlineMath math="6\sqrt2+5" /> also adds 5, although a shift does not change the spread. The value <InlineMath math="2\sqrt2" /> ignores the multiplication, <InlineMath math="18\sqrt2" /> multiplies by 9, and 72 is the variance, not the standard deviation.

So the standard deviation of the new data is **<InlineMath math="6\sqrt2" />**.
