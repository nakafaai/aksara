This is a conditional probability. Knowing that the product is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> **shrinks the sample space**: you only count the rolls that meet that condition, then see how many of them have a prime sum. The two dice can be told apart, so <InlineMath math="(1,4)" /> and <InlineMath math="(4,1)" /> count separately, and all <InlineMath math="36" /> ordered pairs are equally likely.

# Building the new sample space

A product is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> when it contains at least two factors of <InlineMath math="2" />. The faces <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="6" /> each contain one factor of <InlineMath math="2" />, the face <InlineMath math="4" /> contains two, and the odd faces contain none. So the condition holds **when one die shows <InlineMath math="4" />, or both dice show <InlineMath math="2" /> or <InlineMath math="6" />**.

The new sample space has <InlineMath math="1+3+1+6+1+3=15" /> outcomes.

# Finding the prime sums

A sum of <InlineMath math="2" /> only comes from <InlineMath math="(1,1)" />, whose product is <InlineMath math="1" />, so it's impossible. Every other prime is odd. Two faces have an odd sum exactly when **one die is even and the other is odd**. The odd die adds no factor of <InlineMath math="2" />, so the even die has to supply both factors on its own, which means **the even die must show <InlineMath math="4" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
4+1 &= 5\ \text{(prime)} \\
4+3 &= 7\ \text{(prime)} \\
4+5 &= 9\ \text{(not prime)}
\end{aligned}" />

Each pair can come in two orders, so the matching outcomes are <InlineMath math="(4,1)" />, <InlineMath math="(1,4)" />, <InlineMath math="(4,3)" />, and <InlineMath math="(3,4)" />. That's <InlineMath math="4" /> outcomes.

# Working out the probability

A conditional probability is **the number of matching outcomes divided by the size of the new sample space**.

<BlockMath math="P=\frac{4}{15}" />

Sanity check: this is larger than the unconditional <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4}{36}" />, because the condition removes <InlineMath math="21" /> outcomes that fail it while all four matching outcomes stay.

# Why the other options fail

Each wrong option **uses the wrong sample space** or miscounts the orders.

- <InlineMath math="\tfrac19=\tfrac{4}{36}" /> uses all <InlineMath math="36" /> outcomes, so it ignores the condition.
- <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{15}" /> only counts <InlineMath math="(4,1)" /> and <InlineMath math="(4,3)" />, missing the reverse orders.
- <InlineMath math="\tfrac29" /> counts unordered pairs, <InlineMath math="2" /> out of <InlineMath math="9" />. Unordered pairs aren't equally likely, since <InlineMath math="\{4,4\}" /> can only happen one way.
- <InlineMath math="\tfrac14=\tfrac{4}{16}" /> comes from counting <InlineMath math="(4,4)" /> twice, once as the first die showing <InlineMath math="4" /> and once as the second.

So the probability that the sum of the two faces is prime is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4}{15}" />.
