An absolute value changes form where its inside equals zero, at <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=7" />. These two points split the number line into three regions, and **each region is solved separately**.

# Three regions

For <InlineMath math="x<1" />, both insides are negative. For <InlineMath math="1\le x\le7" />, only the second inside is negative. For <InlineMath math="x>7" />, both are positive.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x<1:&\quad (1-x)+(7-x)=10 \;\Rightarrow\; 8-2x=10 \;\Rightarrow\; x=-1\\
1\le x\le7:&\quad (x-1)+(7-x)=6\ne10\\
x>7:&\quad (x-1)+(x-7)=10 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x-8=10 \;\Rightarrow\; x=9
\end{aligned}" />

The value <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> is indeed less than 1 and <InlineMath math="x=9" /> is indeed greater than 7, so **both are accepted**. The middle region gives no solution.

# Meaning as distance

The expression <InlineMath math="\lvert x-1\rvert+\lvert x-7\rvert" /> is **the sum of the distances from <InlineMath math="x" /> to the points 1 and 7**. Between the two points, this sum is always 6. For a sum of 10, the point <InlineMath math="x" /> must lie 2 units outside one of the ends, at <InlineMath math="-1" /> or at <InlineMath math="9" />.

<BlockMath math="(-1)(9)=-9" />

The values 9 and <InlineMath math="-1" /> use only one solution. The value 8 is the sum of the two solutions, not their product. The value 7 is the product of the boundary points 1 and 7.

So the product of all solutions is **<InlineMath math="-9" />**.
