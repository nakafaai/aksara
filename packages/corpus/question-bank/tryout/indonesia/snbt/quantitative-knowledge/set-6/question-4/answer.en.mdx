You're looking for every integer that satisfies the inequality, and then their sum. Three things need care: **the domain comes from the original form**, **the sign flips because the base is less than one**, and the two conditions get combined at the end.

# Finding the domain

A logarithm is only defined for positive inputs. This inequality has two logarithms, so both inputs must be positive.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x-1>0 &\quad\text{and}\quad x-3>0 \\
x>1 &\quad\text{and}\quad x>3
\end{aligned}" />

Both must hold at once, so the condition is <InlineMath math="x>3" />. It **has to come from the original form**, before the two logarithms are merged. After merging, the input becomes <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x-3)" />, which is also positive for <InlineMath math="x<1" />, even though both original logarithms are undefined there.

# Merging the logarithms

A sum of two logarithms with the same base is **the logarithm of the product**, not of the sum. Write the right side as a base <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" /> logarithm too, since <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac12\right)^{-3}=2^3=8" />.

<BlockMath math="\log_{\frac{1}{2}}\big((x-1)(x-3)\big)\ge\log_{\frac{1}{2}}8" />

# Flipping the sign because of the base

With base <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" />, a larger input gives a smaller logarithm. For example, <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}2=-1" /> but <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}8=-3" />. So **when you drop the logarithms, the inequality sign flips**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(x-1)(x-3) &\le 8 \\
x^2-4x+3 &\le 8 \\
x^2-4x-5 &\le 0 \\
(x-5)(x+1) &\le 0 \\
-1\le x &\le 5
\end{aligned}" />

# Combining both conditions

The overlap of <InlineMath math="x>3" /> and <InlineMath math="-1\le x\le5" /> is <InlineMath math="3<x\le5" />. **The only integers are <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="5" />**, so the sum is <InlineMath math="4+5=9" />.

Check against the original inequality. At <InlineMath math="x=5" /> the value sits exactly on the boundary, and at <InlineMath math="x=6" /> it has already gone past it.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x=5:\quad \log_{\frac{1}{2}}4+\log_{\frac{1}{2}}2 &= -2+(-1)=-3 \\
x=6:\quad \log_{\frac{1}{2}}5+\log_{\frac{1}{2}}3 &= \log_{\frac{1}{2}}15<\log_{\frac{1}{2}}8=-3
\end{aligned}" />

The value <InlineMath math="-3" /> satisfies greater than or equal to, while <InlineMath math="x=6" /> doesn't. At <InlineMath math="x=4" /> the sum is <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}3" />, which is more than <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}8=-3" />.

# Why the other options fail

Each wrong option **breaks one of the three rules above**.

- <InlineMath math="14" /> is the sum of the integers from <InlineMath math="-1" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />, which is what you get if you forget the domain. You get the same sum if you only use <InlineMath math="x>1" />.
- <InlineMath math="8" /> is <InlineMath math="-1+0+4+5" />, which comes from taking the domain from <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x-3)>0" />. But at <InlineMath math="x=0" /> the term <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}(-1)" /> isn't defined.
- <InlineMath math="4" /> comes from reading greater than or equal to as greater than, which throws out <InlineMath math="x=5" />.
- <InlineMath math="15" /> comes from merging the two logarithms into <InlineMath math="\log_{\frac12}\big((x-1)+(x-3)\big)" />. Then <InlineMath math="2x-4\le8" /> and <InlineMath math="x>3" /> give <InlineMath math="4+5+6=15" />, but at <InlineMath math="x=6" /> the product <InlineMath math="5\cdot3=15" /> is more than <InlineMath math="8" />.

So the sum of all integers that satisfy the inequality is <InlineMath math="9" />.
