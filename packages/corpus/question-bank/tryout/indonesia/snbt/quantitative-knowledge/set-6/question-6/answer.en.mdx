Factor the left side into linear factors, then **check the sign of the product on each interval** between its zeros.

<BlockMath math="\left(x^2-9\right)(x-5)=(x+3)(x-3)(x-5)" />

# The sign on each interval

The zeros are <InlineMath math="-3" />, 3, and 5. The sign of the product changes each time it passes one zero.

| Interval | <InlineMath math="x+3" /> | <InlineMath math="x-3" /> | <InlineMath math="x-5" /> | Product |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| <InlineMath math="x<-3" /> | negative | negative | negative | negative |
| <InlineMath math="-3<x<3" /> | positive | negative | negative | positive |
| <InlineMath math="3<x<5" /> | positive | positive | negative | negative |
| <InlineMath math="x>5" /> | positive | positive | positive | positive |

Because the sign is less than or equal to, **the zeros are included**. The solution is <InlineMath math="x\le-3" /> or <InlineMath math="3\le x\le5" />.

# Counting the integers

On <InlineMath math="-10\le x\le-3" /> there are 8 integers, and on <InlineMath math="3\le x\le5" /> there are 3, namely 3, 4, and 5.

<BlockMath math="8+3=11" />

The value 8 uses strictly less than and so drops <InlineMath math="-3" />, 3, and 5. The value 13 uses the positive region. The value 3 counts only the part <InlineMath math="3\le x\le5" />, and the value 7 counts the left part without <InlineMath math="-3" />.

So **11** integers satisfy the inequality.
