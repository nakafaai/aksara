You're looking for the largest difference between two repeating decimals. The plan: turn each repeating decimal into a fraction, use the sum equation to get <InlineMath math="a+b" />, then pick the pair of digits that makes the difference as large as possible. The last step hides a condition that's easy to miss: **<InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are single digits**, so neither can be more than <InlineMath math="9" />.

# Turning repeating decimals into fractions

Let <InlineMath math="x=0.\overline{ab}" />. The repeating block is two digits long, so multiply <InlineMath math="x" /> by <InlineMath math="100" />. That shifts one block to the left of the decimal point, and the tail after the point stays exactly the same. Because the tails match, **the tail cancels when you subtract <InlineMath math="x" /> from <InlineMath math="100x" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
100x &= (10a+b)+0.\overline{ab} \\
100x-x &= 10a+b \\
x &= \dfrac{10a+b}{99}
\end{aligned}" />

Here <InlineMath math="10a+b" /> is the two-digit number with tens digit <InlineMath math="a" /> and units digit <InlineMath math="b" />. In the same way, <InlineMath math="0.\overline{ba}=\dfrac{10b+a}{99}" />. The number <InlineMath math="1.\overline{3}" /> repeats only one digit, so multiplying by <InlineMath math="10" /> is enough.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y &= 1.\overline{3} \\
10y &= 13.\overline{3} \\
10y-y &= 12 \\
y &= \dfrac{12}{9}=\dfrac{4}{3}
\end{aligned}" />

# Finding the sum of the digits

Now add the two fractions. The numerator factors, because <InlineMath math="10a+b+10b+a=11a+11b" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{10a+b}{99}+\dfrac{10b+a}{99} &= \dfrac{11(a+b)}{99}=\dfrac{a+b}{9} \\
\dfrac{a+b}{9} &= \dfrac{12}{9} \\
a+b &= 12
\end{aligned}" />

So **the two digits must add up to <InlineMath math="12" />**.

# Making the difference as large as possible

Work out the difference the same way. This time the numerator is <InlineMath math="10a+b-10b-a=9a-9b" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
0.\overline{ab}-0.\overline{ba} &= \dfrac{9(a-b)}{99} \\
&= \dfrac{a-b}{11}
\end{aligned}" />

This difference grows as <InlineMath math="a-b" /> grows. From <InlineMath math="a+b=12" /> you get <InlineMath math="a-b=12-2b" />, so <InlineMath math="b" /> should be as small as possible. But **<InlineMath math="a=12-b" /> can't be more than <InlineMath math="9" />**, which forces <InlineMath math="b\ge3" />. The only possible pairs are <InlineMath math="(9,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(8,4)" />, and <InlineMath math="(7,5)" />. The largest <InlineMath math="a-b" /> is <InlineMath math="9-3=6" />, so the largest value is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{6}{11}" />.

Check it with <InlineMath math="a=9" /> and <InlineMath math="b=3" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
0.\overline{93}+0.\overline{39} &= \dfrac{93+39}{99}=\dfrac{132}{99}=\dfrac{4}{3} \\
0.\overline{93}-0.\overline{39} &= \dfrac{93-39}{99}=\dfrac{54}{99}=\dfrac{6}{11}
\end{aligned}" />

The sum really is <InlineMath math="1.\overline{3}" />, and the difference is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{6}{11}=0.\overline{54}" />.

# Why the other options fail

Each wrong option comes from **one step that's skipped or mixed up**.

- <InlineMath math="\dfrac{10}{11}" /> comes from taking <InlineMath math="a=11" /> and <InlineMath math="b=1" /> to make the gap large, but <InlineMath math="11" /> isn't a single digit.
- <InlineMath math="\dfrac{2}{3}" /> comes from writing the difference as <InlineMath math="\dfrac{a-b}{9}" />, as if the repeating block had only one digit.
- <InlineMath math="\dfrac{2}{33}" /> comes from writing the numerator of the difference as <InlineMath math="a-b" />, when <InlineMath math="(10a+b)-(10b+a)=9(a-b)" />.
- <InlineMath math="\dfrac{31}{33}" /> is just the value of <InlineMath math="0.\overline{93}" />, so it stops before subtracting <InlineMath math="0.\overline{39}" />.

So the largest possible value of <InlineMath math="0.\overline{ab}-0.\overline{ba}" /> is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{6}{11}" />.
