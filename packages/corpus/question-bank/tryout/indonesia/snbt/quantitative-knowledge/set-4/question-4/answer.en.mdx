You're after the probability that the product is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> but not by <InlineMath math="8" />. That condition means **the product contains exactly two factors of <InlineMath math="2" />**. Here's the plan: sort the numbers by how many factors of <InlineMath math="2" /> they have, find the pairs whose factors add up to exactly two, then divide by the number of all pairs.

# The sample space

The two numbers are taken at the same time, so order doesn't matter and **the sample space is made of combinations**.

<BlockMath math="n(S)=\binom{20}{2}=\frac{20\cdot19}{2}=190" />

# Grouping by factors of two

The number of factors of <InlineMath math="2" /> in a product is the sum of the factors of <InlineMath math="2" /> in its two numbers. So for each number you only need to record how many times it can be divided by <InlineMath math="2" />.

| Group | Members | Factors of two | Size |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| odd | <InlineMath math="1, 3, 5, \ldots, 19" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| plain even | <InlineMath math="2, 6, 10, 14, 18" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| multiples of four only | <InlineMath math="4, 12, 20" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| multiples of eight | <InlineMath math="8, 16" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> or more | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

Notice that <InlineMath math="8" /> and <InlineMath math="16" /> **don't belong to the multiples of four only**, because they already contain three or more factors of <InlineMath math="2" />.

# Counting the right pairs

The factors of <InlineMath math="2" /> add up to exactly two in only two ways: one odd number with one number from the multiples of four only, or **two numbers from the plain even group**, such as <InlineMath math="2\cdot6=12" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{odd and multiple of four only}&=10\cdot3=30\\
\text{two plain even}&=\binom{5}{2}=10\\
n(A)&=30+10=40\\
P(A)&=\frac{40}{190}=\frac{4}{19}
\end{aligned}" />

Check it another way. The pairs whose product is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> number <InlineMath math="95" />, and those divisible by <InlineMath math="8" /> number <InlineMath math="55" />. The difference is <InlineMath math="95-55=40" />, matching the count above.

# The tempting wrong options

Each other option comes from **a forgotten group or a mix-up about order**.

- <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{19}" /> comes from forgetting the pairs of two plain even numbers, like <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="6" />, so only <InlineMath math="30" /> pairs get counted.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{5}{19}" /> comes from counting the plain even pairs in order, <InlineMath math="5\cdot4=20" />, even though taking two at once ignores order.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{6}{19}" /> comes from treating <InlineMath math="8" /> and <InlineMath math="16" /> as having only two factors of <InlineMath math="2" />, which makes the odd and multiple of four pairs <InlineMath math="10\cdot5=50" />.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{8}{19}" /> comes from counting the favorable pairs in order, <InlineMath math="80" />, while keeping <InlineMath math="190" /> as the sample space.

So the probability that the product is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" /> but not by <InlineMath math="8" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{4}{19}" />.
