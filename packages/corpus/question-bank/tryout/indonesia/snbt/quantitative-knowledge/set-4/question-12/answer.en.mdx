Given:

- Total number of students: <InlineMath math="S = 800" />
- Students who like Mathematics: <InlineMath math="M = 400" />
- Students who like Physics: <InlineMath math="F = 500" />

We want to find the maximum value (<InlineMath math="x" />) and minimum value (<InlineMath math="y" />) of the intersection of these two sets (students who like both).

#### Finding the Maximum Intersection

The number of students who like both will be maximum if the smaller set is a subset of the larger set.
Here, the number of students who like Mathematics (<InlineMath math="400" />) is less than those who like Physics (<InlineMath math="500" />).
So, the maximum value of the intersection is the number of students in the smallest group.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x = 400" />
</MathContainer>

#### Finding the Minimum Intersection

The intersection is smallest when the union is as large as possible. Because the school has only <InlineMath math="800" /> students, <InlineMath math="n(M \cup F)" /> cannot exceed <InlineMath math="800" />.

Using <InlineMath math="n(M \cup F) = n(M) + n(F) - n(M \cap F)" />, the smallest possible intersection occurs when the union reaches that upper bound. This arrangement is feasible: every student likes at least one of the two subjects.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="800 = 400 + 500 - y" />
  <BlockMath math="800 = 900 - y" />
  <BlockMath math="y = 900 - 800" />
  <BlockMath math="y = 100" />
</MathContainer>

#### Calculating the Difference

Finally, we calculate the difference between the maximum and minimum values.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x - y = 400 - 100 = 300" />
</MathContainer>

So, the value of <InlineMath math="x - y" /> is <InlineMath math="300\text{ people}" />.
