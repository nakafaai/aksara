Write <InlineMath math="n^2+n=n(n+1)" />. This is **a product of two consecutive integers**, so one factor is always even. Since <InlineMath math="6=2\times3" />, divisibility by 6 depends only on the factor 3.

# The condition for the factor three

The prime 3 divides <InlineMath math="n(n+1)" /> exactly when 3 divides <InlineMath math="n" /> or 3 divides <InlineMath math="n+1" />. The second condition means that <InlineMath math="n" /> **leaves remainder 2 on division by 3**.

| Remainder of <InlineMath math="n" /> on division by 3 | Example | Is <InlineMath math="n(n+1)" /> divisible by 3? |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 0 | <InlineMath math="3\times4=12" /> | Yes |
| 1 | <InlineMath math="1\times2=2" /> | No |
| 2 | <InlineMath math="2\times3=6" /> | Yes |

# Counting the numbers

The multiples of 3 from 1 to 100 are 3, 6, up to 99, which is 33 numbers. The numbers with remainder 2 are 2, 5, up to 98, which is <InlineMath math="\frac{98-2}{3}+1=33" /> numbers.

<BlockMath math="33+33=66" />

The value 33 counts only the multiples of 3. The value 67 uses remainder 1, although <InlineMath math="n+1" /> divisible by 3 means that <InlineMath math="n" /> leaves remainder 2. The value 50 counts only the even numbers, although the factor 2 is always there. The value 100 assumes that a product of two consecutive integers is always divisible by 6, which is true only for a product of three consecutive integers.

So **66** numbers <InlineMath math="n" /> satisfy the condition.
