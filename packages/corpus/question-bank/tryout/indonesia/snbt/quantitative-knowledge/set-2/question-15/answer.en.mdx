The variance is the mean of the squared deviations, so you need **the sum of the squared deviations** and the number of data values. The plan: find <InlineMath math="x" /> from the condition on the mean, turn the standard deviation into a variance, find the sum of squared deviations of the old data, add the deviations of the two new values, then divide by the new number of values.

# Finding x

The old data add up to <InlineMath math="10\times15=150" />. The new data hold <InlineMath math="12" /> numbers with mean <InlineMath math="15" />, so they add up to <InlineMath math="12\times15=180" />. That means **the two new values must add up to <InlineMath math="2\times15=30" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x+2x&=180-150=30\\
x&=10
\end{aligned}" />

The two new values are <InlineMath math="10" /> and <InlineMath math="20" />.

# Deviations of the old data

The problem gives the standard deviation <InlineMath math="2" />, so **the old variance is <InlineMath math="2^2=4" />**, not <InlineMath math="2" />. The mean stays at <InlineMath math="15" />, so the deviation of every old value stays the same. The sum of squared deviations of the old data is the variance times the number of values: <InlineMath math="10\times4=40" />.

# The new variance

Add the squared deviations of the two new values, then **divide by the new count**, which is <InlineMath math="12" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(10-15)^2+(20-15)^2&=25+25=50\\
\text{new variance}&=\frac{40+50}{12}=\frac{90}{12}=\frac{15}{2}
\end{aligned}" />

Check it with real data. Five copies of <InlineMath math="13" /> and five copies of <InlineMath math="17" /> have mean <InlineMath math="15" /> and variance <InlineMath math="4" />, so their standard deviation is <InlineMath math="2" />.
