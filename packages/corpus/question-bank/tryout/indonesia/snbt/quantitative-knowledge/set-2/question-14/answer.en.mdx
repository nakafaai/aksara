The main tool is **the remainder theorem**: the remainder when a polynomial is divided by <InlineMath math="x-a" /> equals its value at <InlineMath math="x=a" />. So <InlineMath math="P(1)=3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2)=5" />. A remainder on division by a degree-two polynomial has degree at most one, and it's fixed by the values at the two roots of the divisor.

First, find the remainder of <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> on division by <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x-2)" />. Write <InlineMath math="P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)H(x)+px+q" /> with quotient <InlineMath math="H(x)" />, then plug in <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
p+q&=3\\
2p+q&=5\\
p=2,\quad q&=1
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)H(x)+2x+1" />, where <InlineMath math="H(x)" /> can be any polynomial.

# The remainder of the square

The value of <InlineMath math="\left(P(x)\right)^2" /> is <InlineMath math="9" /> at <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="25" /> at <InlineMath math="x=2" />. The remainder has the form <InlineMath math="sx+t" />, so <InlineMath math="s+t=9" /> and <InlineMath math="2s+t=25" />, which gives <InlineMath math="s=16" /> and <InlineMath math="t=-7" />. The remainder **isn't <InlineMath math="(2x+1)^2" />**, because that has degree two and can still be divided further.

<BlockMath math="(2x+1)^2=4x^2+4x+1=4\left(x^2-3x+2\right)+16x-7" />

The first statement is **true**.

# The value at three

Plug <InlineMath math="x=3" /> into the general form: <InlineMath math="P(3)=2\cdot1\cdot H(3)+7=2H(3)+7" />. This value depends on <InlineMath math="H" />, so **values away from one and two aren't locked in**. For <InlineMath math="H(x)=0" />, <InlineMath math="P(3)=7" />. For <InlineMath math="H(x)=1" />, <InlineMath math="P(x)=x^2-x+3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(3)=9" />. Both polynomials fit the given facts, but their values at <InlineMath math="3" /> differ. The second statement is **false**.

# The remainder after a shift

Let <InlineMath math="G(x)=P(x+1)" />. The divisor <InlineMath math="x^2-x=x(x-1)" /> has roots <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />, so what you need are **the values at the new divisor's roots**: <InlineMath math="G(0)=P(1)=3" /> and <InlineMath math="G(1)=P(2)=5" />. The remainder is <InlineMath math="ux+v" /> with <InlineMath math="v=3" /> and <InlineMath math="u+v=5" />, so <InlineMath math="u=2" /> and the remainder is <InlineMath math="2x+3" />. The third statement is **true**.

# A polynomial of degree three

For the word "there is", **one example is enough**. From <InlineMath math="P(3)=2H(3)+7=0" />, you need <InlineMath math="H(3)=-\tfrac72" />. Pick <InlineMath math="H(x)=x-\tfrac{13}{2}" />, which has degree one, so <InlineMath math="P" /> has degree three.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(x)&=(x-1)(x-2)\left(x-\tfrac{13}{2}\right)+2x+1\\
P(3)&=2\cdot1\cdot\left(-\tfrac72\right)+7=0
\end{aligned}" />

This polynomial also satisfies <InlineMath math="P(1)=3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2)=5" />, because the product in front is zero at both points. The fourth statement is **true**.

So the first statement is true, the second is false, the third is true, and the fourth is true.
