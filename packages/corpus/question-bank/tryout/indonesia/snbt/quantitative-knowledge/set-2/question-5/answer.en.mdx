Both quantities are fixed by <InlineMath math="u_1=4" /> and the ratio <InlineMath math="r" />. The ratio is hidden in the rule <InlineMath math="u_{n+2}=u_{n+1}+u_n" />, and **the convergence condition** picks one of two candidate ratios. After that, compute <InlineMath math="P" /> and <InlineMath math="Q" /> exactly and compare them.

# Finding the ratio

Write <InlineMath math="u_n=4r^{n-1}" />. The ratio <InlineMath math="r" /> can't be <InlineMath math="0" />, because <InlineMath math="r=0" /> gives <InlineMath math="u_3=0" />, while <InlineMath math="u_2+u_1=4" />. Divide the rule by <InlineMath math="u_n=4r^{n-1}" />, which isn't zero.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
4r^{n+1}&=4r^{n}+4r^{n-1}\\
r^2&=r+1\\
r&=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\ \text{or}\ r=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}
\end{aligned}" />

An infinite geometric series **converges only when <InlineMath math="|r|<1" />**. Since <InlineMath math="\sqrt5>2" />, the first candidate is more than <InlineMath math="\tfrac32" /> and drops out. The second candidate satisfies <InlineMath math="-1<\tfrac{1-\sqrt5}{2}<0" /> because <InlineMath math="2<\sqrt5<3" />. So <InlineMath math="r=\tfrac{1-\sqrt5}{2}" />, and the ratio is negative.

# Computing both quantities

For <InlineMath math="P" />, use the infinite-sum formula <InlineMath math="\tfrac{u_1}{1-r}" />. For <InlineMath math="Q" />, **replace <InlineMath math="r^2" /> with <InlineMath math="r+1" />** so you don't have to square a radical.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P&=\frac{4}{1-r}=\frac{4}{\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}}=\frac{8}{1+\sqrt5}=\frac{8(\sqrt5-1)}{4}=2\sqrt5-2\\
Q&=4\left(1+r+r^2\right)=4\left(2+2r\right)=8(1+r)=8\cdot\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}=12-4\sqrt5
\end{aligned}" />

# Comparing the radicals

Find the difference, then compare two positive numbers through their squares.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Q-P&=12-4\sqrt5-2\sqrt5+2=14-6\sqrt5\\
14^2=196&>180=\left(6\sqrt5\right)^2
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="14>6\sqrt5" />, which means <InlineMath math="Q-P>0" />. Check it a second way. Since <InlineMath math="r<0" />, **the terms alternate in sign**, and <InlineMath math="u_4=4r^3" /> is negative. The rest of the series after the third term is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{u_4}{1-r}" />, which is also negative, so <InlineMath math="P=Q+\tfrac{u_4}{1-r}" /> is smaller than <InlineMath math="Q" />.

# Traps in the other options

- <InlineMath math="P>Q" /> comes from assuming an infinite sum is **always bigger than the sum of its first few terms**. That's only true when every term is positive.
- <InlineMath math="P=Q" /> would mean the rest of the series, <InlineMath math="u_4+u_5+\cdots" />, is zero, but that rest equals <InlineMath math="\tfrac{u_4}{1-r}\ne0" />.
- The not-enough-information option comes from thinking both candidates for <InlineMath math="r" /> are possible, but <InlineMath math="r=\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}" /> makes the series diverge.

So the quantity <InlineMath math="Q" /> is greater than the quantity <InlineMath math="P" />.
