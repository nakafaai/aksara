Two ideas work together here. For a fixed sum, **the product grows as the numbers bunch closer together**. But the numbers must be distinct and there can be at most four, so the tightest shape may not be allowed.

# How many parts

With two parts, the best is <InlineMath math="11\cdot13=143" />. With three, the consecutive <InlineMath math="7+8+9=24" /> gives <InlineMath math="504" />. Four parts give more factors that are all still above <InlineMath math="1" />, so **the best candidate has four parts**.

# The tightest four parts

Four consecutive numbers <InlineMath math="m,m+1,m+2,m+3" /> add up to <InlineMath math="4m+6" />, which **never equals <InlineMath math="24" />**. So there must be one jump. Among the shapes with exactly one jump of two, only <InlineMath math="m,m+1,m+3,m+4" />, with sum <InlineMath math="4m+8" />, can reach <InlineMath math="24" />, namely with <InlineMath math="m=4" />: <InlineMath math="4+5+7+8" />.

Another way to see it: the four numbers average <InlineMath math="6" />. A pair symmetric about <InlineMath math="6" /> gives <InlineMath math="(6-a)(6+a)=36-a^2" />, so pick the smallest different distances, <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="a=2" />:

<BlockMath math="(5\cdot7)(4\cdot8)=35\cdot32=1{,}120." />

# The tempting wrong options

- <InlineMath math="1{,}680" /> uses the split <InlineMath math="2+4+5+6+7" />, that is **five parts, breaking the limit of four**.
- <InlineMath math="1{,}080" /> starts from <InlineMath math="4+5+6+7=22" /> and adds the leftover <InlineMath math="2" /> to the largest part, which spreads the numbers out at the end.
- <InlineMath math="1{,}008" /> uses <InlineMath math="3+6+7+8" />, with a bigger jump at the start.
- <InlineMath math="504" /> stops at three consecutive parts.
