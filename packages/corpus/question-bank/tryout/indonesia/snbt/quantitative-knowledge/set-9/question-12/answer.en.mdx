# Statement one

Meeting the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis at two points means <InlineMath math="r>3" />, so <InlineMath math="c<16" />. Not meeting the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis means <InlineMath math="r<4" />, so <InlineMath math="c>9" />. **Both bounds are strict**: at <InlineMath math="c=9" /> the circle touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, and at <InlineMath math="c=16" /> it touches the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis at a single point. The integers are <InlineMath math="10,11,\dots,15" />, six of them, not seven. The statement is false.

# Statement two

The origin inside the circle means <InlineMath math="r>5" />. **A radius above <InlineMath math="5" /> is automatically above <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />**, so the circle meets both axes, each at two points. The statement is true.

# Statement three

Touching the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis forces <InlineMath math="r=4" />. The distance from the center to the line <InlineMath math="x-y=0" /> is

<BlockMath math="\frac{|3-(-4)|}{\sqrt{1^2+1^2}}=\frac{7}{\sqrt2}\approx4.95\neq4." />

**One radius can't equal two different distances**, so no such <InlineMath math="c" /> exists. The statement is false.

# Statement four

Put the center into the line's equation: <InlineMath math="3\cdot3+4\cdot(-4)+7=0" />. **The line passes through the center**, so it meets the circle at the two ends of a diameter, whatever the radius, as long as it's positive. The statement is true.

So statements two and four are true, while statements one and three are false.
