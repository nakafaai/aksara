# Statement one

For <InlineMath math="g(x)=2^x-mx" /> with <InlineMath math="m\ge2" />, check three points: <InlineMath math="g(0)=1>0" />, <InlineMath math="g(1)=2-m\le0" />, and for large <InlineMath math="x" /> the power <InlineMath math="2^x" /> far outgrows <InlineMath math="mx" />, so <InlineMath math="g" /> turns positive again. For <InlineMath math="m>2" />, **the sign changes twice**, so there's an intersection between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> and another after <InlineMath math="1" />. For <InlineMath math="m=2" />, the points are exactly <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" />. Concavity rules out a third point. The statement is true.

# Statement two

<InlineMath math="2^0=0+1" /> and <InlineMath math="2^1=1+1" />, so the line <InlineMath math="y=x+1" /> meets the curve at <InlineMath math="(0,1)" /> **and** at <InlineMath math="(1,2)" />, exactly two points. The statement is false.

# Statement three

<InlineMath math="h(x)=2^x+2x-k" /> **always increases**, since <InlineMath math="2^x" /> and <InlineMath math="2x" /> both increase. For very small <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="h<0" />, and for very large <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="h>0" />, so <InlineMath math="h" /> is zero at exactly one point for every <InlineMath math="k" />. No <InlineMath math="k" /> makes the line miss. The statement is false.

# Statement four

Split into three regions. **For <InlineMath math="x\le0" />**, <InlineMath math="2^x>0\ge x" />. For <InlineMath math="0<x<1" />, <InlineMath math="2^x>1>x" />. For <InlineMath math="x\ge1" />, the curve rises faster than the line <InlineMath math="y=x+1" /> after they meet at <InlineMath math="x=1" />, so <InlineMath math="2^x\ge x+1>x" />. The line <InlineMath math="y=x" /> stays below the curve, so the statement is true.

So statements one and four are true, while statements two and three are false.
