To build the new quadratic equation, you need **the sum and the product of its roots**. The product is given, but the sum depends on <InlineMath math="k" />. The plan: write the product of the new roots in terms of <InlineMath math="k" />, find <InlineMath math="k" />, throw out any value that breaks the real-roots condition, then find the sum of the new roots.

# The product of the new roots

By Vieta's formulas for <InlineMath math="x^2-3x+k=0" />, the sum of the roots is <InlineMath math="\alpha+\beta=3" /> and the product is <InlineMath math="\alpha\beta=k" />. Multiply the two new roots term by term. **The two middle terms are always <InlineMath math="1" />**, because <InlineMath math="\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}=1" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\beta=1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\left(\beta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)&=\alpha\beta+1+1+\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\\
&=k+2+\frac{1}{k}
\end{aligned}" />

This product equals <InlineMath math="\frac{16}{3}" />. Multiply both sides by <InlineMath math="3k" /> to clear the fractions.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
k+2+\frac{1}{k}&=\frac{16}{3}\\
3k^2+6k+3&=16k\\
3k^2-10k+3&=0\\
(3k-1)(k-3)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="k=\frac{1}{3}" /> or <InlineMath math="k=3" />.

# The real distinct roots condition

The question says <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> and <InlineMath math="\beta" /> are two distinct real roots. That means **the discriminant must be positive**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
k=3&\;\Longrightarrow\;D=9-4\cdot3=-3<0\\
k=\tfrac{1}{3}&\;\Longrightarrow\;D=9-\tfrac{4}{3}=\tfrac{23}{3}>0
\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="k=3" /> the roots aren't real, so that value goes. Only <InlineMath math="k=\frac{1}{3}" /> is left.

# The sum of the new roots

You can join the two fractions because <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}" />. So **the new sum is the old sum plus the old sum divided by the old product**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)+\left(\beta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)&=(\alpha+\beta)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}\\
&=3+\frac{3}{1/3}\\
&=12
\end{aligned}" />

# Building the equation

The quadratic with root sum <InlineMath math="12" /> and root product <InlineMath math="\frac{16}{3}" /> is <InlineMath math="x^2-12x+\frac{16}{3}=0" />. **Multiply by <InlineMath math="3" />** so every coefficient is a whole number.

<BlockMath math="3x^2-36x+16=0" />

Check it another way. Since <InlineMath math="\alpha\beta=\frac{1}{3}" />, you get <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha\beta}=3\alpha" />, so the first new root is <InlineMath math="\alpha+3\alpha=4\alpha" />. In the same way, the second new root is <InlineMath math="4\beta" />. Their sum is <InlineMath math="4\cdot3=12" /> and their product is <InlineMath math="16\cdot\frac{1}{3}=\frac{16}{3}" />, matching the result above.

# The tempting wrong options

- <InlineMath math="3x^2-12x+16=0" /> uses <InlineMath math="k=3" />, which gives the sum <InlineMath math="3+\frac{3}{3}=4" />. That value **breaks the real-roots condition**.
- <InlineMath math="3x^2-18x+16=0" /> assumes <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}=3" />, so the sum comes out as <InlineMath math="6" />.
- <InlineMath math="3x^2-27x+16=0" /> only adds <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{3}{1/3}=9" /> and forgets to add <InlineMath math="\alpha+\beta=3" />.
- <InlineMath math="3x^2+36x+16=0" /> has the wrong sign. An equation with root sum <InlineMath math="S" /> is written <InlineMath math="x^2-Sx+P=0" />, so the <InlineMath math="x" /> coefficient must be negative.

So the other quadratic equation is <InlineMath math="3x^2-36x+16=0" />.
