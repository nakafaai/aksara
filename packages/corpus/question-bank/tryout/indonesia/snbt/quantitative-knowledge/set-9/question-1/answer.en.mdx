Trying primes one by one never ends. The complete method is **checking the remainder of <InlineMath math="p" /> on division by 3**, because 10 and 14 leave different remainders.

# Small primes

For <InlineMath math="p=2" />, <InlineMath math="p+10=12" /> is not prime. For <InlineMath math="p=3" />, we get <InlineMath math="13" /> and <InlineMath math="17" />, **both prime**. So <InlineMath math="p=3" /> works.

# Primes greater than 3

Every prime <InlineMath math="p>3" /> is not divisible by 3, so its remainder is 1 or 2.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
p=3k+1&:\quad p+14=3k+15=3(k+5)\\
p=3k+2&:\quad p+10=3k+12=3(k+4)
\end{aligned}" />

In both cases one of the numbers is **divisible by 3 and larger than 3**, so it is not prime. No other prime works.

The choice infinitely many comes from assuming that infinitely many primes guarantee this. The value 0 misses <InlineMath math="p=3" />, and 2 or 3 usually wrongly accepts 7 or 13, although <InlineMath math="7+14=21" /> and <InlineMath math="13+14=27" />.

So the number of such primes is **1**.
