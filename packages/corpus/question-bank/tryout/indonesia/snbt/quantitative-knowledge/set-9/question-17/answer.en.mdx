The second sentence of the question is the key: **every student passed at least one exam**. So nobody sits outside both circles, and

<BlockMath math="\text{passed Mathematics}+\text{passed Physics}-\text{passed both}=200." />

Without that sentence, percentage data usually gives only a bound, not an exact value. With it, test whether each statement produces **exactly one** value.

# Statement (1) alone

The students who passed Mathematics number <InlineMath math="85\%\times200=170" />, and those who passed Physics <InlineMath math="170-30=140" />. Since nobody failed both,

<BlockMath math="x=170+140-200=110." />

There's only one value, so **statement (1) alone is sufficient**.

# Statement (2) alone

Let the number who passed only Physics be <InlineMath math="a" />. Those who passed only Mathematics number <InlineMath math="3a" />, and those who passed both

<BlockMath math="x=200-a-3a=200-4a." />

The value <InlineMath math="x" /> must be a perfect square above <InlineMath math="100" /> and at most <InlineMath math="200" />, so the candidates are <InlineMath math="121" />, <InlineMath math="144" />, <InlineMath math="169" />, and <InlineMath math="196" />. The condition is that <InlineMath math="200-x" /> is divisible by <InlineMath math="4" />.

# The tempting wrong options

- Each statement alone being sufficient comes from **stopping at the candidate <InlineMath math="144" />** without checking <InlineMath math="196" />.
- Neither being sufficient comes from treating statement (1) as only a lower bound <InlineMath math="170+140-200" />, though the sentence "every student passed at least one exam" turns that bound into an exact value.
- Needing both statements together comes from the same slip, plus guessing that statement (2) picks one of the values.
- Statement (2) alone being sufficient reverses both tests above.

So statement (1) alone is sufficient to answer the question, but statement (2) alone is not.
