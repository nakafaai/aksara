An old public hall was to be restored after roof cracks and timber decay were found. The first proposal replaced many parts and repainted every surface to make the building look new. The second repaired dangerous structures, retained sound material, and left age marks that did not threaten safety.

The conservation team explained that authentic does not always mean visibly old, nor does it mean returning to one chosen year. Authenticity may lie in materials, techniques, changing uses, and evidence of care by several generations. Old paint layers, for example, may contain information even when not all remain visible.

Before deciding, the team mapped damage, sampled paint, read old photographs, and interviewed local craftspeople. Some timber had to be replaced, but much could be strengthened. The oldest paint appeared in only a few rooms, so repainting the whole building in one old colour would create a uniformity that never existed.

The final plan uses the minimum safe intervention and records every new part. Visitors can distinguish old material, repairs, and the conservator's interpretation without freezing the building in one period.
