A community group recorded mangrove change with phone photographs. Reports of new seedlings rose sharply over three months. An initial map appeared to show the fastest recovery near a road, but managers noted that this was also the easiest area for volunteers to visit.

The team examined two uncertainties. Blurred photographs caused young roots to be mistaken for seedlings, while uneven visits made a lack of reports difficult to distinguish from a lack of observation. They created a photo guide, required a size scale, and marked every site with its number of visits.

Ecologists reviewed a random sample of reports. Agreement between volunteers and experts improved after training but remained lower in high-tide photographs. The public map therefore separates report counts, observation intensity, and validated reports. An area without a report is not automatically labelled as having no seedlings.

Community data remain useful because they cover a wide area and can trigger field checks. Conclusions about recovery are made only after observation opportunity and classification accuracy are considered. The team calls this calibration, not rejection of volunteer contributions.
