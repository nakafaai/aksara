A documentation centre created a digital catalogue of batik motifs from family collections. One cloth was called wave motif by its owner, but artisans in two villages used different names. An old exhibition book listed a fourth name that a curator had invented for visitors.

The first catalogue design allowed one official-name field. That simplified sorting but made community names appear to be spelling errors. The team then separated the owner's name, artisans' terms, exhibition labels, place and date of recording, and the person who supplied each description.

The metadata revision exposed another issue. A photograph of the cloth had circulated without its maker's name, while the curator's label was cited more often than the family's account. Search results therefore now show the source of every name and do not merge similar-looking terms before their relationship is established.

The catalogue still needs a primary display title. Yet that title acts as an entry point, not a ruling that other names are wrong. The team calls this layered attribution: users can find the object through several terms while seeing who used each term and under what circumstances.
