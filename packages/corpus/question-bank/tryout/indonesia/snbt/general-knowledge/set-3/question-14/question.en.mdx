A community radio station prepared an evacuation message for three villages that use different language varieties. The first version translated every official term literally. Its grammar was correct, yet in a listening test several residents misunderstood whether they should go to the village hall or wait for responders.

The team then worked with local announcers and emergency volunteers. They looked for familiar equivalents for directions, deadlines, and levels of danger. One technical term was retained but immediately followed by an action explanation. The information order also changed: the required action came before the reason so listeners would not miss the main instruction if the signal failed.

In the second test, participants who had never heard the message chose the route and departure time more accurately. The team did not conclude that one version would suit every village. Vocabulary, radio-listening habits, and knowledge of safe locations could all affect comprehension.

For the team, translation accuracy was not just word matching. A message was accurate when its meaning, danger level, and required action remained intact. Each version would be tested again with residents before use.
