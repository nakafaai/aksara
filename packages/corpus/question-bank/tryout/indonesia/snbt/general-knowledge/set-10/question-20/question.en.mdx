A digital archive launched automated search across thousands of scanned letters. Researchers soon found typed letters easier to retrieve than handwritten notes, while names with historical spellings often failed to appear. An empty result was initially taken to mean that no document existed.

The team traced the pipeline. Optical character recognition produced text from images, after which a model suggested names, places, and topics. Errors in the first stage could pass into second-stage metadata. User corrections helped, but they were submitted more often for popular collections, so search quality did not improve evenly.

The interface then displayed confidence levels, original images, and an option to search spelling variants. Documents with unreadable text still appeared through collection numbers and date ranges. The team also sampled zero-result searches for human review because failure to find a document does not prove its absence.

Automation remained in use because it widened access, but its output was treated as a revisable index rather than a final description. The team audits performance differences across writing types and periods. The aim is not to remove every error before use, but to make system limits visible and provide a traceable correction route.
