A city market planned to add origin labels to processed foods. In an initial trial, a crisp packet said made in Rawa Village, although its cassava was grown in two districts, sliced in that village, fried in the city, and packed by another distributor. Every statement was true, but the word origin led buyers to imagine one location.

Vendors requested short labels because package space was limited. Consumer groups wanted information that could be checked when quality problems arose. The team therefore distinguished ingredient origin, processing location, and the party responsible for each stage. A package code linked to a fuller record, while the front displayed only the two details most relevant to the product.

The system was tested through a simulated recall. When one batch of cooking oil was marked as faulty, layered records narrowed the products that needed withdrawal. A single-location label instead mixed products made from different ingredients and production periods. The team also found that a code was useless when supplier records were not updated.

The market defines traceability as the ability to follow a product's journey, not merely attach a place name. A useful system must be concise for buyers, detailed enough for inspection, and updated throughout the supply chain.


Which statement functions as evidence rather than an evaluation or a plan?
