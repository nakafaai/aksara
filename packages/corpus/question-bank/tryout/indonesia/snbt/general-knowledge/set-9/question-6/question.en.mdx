A bus operator tested messages intended to reduce loud phone conversations. The old poster said, Do not disturb other passengers. For two weeks, some buses replaced it with, Most passengers on this route choose to speak quietly. Complaints fell further in the second group.

The communications team did not immediately conclude that describing majority behaviour caused the change. Buses with the new message were accompanied by volunteers more often, and their drivers had received extra guidance. Complaints also captured only reported incidents, not every conversation.

In a second trial, volunteer schedules were balanced, drivers received the same guidance, and anonymous observers counted conversations using predefined volume criteria. The norm message continued to reduce loud conversations, but only when its majority figure came from recent observations.

The operator therefore rejected inflated figures. A descriptive-norm message can influence people by showing common behaviour, but its credibility depends on an accurate claim. If passengers repeatedly observe the opposite, the message may lose force or even normalise the wrong behaviour.
