A plaque stated that a city bridge had stood since 1912. During revision, the archive team found three dates: foundation work began in 1912, limited use opened in 1914, and a public inauguration occurred in 1916. Photographs also showed that the central span was substantially replaced in 1958.

The dispute arose because each date answered a different question. Engineers emphasised the start of construction, old newspapers marked the first public crossings, and government documents used the inauguration. Printing one unexplained number would suggest that all those events occurred together.

The team distinguished chronology from periodisation. Chronology orders events; periodisation selects boundaries useful for explaining change. For engineering history, 1958 matters because the structure changed. For the history of urban movement, limited opening in 1914 is more relevant.

The new plaque will show a short timeline and the sentence, Begun in 1912, used from 1914, inaugurated in 1916, partly rebuilt in 1958. The team does not see this as reducing certainty. Explaining what each date means prevents precise figures from producing an inaccurate story.


Which additional finding would most weaken the basis for the decision in the passage?
