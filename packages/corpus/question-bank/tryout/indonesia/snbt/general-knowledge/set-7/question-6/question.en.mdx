A village was deciding how to use a former office site. Many residents attended the first meeting, but the minutes showed that nearly every speaker came from the three nearest neighbourhoods. Residents from the hills left before the decision session because the last transport departed early. A Deaf participant attended with a relative, but no sign-language interpreter was available.

The organisers initially called the meeting open because everyone had been invited. After reviewing the record, they distinguished a formal opportunity to attend from a practical ability to participate. An open invitation did not remove barriers of time, travel, language, or access to information.

For the next stage, the organisers offered daytime and evening meetings, transport from two hamlets, plain-language summaries, and a sign-language interpreter. Proposals could be delivered orally, in writing, or through a representative whose mandate was recorded. The result was not assumed to represent the village automatically; the organisers still compared where participants came from and why others were absent.

The final decision will state the chosen option, the main objections, and how contributions changed the design. For the organisers, inclusive participation does not mean accepting every wish. It means giving each group a workable route to be considered.
