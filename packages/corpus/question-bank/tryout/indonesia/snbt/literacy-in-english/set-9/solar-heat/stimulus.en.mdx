Westford Primary School fitted <InlineMath math="40" /> solar panels to its gym roof last winter. Each month the caretaker records two figures: the solar energy reaching the panels, measured by a sensor mounted beside them, and the electricity the panels produce. The share of the arriving energy that comes out as electricity is the panels' efficiency.

When the summer figures came in, the school newsletter called July "the panels' best month yet" and concluded that hot weather does them no harm. The installer who maintains the system disagreed. Solar cells lose voltage as they warm, she explained, so above a panel temperature of roughly <InlineMath math="25" /> °C they turn a smaller share of the arriving sunlight into electricity, and on a sunny roof the panels run far hotter than the air around them. July produced the most electricity only because the most sunlight arrived. She added one caution about her own explanation: dust builds up on the glass in a dry summer, and the panels had not been washed since March.

She proposed mounting the panels on frames that leave a gap underneath, so that air can flow behind them and carry heat away. The head teacher doubted that the frames would pay for themselves. "The panels only lose efficiency in summer," he said, "and summer is when they produce the most anyway." The installer replied that the frames cannot add any sunlight. They can only win back part of what the heat takes.
