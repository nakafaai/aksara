# Given

**Text 1** uses Thomas Allen's <InlineMath math="1970" />s study (greater desk distance sharply lowers contact) as the basis for removing office partitions. **Text 2** opens by responding directly to Allen's finding before presenting the <InlineMath math="2018" /> sociometric badge studies.

# Asked

How does Text 2 treat Thomas Allen's research that Text 1 cites in favour of open-plan offices?

# Solution

> Allen's finding has held up well: distance does reduce how often colleagues talk. What does not follow is that removing walls shortens the distance that matters.

Check whether Text 2 rejects Allen's result or **accepts the finding while blocking Text 1's inference** from it.

## Accepting the finding while blocking the leap

Text 2 explicitly affirms that <Highlight>distance does reduce how often colleagues talk</Highlight>, so it does not dispute Allen's data. Instead, it argues that <Highlight>removing walls</Highlight> fails to shorten the distance that matters for conversation, because employees protect privacy with headphones and screens so face-to-face contact drops by roughly <InlineMath math="70\%" />.

The four distractors misread Text 2's reasoning. Text 2 expressly notes the badge studies did not measure whether work quality worsened. It rejects Text 1's conclusion about open plans rather than agreeing with it. Its main explanation is privacy protection rather than inability to hear over noise, and it links rising email to privacy rather than desk distance.

# Answer

Text 2 **grants that distance cuts contact but argues that removing partitions does not bring people closer in the way that counts**.
