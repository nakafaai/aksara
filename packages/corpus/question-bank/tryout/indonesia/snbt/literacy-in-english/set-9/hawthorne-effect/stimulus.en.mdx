Few findings from social science have travelled as far as the "Hawthorne effect", the idea that people change what they do because they know they are being studied. Textbooks of management, medicine and teaching repeat it with the same origin story. Between 1924 and 1927, at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois, investigators varied the lighting in rooms where employees assembled electrical parts. A committee of the National Research Council, with money from the electrical industry, had set them up to learn whether better lighting raised output. In the version that later circulated, output rose almost whatever the lamps did, even when they were dimmed, and also in a comparison room whose lighting never changed.

The label came decades afterwards. The sociologist Henry Landsberger popularized it in 1958 while reviewing the wider research programme at the plant, and by then the lighting tests had hardened into a parable: the workers had responded to the attention, not to the lamps. That reading leaned on the later studies more than on the lighting tests, whose records nobody analysed formally for more than eighty years. In the best known of the later studies, a small group of women assembled telephone relays in a separate room for several years while their breaks and hours were changed, and their output climbed. In 1974 the psychologist H. McIlvaine Parsons proposed a duller explanation for that climb. The women received regular information about how much they were producing, and their pay depended on what their small group produced. On his account, attention mattered only because it arrived together with feedback and money.

The lighting records eventually turned up, and in 2011 the economists Steven Levitt and John List published a statistical analysis of them. The striking patterns of the textbooks were not there. They also found a dull confound. The lighting was always changed on a Sunday, when the plant was closed, and its effect was judged from the days that followed, yet output tended to be higher on Mondays whether or not anything had been altered. Part of every rise that followed a change was simply what any Monday would have produced. Even so, Levitt and List stopped short of declaring the effect dead, reporting subtler patterns consistent with workers responding to being studied.

None of this shows that observation leaves behaviour untouched. A systematic review published in 2014 by Jim McCambridge and two colleagues gathered <InlineMath math="19" /> studies, all but one from the health sciences, that tested whether taking part in research altered what participants did. Most reported some effect. But the studies differed so widely in method and setting, and were so exposed to bias, that the reviewers could not say how large the effect is or under what conditions it appears. Their complaint was less that the effect is imaginary than that the old label had led nowhere, and they called for new concepts to study what they termed research participation effects.

The lesson for readers of research is narrower than the legend and more useful. When a group under study improves, the improvement may come from attention, from feedback, from pay, from the calendar, or from a change nobody recorded. "Hawthorne" names a worry, not a mechanism, and a report that dismisses an awkward result as "just the Hawthorne effect" has explained nothing until it says which of these it means.
