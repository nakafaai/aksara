# Given

In **Text 2**, face-to-face interaction fell by roughly <InlineMath math="70\%" /> after staff moved to open-plan offices. The writer's likeliest explanation is that employees **protect their privacy** with headphones and screens once partitions disappear.

# Asked

Which hypothetical finding would most weaken Text 2's privacy explanation for the drop in face-to-face talk?

# Solution

> The likeliest explanation is that people protect their privacy by other means once the partitions are gone.

A causal explanation weakens most when an **independent rival cause** already accepted by the writer can produce the exact same drop.

## Introducing desk distance as a rival cause

Both Text 1 and Text 2 accept Allen's law that <Highlight>distance does reduce how often colleagues talk</Highlight>. If the move placed teammates **farther apart than in their cubicles**, even across different floors, physical distance alone could explain why face-to-face conversations fell, leaving far less work for the privacy explanation.

The other choices either strengthen Text 2 or leave its causal mechanism untouched. Heavy headphone users talking least and close neighbours emailing most are exact predictions of the privacy theory. Whether lost talk was non-work chatter addresses value rather than cause, and a consistent drop across both firms makes the drop sturdier without offering another cause.

# Answer

The finding that would most weaken Text 2's explanation is that **after the move, many members of the same team sat farther apart than they had in their cubicles, some of them on different floors**.
