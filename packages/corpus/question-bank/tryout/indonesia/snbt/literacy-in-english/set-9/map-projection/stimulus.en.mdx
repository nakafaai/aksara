In 1569 the Flemish mapmaker Gerardus Mercator published a world map designed for one job: steering a ship. A sailor who holds a single compass bearing traces a path on the globe that spirals towards a pole, a curve known as a rhumb line. Mercator drew the meridians as parallel vertical lines and then spaced the parallels of latitude farther and farther apart towards the poles, by exactly the amount needed to turn every rhumb line into a straight line. A navigator could rule a line between two ports, measure its angle, and hold that bearing for the whole crossing.

The price is size. On the globe the meridians meet at the poles, but on Mercator's map they never meet, so everything far from the equator is stretched from east to west. Mercator stretched it from north to south by the same factor, and that matching stretch is what keeps angles, and the shapes of small areas, true. At latitude <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> both stretches double every length, so areas appear four times too large. Near the poles the stretch grows without limit, which is why the map is usually cut off before reaching them. Greenland therefore looks about as large as Africa, although Africa has roughly fourteen times its area.

The straight lines are not the shortest routes either. On a globe, the shortest path between two distant points runs along a great circle, and except along the equator or a meridian, a great circle appears curved on Mercator's map. Navigators on long voyages learned to plot the great circle on a different kind of chart and then follow it as a series of short straight legs, each held on its own bearing.

In 1973 the German historian Arno Peters presented an equal-area world map as a fairer alternative, arguing that Mercator's map flattered Europe and shrank the tropics. His map kept the areas of countries in proportion, but it squeezed their shapes, stretching Africa and South America into tall, narrow forms. Cartographers objected that the shapes were badly distorted and that equal-area maps were nothing new: the Scottish clergyman James Gall had described almost the same projection in 1855.

No flat map can keep every shape and every area true at once, so the useful question is which property a task needs. Mercator's design has even returned in a new form. Most online street maps use a version of it, because a map that can be zoomed into any city must keep street corners square at every scale. The familiar complaint that Mercator "distorts the world" is true but misses the point. The map was never meant for comparing the sizes of countries, and it is still the wrong map for that job.
