Louis Braille was born in 1809 in a small French village. At the age of three, while playing in his father's leather workshop, he injured one eye with a sharp tool. The wound became infected, the infection spread to his other eye, and by the age of five he was completely blind.

At ten he won a place at a school for blind children in Paris. The school owned books printed with large raised letters that pupils could trace with their fingers. These books were heavy, expensive, and slow to read, because a finger has to feel the whole outline of each letter. There were only a few of them, and pupils could not write with raised letters at all.

In 1821, the school was shown a system for writing in the dark, designed by a former army officer, Charles Barbier. It used raised dots instead of letters and represented sounds rather than spelling. Each symbol, however, needed up to twelve dots, too many to feel with a single touch of the fingertip.

Braille, then twelve, saw both the promise and the problem. Over the next few years he reduced the system to a cell of just six dots, two columns of three, small enough to be felt under one fingertip. Six dots can form 63 different patterns with at least one raised dot, enough for every letter, number, and punctuation mark, and later for musical notes. Braille published his system in 1829.

Recognition came slowly. Some teachers preferred raised letters, which sighted people could also read, and the school did not officially adopt Braille's system until 1854, two years after his death. Today the six-dot cell is used, with local adaptations, for a great many of the world's languages.
