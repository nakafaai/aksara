# Given

Mercator's <InlineMath math="1569" /> projection turns every **rhumb line** (constant compass bearing) into a straight line and keeps **angles and small local shapes true**, at the cost of enlarging areas away from the equator and curving great-circle shortest paths.

# Asked

For which tasks is Mercator's projection well suited according to the passage?

# Solution

> Most online street maps use a version of it, because a map that can be zoomed into any city must keep street corners square at every scale.

Match each task directly against **what Mercator preserves** (angles and constant bearings) versus **what it distorts** (areas, shortest great-circle routes, and large polar outlines).

## Evaluating all five tasks

| Task | Alignment with Mercator properties | Well suited |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparing land areas across distant latitudes | Areas swell fourfold at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> (<Highlight>wrong map for that job</Highlight>) | **No** |
| Plotting a course on one compass heading | Straight lines match constant bearings (<Highlight>hold that bearing</Highlight>) | **Yes** |
| Finding the shortest route with a ruler | Shortest great circles appear curved on Mercator | **No** |
| Showing true outline of a whole polar continent | Only <Highlight>shapes of small areas</Highlight> stay true, while poles stretch endlessly | **No** |
| Zooming into any city with square street corners | Online street maps use it to <Highlight>keep street corners square</Highlight> | **Yes** |

Watch the distinction between **small areas** and **whole continents**: local angles stay accurate everywhere, so city street corners remain square, yet a large landmass near a pole stretches unevenly across latitudes.

# Answer

Mercator's projection is well suited for:

- Plotting a course that a ship can follow on one compass heading.
- Zooming into any city without bending the angles of its street corners.
