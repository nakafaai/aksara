# Given

In paragraph <InlineMath math="2" />, Parsons explains the **multi-year climb in the relay room** through feedback and small-group pay. In paragraph <InlineMath math="3" />, Levitt and List analyse the **lighting records** and find that Sunday lighting changes coincided with higher Monday output.

# Asked

How does Levitt and List's Sunday-Monday finding relate to Parsons's explanation in paragraph <InlineMath math="2" />?

# Solution

> The lighting was always changed on a Sunday, when the plant was closed, and its effect was judged from the days that followed, yet output tended to be higher on Mondays whether or not anything had been altered.

Check **which experiment each scholar analysed**: the two non-attention explanations target two completely different studies at the Hawthorne plant.

## Matching each explanation to its own study

| Scholar | Study examined | Alternative factor identified |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H. McIlvaine Parsons (<InlineMath math="1974" />) | Relay assembly room (several years) | Regular output feedback and small-group pay |
| Levitt and List (<InlineMath math="2011" />) | Lighting tests (<InlineMath math="1924" /> to <InlineMath math="1927" />) | Sunday changes followed by naturally higher Monday output |

Because the Sunday-Monday pattern explains artifacts in <Highlight>the lighting records</Highlight> rather than the multi-year climb in the relay room, it addresses the **lighting legend** and neither confirms nor undercuts Parsons's account of the relay room.

The other choices mix the two studies or causes. A calendar day effect is not output feedback, a weekly cycle cannot explain a multi-year climb in another study, nobody tested Monday effects in the relay room, and subtle signs in the lighting data do not overturn Parsons's relay-room findings.

# Answer

Levitt and List's finding **gives a different dull explanation for a different set of tests, so it bears on the lighting legend, not on his account of the relay room**.
