Modern zoos no longer describe themselves mainly as places of entertainment. Ask a zoo director why the institution exists, and you will probably hear about conservation: zoos, the argument goes, protect endangered species and return them to the wild. Some genuinely have. The Arabian oryx, a desert antelope that had disappeared from the wild, was bred in captivity and reintroduced to its homeland, as was the Przewalski's horse of Central Asia.

These successes are real, but they are not typical. Most animals in most zoos belong to species that are not endangered, and very few zoo-born animals are ever released. A family that watches meerkats or penguins on a Sunday afternoon is not, for the most part, watching a conservation programme.

There is a further problem with certain species. Animals that range over huge areas in the wild, such as elephants and polar bears, often show signs of stress in captivity: pacing the same route again and again, swaying, or chewing the bars of their enclosure. Even large, well-designed enclosures cannot come close to the distances these animals would cover naturally.

None of this means zoos should close. They can do valuable work, but only if they choose it deliberately. A zoo that wants conservation to be its purpose should concentrate its space and money on species it can actually help, run breeding programmes connected to protected habitats, and stop keeping animals whose needs it cannot meet. Visitors would see fewer famous animals. They would, however, be visiting an institution whose claims matched its practice.
