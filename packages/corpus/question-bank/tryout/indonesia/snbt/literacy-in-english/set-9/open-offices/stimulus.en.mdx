**Text 1**

Walls are expensive, and not only because of what they cost to build. Every wall between two colleagues stands for conversations that will never happen. In the 1970s Thomas Allen of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology tracked how often engineers spoke to one another and found that the chance of regular contact fell steeply as the distance between their desks grew. People talk most to the colleagues they pass and see.

That is the case for the open-plan office. When a team sits together without partitions, a question that would have waited for next week's meeting is answered across a desk in a minute, and a colleague who overhears it may add the piece of knowledge nobody thought to ask for. Exchanges like these cannot be scheduled and do not survive being turned into email, which is exactly why they are worth designing for.

Open plans are also cheaper per desk, and critics like to claim that this is the real reason companies choose them. The savings are a welcome bonus, but they are not why the firms I have advised took down their walls. Those firms did it because their teams were not talking.

Noise is the honest objection, and it deserves an answer rather than a shrug. An open floor needs quiet rooms that anyone can book, clear rules about phone calls, and some distance between teams whose work does not overlap. With those in place, the gains in collaboration easily outweigh the occasional distraction. A team that can see itself is a team that talks.

**Text 2**

Allen's finding has held up well: distance does reduce how often colleagues talk. What does not follow is that removing walls shortens the distance that matters. In 2018 Ethan Bernstein and Stephen Turban published two field studies of companies whose staff moved into open-plan offices. Employees wore sociometric badges, sensors that recorded when they were talking face to face, though not what they said. After the move, face-to-face interaction fell by roughly <InlineMath math="70\%" />, while email and instant messaging rose.

The likeliest explanation is that people protect their privacy by other means once the partitions are gone. They put on headphones against the noise, try to look busy, and move conversations to a screen, where nobody nearby can listen. Take the partitions away, and the walls go up again inside people's habits.

Several limits apply. The badges counted conversations, not their worth, and some of the lost talk may have been chatter that nobody will miss. The studies covered two companies, and they did not measure whether the work itself got better or worse. Still, a design sold on the promise of more face-to-face collaboration should at least produce more face-to-face contact, and here it produced less.

I also suspect that the savings explain more than advocates admit. An open floor fits more desks into the same rent, an argument that is easy to win in a budget meeting. None of this means that every team should return to cubicles. Teams whose work depends on overhearing one another, such as a newsroom, may thrive in the open. For most office workers, though, the quick question at a colleague's desk is valuable because it is private enough to ask, and noise and constant visibility make it harder to ask.
