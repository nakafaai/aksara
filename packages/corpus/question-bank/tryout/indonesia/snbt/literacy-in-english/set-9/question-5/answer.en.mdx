This is a new case, so you apply the author's rule from the last paragraph to it.

Paragraph 5 says "Hawthorne" **names a worry, not a mechanism**. It lists what can lie behind an improvement in a group under study: "attention", "feedback", "pay", "the calendar", or "a change nobody recorded". Then it says a report that waves a result away as "just the Hawthorne effect" "has explained nothing until it says which of these it means".

The school's report does exactly that. Calling the rise "just the Hawthorne effect" only says that being inspected might matter. It doesn't say whether the inspectors' attention, new feedback, some reward, the timing of the term, or an unrecorded change raised the scores. So the author would say the report **hasn't explained the rise yet**.

- The "set the rise aside" option stretches the Monday finding from one set of factory tests to every rise anywhere.
- The "not real learning" option treats the label as an explanation, the very move paragraph 5 rejects.
- The "credit the inspectors' attention" option repeats the old parable. The author lists attention as only one possible cause.
- The "could not be measured" option misreads the review. The reviewers couldn't say how large the effect is or when it appears, and most studies still found some effect.

The author would answer that the school has explained nothing yet and must show **which feature of the inspected term** changed the scores.
