In April 1815, Mount Tambora on the island of Sumbawa in what is now Indonesia exploded in the largest volcanic eruption in recorded history. The blast was heard more than a thousand kilometres away, and tens of thousands of people on Sumbawa and nearby islands died, many of them later, from the famine and disease that followed.

The effects did not stay in the region. Besides ash, the eruption threw huge amounts of sulphur dioxide high into the stratosphere, well above the clouds and weather. There the gas formed tiny droplets that spread around the globe and reflected part of the sunlight back into space. Because no rain falls that high, the droplets stayed for months.

The following year, 1816, became known in Europe and North America as "the year without a summer". Snow fell in parts of New England in June, frosts returned in July and August, and crops failed across large areas. In parts of Europe, cold rain ruined harvests, food prices soared, and thousands of families left their homes in search of food.

The same gloomy summer left a mark on literature. Kept indoors by constant rain beside Lake Geneva, a group of young writers held a contest to write ghost stories. One of them, Mary Shelley, began the novel that became *Frankenstein*.

People at the time did not connect the strange weather with an eruption on the other side of the world. News travelled slowly, and the idea that a volcano could cool the whole planet was not yet established. Scientists made the link only in the twentieth century, and ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica, which hold a layer of volcanic sulphate from that time, later supported it.
