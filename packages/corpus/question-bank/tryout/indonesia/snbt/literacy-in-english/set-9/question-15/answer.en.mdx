The head teacher treats summer as the season when losses matter least. Check that idea against the numbers.

Statement 1. Above <InlineMath math="25" /> °C are May, June, July and August, with <InlineMath math="3{,}200" />, <InlineMath math="3{,}600" />, <InlineMath math="3{,}800" /> and <InlineMath math="3{,}000" /> kWh of sunlight. March and April had only <InlineMath math="2{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="2{,}600" />. The hot months are the four sunniest. Yes.

Statement 3 comes next, because statement 2 depends on it. One percentage point of efficiency is <InlineMath math="1\%" /> of the sunlight that arrives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{July: } 1\% \times 3800 &= 38 \text{ kWh} \\
\text{March: } 1\% \times 2000 &= 20 \text{ kWh}
\end{aligned}" />

A point is worth almost twice as much in July. Yes.

Statement 2 turns that around. Because more sunlight arrives in summer, **the same fall in efficiency costs more electricity** in summer, not less. No.

Statement 4 goes too far. The installer says the frames win back only "part of what the heat takes", and dust may also be lowering July's efficiency. No.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| The hot months were the four sunniest | Yes |

So the head teacher has it backwards: losses in summer matter most because summer is when the most sunlight arrives.
