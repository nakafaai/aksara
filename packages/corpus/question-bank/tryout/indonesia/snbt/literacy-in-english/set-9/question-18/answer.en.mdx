Paragraph 4 describes the Peters map and the objections to it, and the last paragraph adds a general rule. Keep **Peters's own claims** apart from **the cartographers' objections**.

Statement 1. His map "kept the areas of countries in proportion, but it squeezed their shapes". Correct areas, distorted shapes. Yes.

Statement 2. The cartographers answered that "equal-area maps were nothing new", because James Gall had described almost the same projection in 1855, more than a century earlier. No.

Statement 3. The political argument, that Mercator "flattered Europe", was Peters's own. The cartographers objected to distorted shapes and to the claim of novelty. No.

Statement 4. The last paragraph says "no flat map can keep every shape and every area true at once". So a map that keeps every area true has to give up some shapes. Yes.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Peters traded shapes for areas | Yes |
| Peters drew the first equal-area map | No |
| Cartographers objected over politics | No |
| An equal-area map must distort shapes somewhere | Yes |
