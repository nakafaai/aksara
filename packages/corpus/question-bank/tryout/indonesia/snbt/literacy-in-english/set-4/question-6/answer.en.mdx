# Given

In **Text A**, moving high schools from 7:50 to 8:45 (a <InlineMath math="55" />-minute delay) raised sleep by a median of <InlineMath math="34" /> minutes. In **Text B**, the local district starts at 8:00 and considers moving to 8:30 (a **<InlineMath math="30" />-minute delay**).

# Asked

Which pair of facts from Text B gives the strongest reason to expect a smaller sleep gain than <InlineMath math="34" /> minutes?

# Solution

Distinguish **schedule-size facts** (how much later students can actually sleep) from **cost and logistics facts** (bus rounds and parent schedules).

> Because the district already starts at 8:00 and would shift by only thirty minutes rather than nearly an hour, students have roughly half as much extra morning time to convert into sleep.

## Evidence from Text B

Text B states directly: <Highlight>Our high schools already start at 8:00, so moving to 8:30 would delay them by half an hour, not by almost an hour</Highlight>.

Pairing the **8:00 baseline** with the **half-hour shift to 8:30** explains directly why the district should not promise another city's <InlineMath math="34" />-minute sleep gain.

## Why the other options fail

- **Three bus rounds and <InlineMath math="2.4" /> million dollars** measure financial cost, not the size of the sleep benefit.
- **Working parents unable to manage a 9:30 primary start** is a family scheduling cost, not a reason teenagers would sleep less.
- **Accepting the biology and wanting a later start** support expecting some benefit, not a smaller one.
- **Wrist monitors and lower lateness** come from Text A's city study, not Text B.

# Answer

**The district's high schools already start at 8:00, and the planned start of 8:30 is only half an hour later**.
