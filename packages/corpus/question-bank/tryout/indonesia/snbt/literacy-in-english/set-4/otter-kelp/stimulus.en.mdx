Ecologists use the term "trophic cascade" for an effect that passes down more than one link of a food chain. The textbook example comes from the rocky coasts of the North Pacific. Sea otters eat sea urchins, and urchins graze on kelp, the large brown algae that form underwater forests. Take the otters away, the textbook says, and the urchins multiply until the forest is reduced to a bare "urchin barren". Bring the otters back, and the forest returns. Each half of that account rests on a different kind of evidence, and the two kinds are not equally strong.

The earliest evidence was a comparison, not an experiment. In the early 1970s, James Estes and John Palmisano surveyed reefs around two islands in the western Aleutians. Around Amchitka, where otters had recovered after the fur trade ended, kelp grew thickly and urchins were small and scarce. Around Shemya, which otters had not yet recolonised, urchins covered the rock and kelp was sparse. Two islands can differ in many ways besides their otters, from wave exposure to water temperature, so the contrast was suggestive rather than decisive.

Firmer evidence arrived by accident. During the 1990s, otter numbers fell steeply across much of the western Aleutians, and on reefs that had been surveyed before the decline, urchin biomass rose and kelp thinned within a few years. This time the comparison was not between two places but between two moments in the same place. What removed the otters is a separate question. Estes and his colleagues argued in 1998 that killer whales had begun hunting them, a case built largely on energy calculations rather than on counts of otters eaten, and not every researcher accepts it. The cascade below the otters, however, does not depend on who removed them.

The return journey is a different matter. In Monterey Bay, California, a marine heatwave in 2014 and a disease that devastated sunflower sea stars, another urchin predator, allowed urchins to multiply, and barrens spread beside surviving patches of forest. Otters were present throughout. A study led by Joshua Smith, published in 2021, found that the otters ate about <InlineMath math="3" /> times as many urchins as before, yet they foraged almost entirely in the remaining kelp. Urchins in the forest patches were well fed and full of energy-rich gonads, while those in the barrens were starving and offered little food. By feeding where meals were best, the otters appear to have helped keep the patches from collapsing, but they did little to clear the barrens.

A cascade, in other words, can run more easily in one direction than in the other. Losing a predator lets grazers multiply quickly, whereas a barren, once formed, is full of prey that a predator has little reason to hunt. Some ecologists therefore describe kelp forests and barrens as alternative states, each able to persist once it is established. If they are right, returning otters to a coast that has already turned to barren may achieve little unless it is combined with other measures, such as removing urchins by hand.
