Three years ago, a mid-sized city built protected bike lanes, separated from traffic by low concrete barriers, on three busy streets. The city's transport department compared the year before construction with the first full year after the lanes opened.

At a public meeting, one council member argued that the lanes had made Main Street more dangerous, since cyclist injuries there had risen. A transport analyst replied that the council member was looking at the wrong figure. What matters for an individual rider, she said, is the risk per cyclist, and by that measure every street had become safer.

The department's report added two cautions. Over the same period, the number of people cycling across the whole city rose by about 25 percent, including on streets where no lanes were built. The comparison also covers only one year before and one year after construction, so a few unusual crashes could shift the figures noticeably.
