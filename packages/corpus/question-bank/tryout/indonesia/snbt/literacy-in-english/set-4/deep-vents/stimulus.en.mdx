In 1977, scientists aboard the research submarine *Alvin* descended about 2,500 metres to the floor of the Pacific Ocean near the Galápagos Islands. They expected to find little more than scattered life in the cold and darkness. Instead, around cracks in the sea floor where hot, mineral-rich water gushed out, they found crowded communities of clams, mussels, crabs, and red-tipped tube worms taller than a person.

The discovery raised an obvious question: what were these animals eating? Almost every food chain on the surface of the Earth begins with photosynthesis, in which plants and algae use sunlight to build sugars. At the depth of the vents, however, there is no sunlight at all. The answer turned out to lie in the water itself. The fluid pouring from the vents carries dissolved hydrogen sulphide, a chemical poisonous to most animals. Certain bacteria can extract energy from it and use that energy to make organic matter, a process called chemosynthesis. These bacteria, rather than plants, form the base of the vent food web.

The giant tube worms show how close this partnership can be. An adult worm has no mouth and no gut. Instead, a large organ inside its body is packed with chemosynthetic bacteria. The worm's feathery red plume, coloured by a special form of haemoglobin, absorbs hydrogen sulphide, oxygen, and carbon dioxide from the surrounding water and delivers them to the bacteria, which in turn supply the worm with food.

It is tempting to describe vent communities as entirely independent of the sun, but that is not quite accurate. Most vent bacteria need oxygen to extract energy from hydrogen sulphide, and the oxygen dissolved in seawater was made by photosynthesis in sunlit waters and on land. Sunlight is absent from the vents, yet its influence still reaches them.

Vents are also unstable homes. A single vent may stop flowing after a few years or decades, leaving its community to starve. Many vent animals release tiny larvae that drift in deep currents, and only a few of these larvae reach a newly opened vent. Scientists studying vents today are interested not only in how these animals live but in what vents might mean elsewhere. Moons such as Jupiter's Europa may hide oceans beneath their ice, and if similar chemical energy exists there, it could in principle support life without light.
