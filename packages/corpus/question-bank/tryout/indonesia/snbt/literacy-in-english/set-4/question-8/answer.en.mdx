# Given

The study city moved high schools from 7:50 to 8:45 (**<InlineMath math="55" /> minutes** later) and gained **<InlineMath math="34" /> minutes** of sleep. This district would move from 8:00 to 8:30 (**<InlineMath math="30" /> minutes** later).

# Asked

Assuming sleep gain scales proportionally with the start-time delay, about how many extra minutes of sleep would this district's students gain per night?

# Solution

Compute **both schedule shifts** in minutes first, then multiply <InlineMath math="34" /> minutes by the ratio of **<InlineMath math="30" /> to <InlineMath math="55" />**.

> Scaling thirty-four minutes of sleep down from a fifty-five-minute delay to a thirty-minute delay yields eighteen point five minutes, or about nineteen minutes a night.

## Proportional scaling of sleep gain

| Location | Start shift | Delay length | Sleep gained |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Study city (Text A) | 7:50 to 8:45 | <InlineMath math="55" /> minutes | <InlineMath math="34" /> minutes |
| Local district (Text B) | 8:00 to 8:30 | <InlineMath math="30" /> minutes | <InlineMath math="\approx 19" /> minutes |

Applying **direct proportion** to the two delays:

<BlockMath math="\text{Estimated gain} = 34 \times \frac{30}{55} = \frac{1{,}020}{55} \approx 18.5 \approx 19 \text{ minutes}" />

## Why the other options fail

- **<InlineMath math="34" />** copies the other city's **full gain** without adjusting for the shorter delay.
- **<InlineMath math="30" />** assumes every single delayed minute becomes sleep, even though <InlineMath math="55" /> minutes yielded only <InlineMath math="34" /> minutes.
- **<InlineMath math="62" />** inverts the ratio (<InlineMath math="34 \times \frac{55}{30}" />) instead of scaling down.
- **<InlineMath math="4" />** subtracts <InlineMath math="34 - 30" /> instead of computing a proportion.

# Answer

**About <InlineMath math="19" />**
