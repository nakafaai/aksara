The passage defines the effect as a subject "responding to unintended cues from the people or the setting around it". So you need **two things at once**: someone near the child who knows what result to expect, and a live situation in which that person's small movements could steer the child or the score.

The examiner who knows the groups, sits with each child and judges each answer on the spot has both. Like Hans's questioners, they could lean in, nod or relax without meaning to, and they also decide the score.

- The children know their own group, but no one around them gives cues, and a computer marks the test. Expecting to do well is a different effect.
- The researcher knows the groups and is present, but hidden behind a one-way mirror, like a knower behind blinkers, and a computer asks and records. No cue can reach the child.
- The stranger is face to face with each child but knows nothing about the groups, so there's no expectation to leak. This is Pfungst's remedy.
- The teacher knows the groups but leaves the room, like the knower hidden behind blinkers.

The study most open to the effect is the examiner who knows which children had the new method, gives each a spoken test and decides on the spot whether each answer is good enough.
