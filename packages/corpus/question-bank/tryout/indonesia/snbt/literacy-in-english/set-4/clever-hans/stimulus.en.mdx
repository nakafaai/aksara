In the early 1900s a retired Berlin schoolteacher, Wilhelm von Osten, showed crowds a horse that seemed able to do arithmetic. Asked to add five and three, Hans tapped his hoof eight times and stopped. He answered questions about dates, fractions and the value of coins, and he was right far more often than chance would allow. A commission of experts, among them a circus trainer and a zoo director, watched him in 1904 and found no trickery. Von Osten gave no signal they could detect, and Hans answered even when strangers asked the questions.

The puzzle was solved by a young psychologist, Oskar Pfungst, who changed the conditions one at a time. When the questioner knew the answer and stood where Hans could see him, Hans was nearly always right. When the questioner did not know the answer, or when Hans wore blinkers that hid the questioner from view, his success fell to the level of guessing. Hans was not counting. He was watching. As the count neared the right number, questioners leaned forward slightly as they waited, then relaxed or lifted their heads when he reached it, and Hans stopped tapping at that cue. None of the questioners knew they were doing it, and Pfungst found that he made the same movements himself even when he tried not to.

The commission had not been careless. It had ruled out deliberate signals, which was the question it set out to answer. What it had not tested was whether anyone in the room needed to know the answer at all.

Psychologists now speak of the "Clever Hans effect" whenever an animal, a child or a machine appears to show an ability but is in fact responding to unintended cues from the people or the setting around it. The remedy Pfungst used, keeping the answer from anyone the subject can see, is one reason many experiments today are run "double-blind". Hans, it should be said, was remarkable in his own way. Reading the tiny movements of a human head is not a skill most animals have.
