In 1901, sponge divers working off the small Greek island of Antikythera brought up statues, pottery, and coins from the wreck of a ship that had sunk around two thousand years earlier. Among the finds was a shoebox-sized lump of corroded bronze and wood that attracted little notice at first. Months later, after the lump had cracked apart, a museum worker noticed something no one expected from the ancient world: a toothed gear wheel.

For decades, scholars argued about what the object had been. The fragments were fused together by corrosion, and many of the gears were hidden inside. A breakthrough came in the second half of the twentieth century, when X-ray images allowed researchers to count gear teeth they could not see directly. In 2005, a team using high-resolution scanning revealed even more: the remains of at least thirty gears and thousands of tiny letters engraved on the surfaces, many of which had been unreadable for two millennia.

The letters turned out to be something like a user's manual. Combined with the gear counts, they showed that the device was an astronomical calculator. By turning a handle, a user could display the positions of the Sun and Moon through the signs of the zodiac, show the phase of the Moon, and predict when eclipses were likely to occur. One dial even tracked a four-year cycle of athletic games, including the Olympics.

The most impressive detail is a pair of gears linked by a pin that slides in a slot. Because one wheel turns slightly off-centre relative to the other, the pin makes the output move faster at some points and slower at others. This reproduces a real feature of the Moon's motion, which speeds up and slows down as it travels around the Earth. In other words, the makers did not simply mark the Moon's average position. They built an astronomical theory into bronze.

No other object of comparable mechanical complexity is known from the ancient world. Geared devices of similar complexity are not known again until the astronomical clocks of the Middle Ages, more than a thousand years later. Some historians think the Antikythera mechanism may not have been unique in its own time, since bronze was valuable and was often melted down for reuse. If that is right, the lucky survival of one shipwreck may be the only reason we know such machines existed at all.
