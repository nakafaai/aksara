In 2024, Venice began charging day visitors a small fee to enter its historic centre on its busiest days. Other crowded destinations are watching closely, and some are preparing similar schemes. The appeal is easy to see: a fee seems to reduce crowds and raise money at the same time. Yet a fee on its own is unlikely to do either job well.

Consider the size of such charges. A few euros is a small addition to the cost of a day trip that already includes train tickets, meals, and souvenirs. People who have planned a holiday around seeing a famous square are rarely turned back by a charge smaller than the price of a coffee. If the aim is to keep numbers below what a city can bear, a fee low enough to be politically acceptable may barely change behaviour, while a fee high enough to deter visitors would turn the city into a place only the wealthy can see.

What actually limits crowds is limiting numbers. Some popular sites already require visitors to book a time slot, and once the slots are full, no more tickets are sold. This approach protects the experience of those who come and the daily lives of residents far more reliably than a price that most visitors will simply pay. It also spreads visits across the day instead of letting everyone arrive at once.

A fee can still play a useful role if it is attached to such a system. The money could pay for street cleaning, extra public transport, or housing support for residents who are being priced out by holiday rentals. For that to happen, however, the revenue must be reported openly and spent where residents can see the results. Otherwise a fee risks becoming a symbol that a city has "done something" about overtourism while the crowds remain exactly as they were.

Cities that depend on tourism cannot simply close their gates, and they should not try. But they should be honest about what each tool can achieve. A fee raises money; a cap controls numbers. Treating the first as if it were the second only postpones the harder decision.
