# Given

Medical photo-screening software claims to spot serious illness, yet researchers discover most seriously ill training photos came from **the hospital's portable bedside camera**.

# Asked

Which diagnosis and remedy best fit the passage's explanation of the Clever Hans effect?

# Solution

Match **both halves** of the option to paragraph <InlineMath math="4" />: diagnose the **unintended setting cue** (camera type) and apply **Pfungst's blinding remedy** (test where the cue cannot reveal the answer).

> Just as Hans read head movements instead of doing math, software trained on bedside-camera photos may read camera clues instead of illness and must be tested where camera type gives no hint.

## Matching diagnosis and remedy to the text

Paragraph <InlineMath math="4" /> explicitly includes machines: <Highlight>whenever an animal, a child or a machine appears to show an ability but is in fact responding to unintended cues from the people or the setting around it</Highlight>. It then states Pfungst's remedy: <Highlight>keeping the answer from anyone the subject can see</Highlight>.

For this software, the camera is an unintended cue from the setting, so the proper test uses **photos in which the camera gives no clue** (for instance, photographing both ill and healthy patients with the same camera).

## Why the other options fail

- **Stop using the bedside camera** never tests whether the software can actually detect illness without hidden cues.
- **Use it as it is** confuses Hans's clever cue-reading with genuine medical diagnosis.
- **Researchers signalled on purpose** ignores that Clever Hans cues are unintended.
- **Retrain on more bedside-camera photos** reinforces the spurious shortcut instead of removing it.

# Answer

**The software may be responding to the type of camera rather than to illness, so it should be tested on photos in which the camera gives no clue to the answer**.
