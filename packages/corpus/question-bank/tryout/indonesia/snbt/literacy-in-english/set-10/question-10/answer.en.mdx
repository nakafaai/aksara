# Given

At the end of **Text 1**, the mother asserts that her children's success **"is not a fluke"**. **Text 2** accepts that many homeschooled children do very well, yet explains why success stories dominate public discussion.

# Asked

How would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim that the family's experience "is not a fluke"?

# Solution

> But stories from families for whom it works are exactly the evidence we should expect to hear most, since families for whom it went badly are far less likely to write about it.

Pair Text 2's **acceptance of individual success** with its warning about **reporting bias** in personal anecdotes.

## Accepting the story while limiting generalisation

Text 2 never doubts individual triumphs (<Highlight>which I do not doubt</Highlight>), so it would grant that this family's success may be genuine. Yet because families for whom homeschooling failed rarely publish blog posts, hearing a polished success story is **exactly what reporting bias predicts** and tells us little about typical outcomes.

The other choices contradict Text 2. The researcher never predicts the children's gains will fade. Census surveys count households rather than learning results. Rare comparative studies gave <Highlight>mixed</Highlight> findings rather than proving her case typical, and it is unsuccessful families, not successful ones, who rarely write about their experience.

# Answer

The author of Text 2 would most likely respond **by granting that the family's success may be real, but noting that families for whom it works share their stories most, so one story says little in general**.
