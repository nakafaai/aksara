A glacier looks frozen and still, but it is always moving. It begins where more snow falls each winter than melts each summer. Year after year, new snow presses down on the old, squeezing out the air until the lower layers turn into dense ice. Once the ice is thick enough, usually a few tens of metres, its own weight makes it flow downhill.

The ice moves in two ways. Deep inside, the ice crystals slowly deform under pressure, so the whole mass creeps forward like very thick honey. At the base, meltwater can collect between the ice and the rock, and the glacier slides on it. Because sliding depends on this water, many glaciers move faster in summer than in winter.

The speed is not the same everywhere. In the 1840s, researchers on a glacier in the Swiss Alps hammered a straight line of stakes across the ice. A few years later the line was no longer straight: the stakes in the middle had been carried farthest downhill. Near the valley walls, friction with the rock had held the ice back.

Most mountain glaciers move a few tens or hundreds of metres a year. Some outlet glaciers in Greenland, which carry ice from the ice sheet to the sea, have been measured at more than ten kilometres a year.

When a glacier is said to be retreating, the ice is not flowing backwards. It is still moving forward. But at its lower end, called the snout, the ice melts faster than new ice arrives from above, so the snout moves back up the valley.
