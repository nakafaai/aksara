# Given

Text <InlineMath math="1" /> **advocates homeschooling citing high** test scores and her own children, while Text <InlineMath math="2" /> questions selection bias in those studies and notes survey limits.

# Asked

Which views about homeschooling are supported by both Text <InlineMath math="1" /> and Text <InlineMath math="2" />.

# Solution

A statement gets Yes only if **both texts** support it. One text alone isn't enough.

> Two observers can both cheer that a group of runners finished fast while disagreeing completely on whether it was the shoes or their prior training.

## Separating shared facts from rival explanations

**Statement <InlineMath math="1" />**. Text <InlineMath math="2" /> says the opposite: "A survey is a snapshot. It cannot tell us how many families who began homeschooling during the pandemic have since returned to school." Text <InlineMath math="1" /> cites a survey only for the rise in <InlineMath math="2020" />, and her own children "never went back". At least one text rejects the statement, so it is not shared. No.

**Statement <InlineMath math="2" />**. Text <InlineMath math="1" /> cites studies with scores "well above the national average." Text <InlineMath math="2" /> agrees that "many homeschooled children do very well, which I do not doubt." Both support it. Yes.

**Statement <InlineMath math="3" />**. Text <InlineMath math="1" /> implies this when it says good results are "what happens when learning is fitted to the child." Text <InlineMath math="2" /> says the studies "cannot tell us" whether learning at home "is the reason the high scorers score high." Only Text <InlineMath math="1" /> holds it. No.

**Statement <InlineMath math="4" />**. Text <InlineMath math="1" /> describes a daughter "drifting through math" and a son who "could not sit still" who both do better at home. Text <InlineMath math="2" /> says children who were bullied or whose needs a school never met "plainly flourish at home." Yes.

**Statement <InlineMath math="5" />**. Text <InlineMath math="1" /> rejects this, since a class of her own children "hardly needs classroom-management skills." Text <InlineMath math="2" /> mentions parents without "the time nor the training" only as a group we know little about. It doesn't claim they teach worse. No.

# Answer

Both texts agree that many homeschooled children do well and some came after struggling in school, while splitting on what caused those results.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Surveys show how many 2020 starters still homeschool | No |
| Many homeschooled children achieve highly | Yes |
| Studies show home learning causes the results | No |
| Some children who struggled at school do better at home | Yes |
| Untrained parents generally teach less well | No |
