A statement gets Yes only if **both texts** support it. One text alone isn't enough.

**Statement 1**. Text 2 says the opposite: "A survey is a snapshot. It cannot tell us how many families who began homeschooling during the pandemic have since returned to school." Text 1 cites a survey only for the rise in 2020, and her own children "never went back". At least one text rejects the statement, so it is not shared. No.

**Statement 2**. Text 1 cites studies with scores "well above the national average." Text 2 agrees that "many homeschooled children do very well, which I do not doubt." Both support it. Yes.

**Statement 3**. Text 1 implies this when it says good results are "what happens when learning is fitted to the child." Text 2 says the studies "cannot tell us" whether learning at home "is the reason the high scorers score high." Only Text 1 holds it. No.

**Statement 4**. Text 1 describes a daughter "drifting through math" and a son who "could not sit still" who both do better at home. Text 2 says children who were bullied or whose needs a school never met "plainly flourish at home." Yes.

**Statement 5**. Text 1 rejects this, since a class of her own children "hardly needs classroom-management skills." Text 2 mentions parents without "the time nor the training" only as a group we know little about. It doesn't claim they teach worse. No.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Surveys show how many 2020 starters still homeschool | No |
| Many homeschooled children achieve highly | Yes |
| Studies show home learning causes the results | No |
| Some children who struggled at school do better at home | Yes |
| Untrained parents generally teach less well | No |

The shared ground is that **many homeschooled children do well**, some of them after struggling at school. The texts split on what causes it.
