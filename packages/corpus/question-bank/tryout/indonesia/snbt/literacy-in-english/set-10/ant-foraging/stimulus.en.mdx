Watch ants streaming between a nest and a crumb, and it is hard not to credit the colony with a plan. Yet no ant knows the route as a whole. A forager that finds food lays a chemical trail, a pheromone, on her way home, and nestmates leaving the nest tend to follow whichever trail smells strongest. Each ant that follows a trail and finds food adds pheromone of her own, so a busy trail draws still more traffic. What looks like a collective decision is this loop of positive feedback.

The loop also explains why colonies so often end up on the shorter of two routes. In laboratory experiments, Argentine ants were offered a bridge to food with two branches of unequal length. At first, ants spread across both branches at random. But those that happened to take the short branch got back to the nest sooner, so the short branch received its second coat of pheromone, on the return trip, before the long one did. Ants leaving the nest met a slightly stronger scent there, and the small head start compounded. In most trials, the bulk of the traffic soon took the short branch. No ant had to compare the two lengths. The comparison was made, in effect, by the clock.

The same experiments produced a result popular retellings skip. When a colony was first given only the long branch, and the short one was added after a trail had formed, most colonies did not switch. They kept marching the long way round. The usual explanation is that the few ants that wandered onto the new branch laid too little pheromone to compete with the old route's scent, and that this species' trail pheromone fades slowly enough for the old route to keep its lead.

Fading does little to explain that head start, but it matters a great deal once a trail exists. Because pheromone fades, a trail survives only as long as ants keep refreshing it. In computer simulations, and in route-finding programs modelled on ants, raising the rate of evaporation makes a colony more willing to abandon an old route for a newly opened better one. The effect has a ceiling, though. If the scent vanishes too quickly, no trail lasts long enough to recruit anyone. Evaporation is a way of forgetting, and a colony needs to forget at the right speed. The idea that real species have evolved pheromones suited to how long their food lasts is attractive but far from established.

Some writers on "swarm intelligence" describe colonies as solving shortest-path problems, like a calculator that reliably returns the best answer. That description flatters the ants in one way and sells them short in another. A colony finds the shorter route mainly when both routes are open from the start, and it can stay with a poor route long after a better one appears. Yet where good food sources tend to stay put, sticking with a working route is usually worth more than constantly checking for a better one. Nor are ants pure trail followers: many species also steer by landmarks, and in some, experienced foragers will follow a remembered route when it disagrees with the trail. A stuck colony is not a machine that has broken down. It is a system paying for a design that trades flexibility for reliability.
