# Given

In the final paragraph of **Text 1**, the parent cites studies showing homeschooled students scoring well above the national average and argues that **if the results are that good**, the burden of proof falls on those wanting tighter rules. **Text 2** critiques how those study samples are collected.

# Asked

Which hidden assumption behind the parent's burden-of-proof argument does Text 2 most directly challenge?

# Solution

> The studies most often cited to show that homeschooled students outperform their peers rely on families who volunteer to take part and to report their children's scores.

Identify the **unstated bridge** between "cited studies show high scores" and "homeschooling results in general are that good".

## Pinpointing the representativeness assumption

The parent's leap works only if the cited studies represent **homeschooled students in general**. Text 2 strikes directly at that bridge: because <Highlight>families whose children are doing well have more reason to volunteer</Highlight>, volunteer studies <Highlight>cannot tell us how a typical homeschooled child performs</Highlight>.

The other options target different parts of Text 1 or invent claims. Friendships and parental knowledge of children's habits appear in paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> to answer separate objections. Future growth and whether tighter rules would lower scores play no role in her burden-of-proof logic.

# Answer

The assumption Text 2 most directly challenges is **that the scores in the studies she cites describe homeschooled students in general, not mainly the families who report them**.
