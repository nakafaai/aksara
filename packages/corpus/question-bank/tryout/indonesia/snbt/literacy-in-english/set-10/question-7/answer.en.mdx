The argument goes like this. Studies find homeschooled students "scoring well above the national average." She then says "If the results are that good, the burden of proof should fall on those who want to tighten the rules." To move from "these studies show high scores" to "the results are that good", she has to assume **the studies show how homeschooled students do in general**.

Text 2 attacks exactly that step. The studies "rely on families who volunteer," and "Families whose children are doing well have more reason to volunteer." So the samples "cannot tell us how a typical homeschooled child performs."

- Friendships come up in paragraph 3 of Text 1 as an answer to a different criticism. The burden-of-proof argument doesn't rest on it.
- Future growth plays no part in the argument, and Text 2 never predicts it.
- Knowing her children's habits answers the training criticism. It isn't the premise of the argument about test results.

The challenged assumption is that the studies' high scores describe homeschooled students in general rather than mainly the families who chose to report them.
