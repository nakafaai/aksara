# Given

**In the ferry story**, a man in a council jacket praises the new bridge using stock phrases while the father nods routinely and remarks that the man has never seen the river high.

# Asked

Which inferences about the father, the narrator, and the council man follow from that brief scene.

# Solution

The scene is short, but every phrase is chosen. Look at **how** the father responds and **how** the narrator frames the man's words.

> Waving someone along with a polite nod is how you get through small talk, not a sign that you agree with their speech.

## Reading tone and unspoken distance in the scene

Statement <InlineMath math="1" />. He nods "as if they were cars he was waving aboard". Waving cars aboard is a routine gesture, done for everyone, without a second thought. The nods are **automatic courtesy**, not agreement. Yes.

Statement <InlineMath math="2" />. The father never mentions jobs. His only comment is about the river. Reading a view on jobs into it goes beyond the text. No.

Statement <InlineMath math="3" />. "He has never been on this boat when the river was high" says the man has seen only the easy days. He talks about the ferry without knowing its hard work. Yes.

Statement <InlineMath math="4" />. The narrator calls the man's words "what everyone says to people like my father", a flat, slightly weary label. She doesn't endorse them. No.

Statement <InlineMath math="5" />. That same phrase shows the opposite: these are stock lines, said to anyone in the father's position. No.

# Answer

**Only the two inferences about polite nods and the council man's unfamiliarity with hard river work fit the scene**.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| The nods are polite, not agreement | Yes |
| The father thinks the bridge brings no jobs | No |
| The man doesn't know what the work involved | Yes |
| The narrator shares the man's view | No |
| The man's words were chosen for this occasion | No |
