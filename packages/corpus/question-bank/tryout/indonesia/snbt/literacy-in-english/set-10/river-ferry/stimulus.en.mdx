My father had made the crossing eleven times a day for thirty-one years, and on the last day he made it twelve times, because, he said, the timetable had never said anything about a last day.

I had come home for it. The bridge would open at noon the next morning, a long pale curve that had been growing out of both banks for three summers, and the ferry would be sold to a company downriver that wanted it for parts. He had told me this on the phone in the voice he used for the weather.

People came that day who had not ridden the ferry in years. A woman brought a cake. Two boys rode back and forth four times without paying, which my father would never have allowed when I was their age. Between crossings he wrote each trip into the green ledger he kept under the wheel: the time, the number of passengers, the state of the river. I had seen that ledger all my life and had never once seen him read it.

On the ninth crossing a man in a council jacket stood beside the wheelhouse and said what everyone says to people like my father: that it was the end of an era, that the bridge would bring jobs, that nobody could stop progress. My father nodded at each of these as if they were cars he was waving aboard. When the man had gone, he said to me, without looking away from the water, "He has never been on this boat when the river was high."

The last crossing left at eight, after dark. The bridge lights had been switched on for a test, and their reflection lay across our path in long, broken stripes. My father steered straight through them. Halfway over, he cut the engine and let us drift, and for a minute there was no sound but the water slapping the hull. I waited for him to say something. He didn't. Then he started the engine again and brought us in, as gently as I have ever seen a boat come to a dock.

He made his final entry standing up. I read it over his shoulder: the time, seven passengers, "river quiet". Then he did something I had never seen him do. He turned the pages back, slowly, to the first year, and read for a long time while the last passengers walked up the ramp.

In the morning he did not go to the opening. He said he had a bad knee. At noon I heard the band from the kitchen, faint across the water, and found him at the window with the ledger closed on the sill, watching the first cars cross and counting them under his breath.
