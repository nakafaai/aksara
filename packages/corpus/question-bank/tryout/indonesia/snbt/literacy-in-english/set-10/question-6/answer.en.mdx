# Given

**Text 1** supports homeschooling with three kinds of evidence: Census Bureau growth from <InlineMath math="5.4\%" /> to <InlineMath math="11.1\%" />, the mother's own children's progress, and standardized test-score studies. **Text 2** evaluates all three.

# Asked

How does Text 2 relate to the evidence the parent presents in Text 1?

# Solution

> Such samples can tell us that many homeschooled children do very well, which I do not doubt. They cannot tell us how a typical homeschooled child performs, or whether learning at home is the reason the high scorers score high.

Track both what Text 2 **concedes** (real growth and many successful children) and what it **rejects** (drawing general or causal conclusions from volunteer studies and success stories).

## Separating concessions from causal critique

Text 2 grants <Highlight>The surge in homeschooling since <InlineMath math="2020" /> is real</Highlight> and agrees that many homeschooled children flourish. However, because test-score studies rely on **volunteer families** and blogs oversample families for whom homeschooling worked, neither the studies nor one mother's story proves what homeschooling itself causes.

The other options misstate Text 2's stance. It never claims most families went back to school, it critiques the test data rather than accepting her conclusion about results, it calls result claims its "larger problem" beyond counting issues, and it withholds judgment on oversight rather than agreeing on the burden of proof.

# Answer

Text 2 **grants that the rise is real and that many homeschooled children do well, but holds that neither her studies nor her story shows what homeschooling causes**.
