In 1977, two NASA spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, set off to study the outer planets. Each carried an unusual passenger: a gold-plated copper record, about thirty centimetres across, fixed to the side of the spacecraft.

The record was meant for anyone, or anything, that might one day find a spacecraft drifting between the stars. A small committee led by the astronomer Carl Sagan chose what to put on it. The result includes spoken greetings in 55 languages, sounds such as thunder, footsteps, laughter and a kiss, about ninety minutes of music from many cultures, and more than a hundred images stored as sound signals.

The cover is a puzzle in itself. Since no one could assume that the finders would know English, or even our way of writing numbers, the designers drew the instructions as diagrams, using a tiny interval of time linked to the hydrogen atom as the basic unit. A map shows the position of the Sun relative to fourteen pulsars, stars that flash with very regular rhythms, so that a skilled finder could work out where the record came from.

Many people doubt that the record will ever be found. Space is enormous, and the spacecraft will take tens of thousands of years even to come near another star. Sagan himself admitted that the chance of discovery was small. He argued, however, that launching such a message said something hopeful about the people who sent it.

In that sense, the record may matter most to its makers. Choosing what to send forced a small group of people to ask what was worth saying about humanity, and to whom.
