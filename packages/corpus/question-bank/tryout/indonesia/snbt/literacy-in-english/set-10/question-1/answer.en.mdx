# Given

In the delayed bridge experiment, Argentine ant colonies stayed on the **long branch** even after a shorter branch was added because the old trail's scent drowned out the new shortcut. Paragraph <InlineMath math="5" /> explains why that design still works in nature.

# Asked

Which statement best captures the author's overall view of colonies that stayed on the long branch after a shortcut appeared?

# Solution

> A stuck colony is not a machine that has broken down. It is a system paying for a design that trades flexibility for reliability.

Connect the **positive-feedback mechanism** in paragraphs <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> with the **closing trade-off evaluation** in paragraph <InlineMath math="5" />.

## Connecting the mechanism to the trade off

The same pheromone loop that finds the shorter path when both open together locks colonies onto the old path when a shortcut arrives late. Yet where good food sources stay put, <Highlight>sticking with a working route is usually worth more than constantly checking for a better one</Highlight>, making the stuck colony an expected **cost of reliable feedback**.

The distractors distort key details. Most colonies chose the short branch when both opened together. The author calls evolved evaporation rates <Highlight>far from established</Highlight> and notes a ceiling on fast fading. Colonies do solve shortest-path setups when both routes open together, and remembered routes apply to other species rather than the Argentine ants here.

# Answer

The author's overall view is that **they show a cost of the same feedback that finds short routes, a cost that is usually worth paying where food sources stay put**.
