Text 1 comes from a mother's blog. Text 2 comes from a column by an education researcher.

**Text 1**

When our district closed its schools in the spring of 2020, I assumed I would be teaching my son and daughter at home for a few weeks. They never went back, and I no longer think of it as an emergency measure. My daughter, who had been drifting through math, covered <InlineMath math="2" /> years of the curriculum in <InlineMath math="18" /> months once she could move at her own pace. My son, who could not sit still through a <InlineMath math="6" />-hour school day, now does his reading in the morning and spends his afternoons at a community farm.

We are hardly alone. Census Bureau surveys found that the share of American households with school-age children that reported homeschooling rose from <InlineMath math="5.4\%" /> in the spring of 2020 to <InlineMath math="11.1\%" /> that autumn. Many of those families, like mine, discovered how much of a school day is spent waiting.

Critics warn that homeschooled children miss out on friendships, yet mine see other children every day, at co-op classes, at soccer and at the library. Critics also say that parents lack training. No one, however, knows my children's habits and gaps better than I do, and a class made up of my own children hardly needs classroom-management skills.

What the critics rarely mention is that study after study finds homeschooled students scoring well above the national average on standardized tests. If the results are that good, the burden of proof should fall on those who want to tighten the rules, not on the families who choose to teach at home. Our experience is not a fluke. It is what happens when learning is fitted to the child instead of the child to the school.

**Text 2**

The surge in homeschooling since 2020 is real, but it is harder to measure than headlines suggest. In several states, families need not tell anyone that they are teaching at home, and the states that do keep records count in different ways, so national figures rest largely on household surveys. A survey is a snapshot. It cannot tell us how many families who began homeschooling during the pandemic have since returned to school.

The larger problem lies in claims about results. The studies most often cited to show that homeschooled students outperform their peers rely on families who volunteer to take part and to report their children's scores. Families whose children are doing well have more reason to volunteer. Such samples can tell us that many homeschooled children do very well, which I do not doubt. They cannot tell us how a typical homeschooled child performs, or whether learning at home is the reason the high scorers score high. Studies that compare similar families, or that follow children who move between home and school, are rare, and their findings are mixed.

None of this is an argument against homeschooling. Some children, especially those who were bullied or whose needs a school never met, plainly flourish at home. But stories from families for whom it works are exactly the evidence we should expect to hear most, since families for whom it went badly are far less likely to write about it. Before deciding how much oversight homeschooling needs, we should learn much more about the children we currently cannot see: those whose parents have neither the time nor the training that the success stories take for granted.
