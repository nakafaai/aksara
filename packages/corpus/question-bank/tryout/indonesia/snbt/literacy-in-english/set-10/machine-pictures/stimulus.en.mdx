Type a short description into an image generator, wait a few seconds, and a finished picture appears. Many people who make such pictures want to own them in the way a painter owns a painting. In the United States, however, the Copyright Office has refused to register images produced entirely by an AI system, on the grounds that copyright protects works of human authorship.

I think the Office is right, though not for the reason most people give. The usual objection is that the machine does the work. But a camera does much of the work of a photograph, and photographs have been protected for well over a century. What matters is not how much a tool does but who makes the creative choices. A photographer chooses the subject, the moment, the angle, and the light. A person typing a prompt chooses only the request. The system decides the shapes, the colours, and the composition, and the same prompt can produce a hundred different pictures. That is closer to ordering a picture than to making one, and a person who commissions a portrait does not become its author.

This does not mean that anything touched by AI should go unprotected. An illustrator who generates a rough background, repaints it, combines it with her own drawings, and arranges the result has made real creative choices. The law should protect those choices, and only those.

A harder question, which this argument does not settle, is whether such systems were trained fairly on the work of human artists.
