In 1799, French soldiers rebuilding a fort near the Egyptian town of Rashid, known to Europeans as Rosetta, uncovered a broken slab of dark stone covered in writing. Two years later, under the agreement that ended the French campaign in Egypt, the stone passed to the British, and it has been displayed in the British Museum since 1802. Its value was recognised almost at once. The slab carried a single decree, issued by priests in 196 BC, written three times: in hieroglyphs, in Demotic, a script used for everyday documents, and in ancient Greek, which scholars could already read.

A text in a known language beside the same text in an unknown script seemed to promise a quick solution. It took more than twenty years. One obstacle was an assumption that had shaped European thinking for centuries: that hieroglyphs were not writing in the ordinary sense but symbols expressing mystical ideas. A scholar who believed that each sign stood for a whole concept had little reason to look for sounds.

The British physician and physicist Thomas Young made important early progress. He noticed that some hieroglyphs were enclosed in oval rings, now called cartouches, and argued that these contained royal names. By matching the cartouche on the stone with the name Ptolemy in the Greek text, he worked out the sound values of several signs. Young, however, believed that such sound signs were used only to spell foreign names like Ptolemy, which was Greek. For native Egyptian words, he assumed, the symbols kept their purely pictorial meaning.

The decisive step came from Jean-François Champollion, a French scholar who had studied Coptic, the late form of the Egyptian language that survived in the Christian church services of Egypt. In 1822 he examined cartouches copied from older temples and recognised in them the names of Egyptian kings such as Ramesses and Thutmose, spelled partly with sound signs. Foreign names were not the only ones written phonetically. Because he knew Coptic, he could also guess how many Egyptian words had sounded and test his readings against them.

Champollion's central insight was that hieroglyphic writing mixed different kinds of signs. Some stood for sounds, some for whole words, and some were silent markers that indicated the category of a word. Once scholars accepted that the system was a working script, readings that had resisted generations of effort began to fall into place.
