Paragraph 1 says the stone carried **a single decree written three times**, and one of the versions was "ancient Greek, which scholars could already read". Having **the same text in a known language** beside the unknown script gave scholars a way to match parts of the two, which is why the stone "seemed to promise a quick solution".

- The decree dates from 196 BC, and the passage never calls it the oldest Egyptian writing.
- The Greek text repeated the decree. It did not explain pronunciation.
- No pictures of meanings accompany the signs.
- The stone was found while soldiers were rebuilding a fort, not in a temple Champollion had studied.

So the stone was valuable because it carried the same decree in hieroglyphs and in readable Greek.
