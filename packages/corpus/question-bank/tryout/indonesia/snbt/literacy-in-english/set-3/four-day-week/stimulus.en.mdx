Few workplace ideas have spread as quickly in recent years as the four-day week. In a 2022 trial in the United Kingdom, most of the roughly sixty participating companies chose to keep the shorter week after the trial ended, and many reported that staff were less exhausted while output held steady. Results like these have led some campaigners to call for laws that would make a four-day week standard. That would be a mistake, not because the idea is bad, but because the evidence does not yet support applying it everywhere.

The first difficulty is that "four-day week" describes two very different arrangements. In a reduced week, employees work fewer total hours, often around thirty-two, for the same pay. In a compressed week, they work the same forty hours squeezed into four longer days. The encouraging trial results come almost entirely from reduced weeks, and they usually depended on companies cutting unnecessary meetings and redesigning how work was done. Simply cramming forty hours into four days offers none of those changes and may leave people more tired, not less.

The second difficulty is who took part. Companies joined these trials voluntarily, and firms that expected the change to suit them were more likely to sign up. Office-based businesses that can measure output by finished projects were heavily represented. Hospitals, bus companies, and care homes, where someone must be present every hour of every day, were not. A rule that works for a design studio may simply leave a ward short of nurses.

None of this means governments should ignore the idea. Public bodies could fund trials in sectors that have so far been left out, require participating employers to share their data, and publish the results whether they are good or bad. Workers in shift-based jobs might benefit from different solutions, such as more predictable rosters, rather than a fixed day off.

The four-day week may well become common in some industries. Turning promising trials in volunteer companies into a national rule, however, would treat a hopeful hypothesis as a settled finding.
