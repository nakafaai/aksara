In 1997 the journal Nature published a field experiment by the forest ecologist Suzanne Simard and colleagues in British Columbia. They fed paper birch and Douglas fir seedlings carbon dioxide labelled with different carbon isotopes and traced where it ended up. Some of the carbon crossed between the species in both directions, with a net gain for the firs that depended on how deeply they were shaded. The obvious route was the mycorrhizal fungi that grow on and into tree roots, trading minerals for sugar. Their threads, or hyphae, can join several trees at once. The journal's cover called the result the "wood wide web", and the phrase soon escaped the journal.

Then the story grew. In bestselling books and documentaries, forests became communities where old "mother trees" feed their shaded offspring through the network, trees under insect attack warn their neighbours, and fungal threads link almost every tree in a stand. Part of the appeal is moral. The image turns a forest from a crowd of competitors into something closer to a family, and that may explain why it travelled so far beyond the evidence that first suggested it.

In February 2023 the mycorrhizal researchers Justine Karst, Melanie Jones and Jason Hoeksema published a review in Nature Ecology & Evolution that checked the most popular claims against the field studies behind them. The claims that the networks are widespread in forests and that resources moving through them improve the performance of seedlings, they judged insufficiently supported: the results vary widely, have other explanations, or come from too few sites to generalise. For the remaining claim, that mature trees preferentially send resources and warning signals to their own offspring, they found no peer-reviewed field evidence at all. The seedling studies were especially awkward: roughly as many found that joining a presumed network helped seedlings as found that it held them back.

The "other explanations" are where the trouble lies. Finding labelled carbon in a neighbour's roots shows that carbon moved, not that it moved through a shared fungus. Roots leak sugars into the soil, and the roots of a nearby tree, or fungi that belong to that tree alone, can take them up. Some experiments separate these routes by growing seedlings in mesh bags. A fine mesh lets water and dissolved substances pass but blocks hyphae, while a coarser mesh lets hyphae through as well. Even a clear difference between the two treatments does not settle the matter alone, because the fine mesh also limits how much soil the seedling's own fungi can explore. And labelled carbon found in a root may still sit inside fungal tissue, in which case the fungus, not the seedling, has kept it.

None of this shows that the networks are imaginary. Fungal links between neighbouring trees have been mapped at a few sites, and carbon clearly can pass between plants. What the critics dispute is the leap from "can" to "routinely does, and for the good of the receiver". Their less comfortable finding concerns the literature itself: citations of the field studies leaned towards positive results, unsupported statements had become more common in recent papers, and the authors conceded that some of those statements appeared in their own earlier work. A field that cites selectively can grow more confident than any single study in it, which matters if the generous mother tree ever shapes how forests are logged.
