My father has lived in this country for twenty-two years and speaks its language well enough to argue with the tax office, which he does every spring with great pleasure. So when he asked me to come with him to the clinic and "help with the words", I assumed he meant the long ones.

The doctor spoke to me, not to him, from the start. She said "blood pressure" and "medication" and "lifestyle", and I turned each word over to my father in our own language as if handing him something hot. He nodded at all of it. When she asked whether he had been taking the tablets every day, he answered her directly, in her language, before I could open my mouth.

"Every day."

On the bus home I told him he had not needed me at all. He looked out of the window for two stops. Then he said that the doctor's words had never been the problem. It was her face while she said them. "When you tell me, I hear the news. When she tells me, I hear the news and I watch her decide what kind of man I am."

I asked why he had answered the question about the tablets himself, then.

"Because that one was about me," he said, "not about my heart."

At home he took the box of tablets out of the kitchen drawer, where it had sat since his previous appointment months before, and put it on the windowsill beside his reading glasses, where he would see it every morning.
