In 1963 a Tanzanian schoolboy named Erasto Mpemba noticed that an ice-cream mixture he had put into the freezer while it was still hot froze before his classmates' cooler mixtures. His teacher told him he must be confused. Years later he described the observation to a visiting physicist, Denis Osborne, who tried it in a laboratory, and in 1969 the two published a short paper reporting that hot water could indeed freeze first. The idea was not new. Aristotle had written of people who warmed water before setting it out to cool, and Francis Bacon and René Descartes repeated similar claims. But the paper gave the effect a name, and the name stuck.

Explanations multiplied. Hot water loses some of its mass by evaporation, so less is left to freeze. It holds less dissolved gas. It stirs itself as warm water rises, carrying heat to the surface. Cooler water, some argued, is more likely to supercool, staying liquid below zero. Each mechanism sounded reasonable, and each could be shown to matter in some setup.

The trouble lies in the word "freeze". Some experiments timed how long the water took to reach zero degrees. Others timed the first crystals of ice, or the moment the whole sample was solid. These are different events, and a sample that wins one race can lose another. Supercooling makes things worse: whether a sample stays liquid at five degrees below zero or freezes at one below can depend on a speck of dust, so two cups prepared in exactly the same way may behave differently.

In 2016 two researchers in Britain set out to measure the simplest version carefully: the time taken for water to cool to zero degrees. They found no sign that hot water got there first. They also found that a small change in where the thermometer sat in the water was enough to produce an apparent Mpemba effect or to erase one, which may explain why earlier results disagreed. Their study does not show that hot water can never freeze first in any freezer. It shows that the effect, if it exists at all, is far smaller and more fragile than its fame suggests.
