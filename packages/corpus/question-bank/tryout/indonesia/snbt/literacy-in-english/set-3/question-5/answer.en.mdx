# Given

In a **single trial**, a student puts two identical cups of water (<InlineMath math="70^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="20^\circ\text{C}" />) into a freezer and observes the hot cup **turning completely solid first**.

# Asked

Based on the passage, which conclusion is best supported by this result?

# Solution

Combine **two caveats** from paragraphs <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="4" />: the student timed a different endpoint than <InlineMath math="2016" />, and a lone trial is vulnerable to random supercooling.

> Turning completely solid is a different milestone from cooling to zero degrees, and because a single speck of dust can shift freezing via supercooling, one run is weak evidence.

## Applying both passage cautions

- **No contradiction with <InlineMath math="2016" />**: The <InlineMath math="2016" /> researchers measured <Highlight>the time taken for water to cool to zero degrees</Highlight>, not turning completely solid, and <Highlight>a sample that wins one race can lose another</Highlight>.
- **Chance in a single trial**: Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> notes freezing under supercooling <Highlight>can depend on a speck of dust, so two cups prepared in exactly the same way may behave differently</Highlight>.

## Why the other options fail

- **<InlineMath math="2016" /> measured the wrong thing** misreads the text: <InlineMath math="2016" /> deliberately isolated the simplest version rather than making an error.
- **Evaporation fully explains the result** overclaims: evaporation is only one of several mechanisms that can matter in some setups.
- **Contradicts the <InlineMath math="2016" /> study** misses that the two tests timed completely different events.
- **<InlineMath math="2016" /> found hot water freezes first in home freezers** invents a finding directly opposite to what the study reported.

# Answer

**The result does not contradict the 2016 study, which timed cooling to zero degrees, and a single trial may reflect chance details such as supercooling**.
