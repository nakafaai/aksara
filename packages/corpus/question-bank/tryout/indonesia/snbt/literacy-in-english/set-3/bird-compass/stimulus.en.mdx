Each autumn, millions of small songbirds leave northern Europe for Africa, many of them flying at night and alone. How they keep their direction has puzzled biologists for more than a century. The sun and stars provide part of the answer, but birds held in cages with no view of the sky still try to head in the correct seasonal direction. Something else is guiding them: Earth's magnetic field.

Experiments in the 1960s and 1970s showed that this sense does not work like a hiking compass. A hiking compass needle points toward magnetic north. Birds, by contrast, seem to respond to the angle at which magnetic field lines dip toward the ground. Near the equator the lines run almost parallel to the surface, while closer to the poles they plunge steeply. When researchers reversed only the vertical part of the field, robins changed direction as if north and south had swapped, even though the horizontal direction was unchanged. The birds were reading "toward the pole" versus "toward the equator", not "north" versus "south".

Where the sensor sits is more disputed. For years, the leading candidate was a set of iron-containing cells in the upper beak, which were thought to act like tiny magnets. In 2012, however, a detailed study found that these cells were mostly immune cells that had absorbed iron, not nerve cells capable of sending signals. Attention has since shifted to the eye. A protein called cryptochrome, found in the retina, undergoes chemical reactions when struck by light, and the outcome of those reactions may depend on how the molecule is aligned with the magnetic field. If so, birds might literally see the field as a pattern overlaid on their normal vision.

Some of the most striking support for this idea came by accident. Researchers studying robins in a German city found that the birds could not orient themselves in wooden huts on the university campus. Only when the huts were lined with grounded aluminium screens, which blocked weak electromagnetic noise from everyday electronic equipment, did the robins recover their sense of direction. Noise that weak should have no effect on an iron-based magnet, but it could disturb the delicate chemical reactions proposed for cryptochrome.

None of this settles the question completely. Cryptochrome's sensitivity has been demonstrated mainly in laboratory conditions, and birds may combine several cues, including the stars, smells, and landmarks. Still, the case for a light-dependent compass in the eye is stronger now than at any time in the past.
