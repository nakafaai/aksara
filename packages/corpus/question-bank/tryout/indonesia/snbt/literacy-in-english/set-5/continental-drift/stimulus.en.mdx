On 6 January 1912, Alfred Wegener, a meteorologist rather than a geologist, told a meeting in Frankfurt that the continents had once formed a single landmass and had since moved apart. A book in 1915, revised until 1929, set out the full case. The coasts of South America and Africa fit together like the torn halves of a page. Matching rock layers and fossils of the same plant, *Glossopteris*, turn up on landmasses that oceans now separate. Traces of ancient ice sheets lie in India and southern Africa, while coal, which forms from swamp plants, is found in Antarctica.

The episode is often retold as a parable: a bold outsider was right, and a closed-minded establishment laughed at him until the evidence forced it to apologise. That version flatters hindsight more than it explains the history. Wegener's critics did not mainly dispute his maps. They asked what could push a continent through the solid rock of the ocean floor, and his answers were weak. He pointed to a slight push away from the poles caused by the Earth's spin, and to the tidal pull of the Moon and the Sun. Physicists showed that both forces were far too feeble to move a continent, and the British geophysicist Harold Jeffreys argued, correctly, that a continent could not plough through the ocean floor without breaking apart. Wegener's own estimate of Greenland's drift, worked out from old longitude readings, did not help. It came out at metres per year, whereas the Atlantic is actually widening by about <InlineMath math="2.5" /> centimetres a year.

Yet the critics' case had a weakness of its own. To explain why the same fossils appear on both sides of an ocean, many relied on land bridges, strips of land that supposedly linked the continents and later sank without trace. No one had found such a bridge, and a sunken bridge could not explain the ancient ice in places that are hot today. A theory that describes a pattern without explaining its cause is not thereby wrong, and a rival that has no evidence of its own is not thereby right. Even so, at a symposium in New York in November 1926, nearly every speaker rejected drift.

Wegener died on the Greenland ice in 1930, his idea still a minority view. The evidence that changed minds came from the ocean floor, which he could hardly study. In the 1950s, ships towing magnetic instruments found the seabed magnetised in long stripes, and by the mid-1960s surveys showed that the stripes on one side of a mid-ocean ridge mirrored those on the other. In 1962 Harry Hess published what is now called seafloor spreading: molten rock rises at the ridges, forms new crust and moves outward, so the seafloor should be youngest at a ridge and older farther away. Cores drilled across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge in 1968 showed exactly that pattern of ages.

The irony is that this evidence confirmed Wegener's conclusion and his critics' main objection at the same time. Continents do not plough through the ocean floor. They ride on plates that include the seafloor itself, carried away from the ridges as new crust forms. Geologists still debate which forces drive the plates, the very question Wegener could not answer. His critics were right to demand a mechanism. Where they went wrong was treating the missing mechanism as proof that the movement itself could not be real.
