A statement gets Yes only if **both writers hold it**. If one writer holds it and the other rejects it or never says it, the answer is No.

**Late and over budget**. Text 1 says the recent reactors "opened years behind schedule and far over their first budgets." Text 2 replies, "The overruns are real." Both hold it, so Yes.

**Batteries already enough**. Text 1 says batteries "cannot yet carry a whole grid through a windless winter week." Its hope that storage "may well have closed the gap" is about the future, not now. Text 2 doubts storage too. Neither writer holds this, so No.

**Later units cheaper**. Only Text 2 hints at this, with "Whether the fourth is depends on whether we build a fourth." Even there it's a condition. Text 1 never says it. One-sided, so No.

**Early cuts worth more**. Text 1: "a tonne not emitted in 2030 does more good than a tonne avoided in 2045." Text 2: "early cuts do count most." Both hold it, so Yes.

**Plant-by-plant comparison is fair**. This is the trap. Text 2 says "I do not dispute it" about the plant-by-plant numbers, but then adds, "A grid, though, is not a pile of separate plants." Text 2 accepts the numbers and **rejects them as the basis for the choice**. So No.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Recent reactors were late and over budget | Yes |
| Batteries can already carry a windless spell | No |
| A series of one design lowers later costs | No |
| Early emission cuts are worth more | Yes |
| Plant-by-plant cost is a fair basis | No |

The shared ground is the record of overruns and the value of early cuts. Everything else belongs to one writer, or to neither.
