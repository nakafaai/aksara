Few policy ideas generate as much excitement as a universal basic income: a regular cash payment to every adult, with no conditions attached. Supporters point to a growing list of pilot programmes. Between 2017 and 2018, for example, Finland paid 2,000 unemployed people 560 euros a month whether or not they found work. Recipients reported less stress than a comparison group, and they did not work less. Results like these are encouraging. They are also far less informative about a universal scheme than they first appear.

The first problem is scale. A pilot gives money to a few thousand people while everyone around them carries on as before. A national scheme would give money to millions at once, and that changes the situation the money enters. Landlords and shopkeepers might raise prices once they knew every customer had extra income. Employers might cut wages, assuming the payment would make up the difference. None of these responses can appear when only a small group receives the payment.

The second problem is time. Many pilots last only a few years, and participants know when the payments will stop. A person who expects the money to end may keep a job they dislike, while someone promised payments for life might leave it, retrain, or start a business. A temporary trial cannot show which of these would happen.

The third problem is cost. In a pilot, the money comes from a government budget or a charity. In a universal scheme, it would have to come from taxes, and higher taxes affect how people work and spend in ways a pilot never tests.

None of this means pilots are useless. They show clearly how people use cash when it arrives without conditions, and they have challenged the fear that recipients would simply stop working. But if governments want to know what a permanent, universal payment would do, they will need larger and longer trials, ideally covering whole towns, and they should admit that even these would leave important questions open.
