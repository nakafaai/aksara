Each statement links two facts. Ask whether the passage itself makes that link, not whether the link sounds reasonable.

**Greenland's speed and the feeble forces**. Paragraph 2 gives two separate problems. Physicists showed the forces "were far too feeble to move a continent." Then, as an extra point, the Greenland estimate "did not help." Nothing says the speed estimate was a reason for the physicists' verdict. This one **joins two separate objections**, so it's No.

**Land bridges and the ice**. Paragraph 3 says critics used land bridges "to explain why the same fossils appear on both sides of an ocean", and that a sunken bridge "could not explain the ancient ice in places that are hot today." Paragraph 1 names those places: "India and southern Africa." Put the two paragraphs together and the statement holds. Yes.

**The 1926 symposium**. Paragraph 3 says only that "nearly every speaker rejected it." The line "did not mainly dispute his maps" in paragraph 2 is about the critics in general, and "not mainly" isn't the same as "accepted". The word "only" goes even further. That's an **overreach**, so No.

**Jeffreys's objection**. Paragraph 2 says Jeffreys argued "correctly" that a continent can't plough through the ocean floor. Paragraph 5 agrees: "Continents do not plough through the ocean floor." The seafloor evidence backed Jeffreys. No.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Greenland's speed was a reason physicists judged the forces too feeble | No |
| Land bridges explained the fossils but not the ice in India | Yes |
| Most 1926 speakers accepted the evidence and objected only to the mechanism | No |
| The seafloor evidence showed Jeffreys was mistaken | No |

Only the land-bridge statement can be concluded. The other three either invent a link, stretch a general remark to one event, or flip what the seafloor showed.
