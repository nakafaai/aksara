The question asks what "flatters hindsight" means here. Hindsight is looking back after you already know how things ended. The everyday meaning of "flatter", to praise someone, is the trap.

Look at what the author does right after the phrase. Paragraph 2 lists the critics' actual objections: no one could say what would "push a continent through the solid rock of the ocean floor", Wegener's forces were "far too feeble", and Jeffreys argued "correctly" that a continent would break apart. Even Wegener's Greenland speed was badly off, at "metres per year".

So the parable makes the story easy for people who already know drift is real. It makes the critics look silly, but it leaves out that **their objections were sound at the time**. That's what "flatters hindsight more than it explains the history" means: the story suits what we know now and skips what people could know then.

Why the others fail:

- This reads "flatters" literally as praise for later scientists. The passage never compares how warmly anyone is praised.
- The author says the critics "did not mainly dispute his maps", so Wegener's evidence wasn't the weak point.
- The passage says Wegener's Greenland estimate was wrong, but it never says **who** measured the real rate, and it doesn't credit the critics.
- No physicist had confirmed drift by 1926. Physicists showed his proposed forces were too feeble.

So the author means that the popular story judges the debate with knowledge from decades later and skips objections that were reasonable when they were made.
