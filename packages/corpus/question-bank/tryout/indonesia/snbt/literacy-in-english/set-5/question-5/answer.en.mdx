This asks what job the Greenland detail does in the argument, not what the detail says.

Look at where it sits. Paragraph 2 opens by calling the popular story a "parable" in which a "closed-minded establishment laughed at him." The author says this version "flatters hindsight." Then the paragraph lists reasons the critics had for doubting Wegener. The Greenland estimate is the last one: it came out "at metres per year", while the real rate is about <InlineMath math="2.5" /> centimetres a year.

So the detail adds **a flaw in Wegener's own data**. If his own measurement was wrong by that much, some doubt was fair. That supports the paragraph's point that the critics weren't simply dismissing him, which **weakens the parable**.

Why the others fail:

- The real rate of about <InlineMath math="2.5" /> centimetres a year means the continents were moving, just slowly. Being far too fast isn't the same as zero.
- The physicists' verdict on the forces is a separate point. The Greenland estimate "did not help", but it isn't the reason the forces were judged feeble.
- The passage never says who measured the true rate, so giving the credit to the critics adds something new.
- The author never argues that a slow rate is too slow. The point is that Wegener's figure was too high.

So the Greenland estimate is there to show that some doubt about drift rested on a real flaw in Wegener's own evidence, which undercuts the story of a closed-minded establishment.
