# Given

At the end of paragraph <InlineMath math="2" />, the author notes Wegener estimated Greenland's drift at **metres per year** from old longitude readings, <Highlight>whereas the Atlantic is actually widening by about <InlineMath math="2.5" /> centimetres a year</Highlight>.

# Asked

Why does the author include this detail about Wegener's Greenland drift estimate?

# Solution

Read the **rhetorical role** of the detail inside paragraph <InlineMath math="2" />, whose topic is overturning the simplistic parable of a closed-minded establishment laughing at a lone genius.

> Highlighting an order-of-magnitude error in Wegener's own longitude calculation shows scepticism rested partly on real flaws in his data, undercutting the myth that critics dismissed him blindly.

## Role inside paragraph two

Paragraph <InlineMath math="2" /> lists legitimate reasons scientists doubted Wegener: weak forces, solid ocean rock, and then <Highlight>Wegener's own estimate of Greenland's drift, worked out from old longitude readings, did not help. It came out at metres per year</Highlight>.

Including a glaring error in **Wegener's own data** proves that contemporary doubt had a **fair evidential basis**, weakening the popular story of unthinking dismissal.

## Why the other options fail

- **Continents were standing still** contradicts the real widening rate of <InlineMath math="2.5" /> centimetres per year.
- **Explaining why spin force was too weak** confuses two separate points: physicists judged the force directly, not via Greenland longitude readings.
- **Critics measured Greenland more accurately** adds a claim absent from the text, which never names who measured the true rate.
- **A few centimetres a year is too slow** invents an argument the author never makes: the point is that Wegener's estimate was far too high.

# Answer

**To show that some doubt about drift had a fair basis in Wegener's own data, which weakens the story of critics who dismissed him unthinkingly**.
