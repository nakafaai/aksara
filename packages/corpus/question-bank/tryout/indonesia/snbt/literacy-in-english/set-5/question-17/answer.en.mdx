# Given

**During a freezing night**, the grandfather keeps sprinklers running on the orchard blossoms and says that as long as the water keeps coming, the freezing keeps them warm.

# Asked

Which statements about the grandfather's frost-protection method can be inferred from the story.

# Solution

**The story explains the** method in one line, which the grandfather says to a branch: "As long as the water keeps coming, the freezing keeps them warm." Hold that line against each statement.

> It is not the frozen shell itself that keeps the blossom safe, it is the continuous freezing of fresh water arriving all night.

## Testing each inference against the grandfather actions

Statement <InlineMath math="1" />. The protection lasts "as long as the water keeps coming", so the water has to keep freezing onto the blossoms through the cold. Yes.

Statement <InlineMath math="2" />. The protection holds only "as long as the water keeps coming". Stop the water and **the freezing that keeps them warm stops too**, ice or no ice. No.

Statement <InlineMath math="3" />. He insists the valve be shut "only when the last of it had run clear off the twigs, not before", and makes her explain why. That much care about timing implies that shutting it early could do harm. Yes.

Statement <InlineMath math="4" />. When he made her say why the valve stays open, "I said it badly." She didn't explain it without mistakes, even by morning. No.

Statement <InlineMath math="5" />. Watered trees set fruit and the unwatered ones across the road stood bare. That fits the method working, but one comparison on one night doesn't prove it, and the statement says exactly that. Yes.

# Answer

**The method relies on uninterrupted freezing water until thaw, not on dry ice coating or turning the valve off early**.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| The method needs water freezing on all night | Yes |
| The blossoms stay safe with the water off | No |
| Shutting the valve too early could harm the blossoms | Yes |
| By morning she could explain it without mistakes | No |
| The Hallers' trees fit the method but don't prove it | Yes |
