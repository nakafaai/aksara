Mina had promised to lead her science-fair team without making every decision herself. Yet during their first laboratory session, she rushed through the plan and dismissed two suggestions before the speakers could explain them. Both students fell silent. The group kept moving, but one measurement in the draft procedure was unreliable and nobody challenged it.

At the next meeting, Mina did not begin by defending the schedule. She asked every member to name one concern before the team chose its next step, and she wrote each concern on the board without arguing. One student then explained why the proposed measurement could vary with the room's lighting. Another suggested a method the team could test that afternoon.

The team replaced the weak measurement and divided the remaining work openly. Mina still had to coordinate the project, and one conversation did not guarantee that everyone would speak freely in future meetings. It did, however, change the decision process: disagreement became information the group could examine rather than a delay to be suppressed.

In her reflection, Mina used *psychological safety* to mean a condition in which people can raise questions or mistakes without fear of humiliation. The term fits the account because her specific action created room for concerns to be heard and tested. It would not fit if the team had merely completed the project quickly while its members remained silent.
