# Given

**Text 1** proposes keeping gas plants in reserve for rare windless winter weeks because their emissions would be small. Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> of **Text 2** replies directly to this reserve-gas plan.

# Asked

Which option accurately names both the part Text 2 accepts and the part Text 2 rejects?

# Solution

Read the **two consecutive sentences** in paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> of Text 2: the first grants low emissions while rarely used, and the second rejects relying on gas after a gas phase-out.

> Text 2 concedes that rarely operated gas reserves stay clean enough for now, yet rejects relying on them once a country actually commits to stop burning gas in the 2040s.

## Splitting acceptance and rejection in Text two

Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> of Text 2 gives **both halves verbatim**: <Highlight>They stay cheap and clean enough only while they run rarely and only while we still accept burning gas. A country that means to stop burning gas will need something else for the windless week</Highlight>.

- **Accepted**: Rarely run reserve gas plants would emit little.
- **Rejected**: Relying on those gas plants once the country stops burning gas.

## Why the other options fail

- **Accepts burning gas, rejects low emissions** reverses the two halves.
- **Rejects quick wind and solar construction** contradicts Text 2 wanting this decade's money to go mainly to wind and solar.
- **Accepts storage will close the gap by the <InlineMath math="2040" />s** contradicts Text 2 doubting storage improves <Highlight>faster than anyone can promise</Highlight>.
- **Rejects gas plants for costing more to build than reactors** invents a cost comparison never made in Text 2.

# Answer

**It accepts that gas plants used only in rare windless weeks would emit little, but rejects the idea that the country can rely on them once it stops burning gas**.
