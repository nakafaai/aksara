You need two things: what Text 1's gas plan quietly depends on, and which part of it Text 2 attacks.

**Text 1's plan.** Its last paragraph says gas plants "kept in reserve for those rare weeks can fill the gap." Then: "By the time a new reactor could be ready, storage may well have closed the gap for good." Gas is the stopgap, and storage is supposed to take over later. If storage never catches up, the stopgap never ends, and Text 1 has given no other way out. So the plan **depends on storage arriving in time**.

**Text 2's objection.** It says a country that means to stop burning gas "will need something else for the windless week, and it will need it in the 2040s unless storage improves faster than anyone can promise." That sentence targets exactly the hope that storage will replace gas before gas has to go.

Why the others fail:

- Text 2 doesn't dispute that rarely used gas plants emit little. It says they stay "clean enough only while they run rarely", which **accepts** that point.
- Both writers agree that renewables can be built faster. Text 2 directs this decade's money to them.
- Low emissions and steady output from reactors are facts both writers accept, so they aren't what's under attack.
- Text 1 never assumes this. It says batteries "cannot yet" do it, which is why it brings in gas.

So Text 2's objection is aimed at Text 1's assumption that storage will improve enough to replace the reserve gas plants before the country must stop burning gas.
