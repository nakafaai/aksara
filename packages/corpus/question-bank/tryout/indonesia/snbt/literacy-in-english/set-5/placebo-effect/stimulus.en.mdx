In 1955, the American anaesthetist Henry Beecher published a paper called "The Powerful Placebo". Reviewing earlier studies, he concluded that about a third of patients felt better after receiving a treatment with no active ingredient, such as a sugar pill. The figure was repeated for decades and helped make the placebo a standard part of drug trials, in which a new medicine must beat a dummy treatment, not merely beat doing nothing.

Later researchers argued that Beecher had counted too much. Many illnesses improve on their own: colds clear up, back pain eases, and patients tend to seek help when their symptoms are at their worst, so they are likely to feel better soon afterwards whatever they are given. When a 2001 review compared patients given placebos with patients given no treatment at all, the difference was small for most conditions and clearest for symptoms that patients report themselves, such as pain.

That does not mean the placebo effect is imaginary. In a well-known experiment from the 1970s, patients recovering from dental surgery received a placebo they believed was a painkiller, and many reported less pain. When some of them were also given naloxone, a drug that blocks the body's own opioid chemicals, much of that relief disappeared. Expectation, it seems, can trigger real chemistry in the brain.

Expectation can also work in the opposite direction. Patients warned about the possible side effects of a drug sometimes report those side effects even when they have been given a dummy pill. This is known as the nocebo effect, and it creates an awkward problem for doctors, who must tell patients about risks without planting symptoms in their minds.

Perhaps the strangest finding is that a placebo may help even when patients know what it is. In a 2010 study, people with irritable bowel syndrome who were openly given "placebo pills" reported greater improvement than a group given no pills. The study was small, and patients' own reports were its main measure, but it suggests that the ritual of treatment, and not only deception, can matter.
