In the summer of 1858, the River Thames smelled so bad that newspapers called it "the Great Stink". For decades, London's sewers, carrying the waste of a fast-growing population, had emptied straight into the river. That June was unusually hot, the water was low, and sewage baked on the exposed riverbanks. Inside the Houses of Parliament, which stand beside the Thames, curtains were soaked in chemicals in an attempt to block the smell.

Many Victorians feared the smell for more than its unpleasantness. A widely held theory claimed that diseases such as cholera spread through "miasma", bad air given off by rotting matter. If foul air caused disease, a stinking river threatened everyone who breathed near it. Within weeks, Parliament passed a law allowing the Metropolitan Board of Works to raise money for a new system of sewers.

The board's chief engineer, Joseph Bazalgette, designed large intercepting sewers that ran alongside the river and caught the waste before it could reach the Thames in central London. Gravity and pumping stations carried it east to outfalls far downstream of the city. Some of the new sewers were built inside embankments that also created new roads along the river. When Bazalgette calculated the size of pipe each district needed, he is said to have doubled it, reasoning that the work would be done only once and that the city would keep growing.

The new system succeeded, but partly for a reason its builders did not fully understand. In 1854, the doctor John Snow had traced a cholera outbreak in Soho to a single contaminated water pump, evidence that the disease spread through water rather than air. Few people accepted his conclusion at the time. Bazalgette's sewers were built to remove a smell, yet by keeping sewage away from water that Londoners drank, they also removed a route for cholera. When the disease returned in 1866, it struck hardest in parts of the East End that the main system did not yet fully serve.

Bazalgette's sewers are still in use, but London's population is now far larger than anything he planned for. During heavy rain, the old sewers overflow into the Thames, and a new tunnel beneath the river has been built to catch that overflow. The problem the Victorians solved has partly returned because the city kept growing.
