**Text 1**

Every few years the case for new nuclear plants comes back, and every few years it meets the same arithmetic. The large reactors completed in Europe and North America over the past two decades opened years behind schedule and far over their first budgets. Supporters reply that nuclear power is clean and steady, and on both counts they are right. A reactor emits almost no carbon dioxide while it runs, and it produces power day and night whatever the weather. The question facing our government, however, is not whether nuclear power is good. It is whether the next large sum of public money should buy it.

Here timing settles the matter. Carbon dioxide stays in the air for centuries, so a tonne not emitted in 2030 does more good than a tonne avoided in 2045. Wind farms and solar parks can be planned and built within a few years. A reactor approved today would, on recent evidence, start generating in the late 2030s at the earliest. None of this would matter if a reactor ordered now could be running by the end of the decade, but no recent project in Europe or North America suggests that it could. Every billion committed to a reactor is a billion not spent on turbines, panels, power lines and batteries that would cut emissions now.

I accept that batteries cannot yet carry a whole grid through a windless winter week, and that some steady source will be needed once the last coal plants close. Gas plants kept in reserve for those rare weeks can fill the gap, and because they would seldom run, their emissions would be small. By the time a new reactor could be ready, storage may well have closed the gap for good. We should keep our existing reactors running while they remain safe and spend new money where it buys the fastest cuts.

**Text 2**

My colleague's arithmetic is sound as far as it goes. The overruns are real, and there is a reason for them that critics rarely mention. Most Western countries stopped building reactors for a generation, and the skills left with the workers who retired. A first reactor built by crews who have never built one will be slow and costly. Whether the fourth is depends on whether we build a fourth.

Two parts of the argument deserve a closer look. The first is cost. Measured plant by plant, new wind and solar now generate power more cheaply than new nuclear, and I do not dispute it. A grid, though, is not a pile of separate plants. The larger the share of its power that rises and falls with the weather, the more it must spend on storage, spare capacity and extra lines to cover the hours when that power falls away. This cost is modest while wind and sun supply part of the power and climbs steeply as they approach all of it. A source that runs steadily keeps it down.

The second is the reserve of gas plants. They stay cheap and clean enough only while they run rarely and only while we still accept burning gas. A country that means to stop burning gas will need something else for the windless week, and it will need it in the 2040s unless storage improves faster than anyone can promise.

So I agree that this decade's money should go mainly to wind, solar and the grid, because early cuts do count most. What I reject is the idea that the choice is one or the other. A short series of reactors of one proven design, started now, is the surest way to have them ready when the gas plants must close. If we wait until storage has failed us, we will have waited too long.
