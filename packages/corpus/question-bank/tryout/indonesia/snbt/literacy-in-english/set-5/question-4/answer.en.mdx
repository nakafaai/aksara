# Given

In paragraph <InlineMath math="5" />, the author pinpoints the **exact reasoning error** made by Wegener's critics: <Highlight>Where they went wrong was treating the missing mechanism as proof that the movement itself could not be real</Highlight>.

# Asked

Which medical scenario illustrates the exact same reasoning error?

# Solution

Match **both structural parts** of the critics' error: **repeated, solid evidence of a real effect** paired with **denying that the effect exists** solely because its mechanism is still unknown.

> Concluding that repeated fever reductions across clinical trials must be an illusion simply because no one knows how the drug acts replicates treating an unexplained mechanism as disproof of a real effect.

## Mapping the analogy to the critics

| Element | Wegener's critics | Matching medical scenario |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong observed pattern | Matching coasts, fossils, ancient ice | Fever drops in trial after trial |
| Missing explanation | No known force driving continents | No one can explain how the drug acts |
| Reasoning error | Conclude movement cannot be real | Decide patients' improvement is an illusion |

The doctor who **dismisses repeated trial results as an illusion** purely for lacking a mechanism matches the critics' exact mistake.

## Why the other options fail

- **Hesitating to prescribe while agreeing results look promising** asks for a mechanism without denying the effect (what the critics were right to do).
- **Crediting an undetected natural defence** mirrors the unsupported land-bridge hypothesis in paragraph <InlineMath math="3" />, not the paragraph <InlineMath math="5" /> error.
- **Prescribing while asking researchers how it acts** accepts the effect and seeks the cause, which avoids the error completely.
- **Waiting after one small trial** reflects caution over weak evidence rather than rejecting strong evidence.

# Answer

**A doctor sees a new drug lower fever in trial after trial but, since no one can say how it acts, decides the patients' improvement must be an illusion**.
