You need the exact error from the last paragraph, then a new situation with the same shape.

The final paragraph says the critics "were right to demand a mechanism." Their mistake was "treating the missing mechanism as proof that the movement itself could not be real." So the error has two parts: **the evidence for an effect is there**, and someone denies the effect **only because no one can explain how it happens**.

Now match that shape. The doctor who sees fever drop "in trial after trial" has the evidence. Because "no one can say how it acts", she decides the improvement is an illusion. Evidence present, cause unknown, effect denied. That's the critics' error.

Why the others fail:

- Waiting to understand a drug while agreeing the results look good is what the author says the critics were **right** to do, asking for a mechanism without denying the effect.
- Crediting an undetected natural defence matches the land-bridge problem in paragraph 3, a rival explanation with no evidence. That's a real criticism in the passage, but it isn't the error the final paragraph names.
- Doubting a study that used a faulty thermometer matches Wegener's bad Greenland estimate. That doubt is reasonable, not the error.
- Trusting a theory against the trial results is the opposite mistake. It puts the mechanism above the evidence.

So the matching case is the doctor who denies an effect that trials keep showing, only because nobody can explain how the drug works.
