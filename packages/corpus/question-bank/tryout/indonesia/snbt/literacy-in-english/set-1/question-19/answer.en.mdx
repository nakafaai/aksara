Ask of each statement: does the passage give you enough to be sure of it? Two of them need a small calculation.

Statement 1 misreads the lunar method. Measuring the moon's angle from a star and checking printed tables "could give the home-port time". The moon itself works as the home-port clock, so this method didn't need a clock carried from port. No.

Statement 2 runs against the passage. The Nautical Almanac "made lunar distances practical for ordinary navigators", and chronometers "stayed costly for decades". Nothing says navigators stopped using lunar distances. No.

Statement 3 needs the Act's limit in time. Earth turns <InlineMath math="15" /> degrees an hour, so a degree takes <InlineMath math="4" /> minutes and half a degree takes <InlineMath math="2" /> minutes, or <InlineMath math="120" /> seconds. A twentieth of that is <InlineMath math="6" /> seconds, and H4 lost "only about <InlineMath math="5" /> seconds". Yes.

Statement 4 joins the limit to the size of a degree. The limit is the same angle everywhere, half a degree. But a degree spans about <InlineMath math="111" /> kilometres at the equator and **shrinks towards the poles**, so the same half degree covers more kilometres near the equator. Yes.

The trap in statement 4 is thinking a fixed limit means a fixed distance. The limit is fixed in degrees, not in kilometres.
