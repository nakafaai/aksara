For most of history, loading a ship was slow and labour-intensive. Goods arrived at the docks in barrels, sacks, crates, and bales of every size, and gangs of dockworkers carried or hoisted each piece into the hold, where it had to be fitted together like an enormous puzzle. A cargo ship could spend as much time in port being loaded and unloaded as it spent at sea. Theft was common, and damage more so.

The man usually credited with changing this was Malcolm McLean, an American trucking businessman. In 1956 he sent a converted tanker, the *Ideal X*, from Newark to Houston carrying 58 metal boxes that could be lifted straight off the deck and onto truck trailers. McLean's insight was not the box itself. Containers of various kinds had been tried before. It was the idea that the box, rather than the goods inside it, should be the unit that ships, trucks, trains, and cranes were all designed to handle.

That idea worked only if the boxes were alike. In the late 1950s, companies used containers of different lengths and with different corner fittings, so a crane built for one firm's boxes could not lift another's. Over the following decade, after long negotiations, international standards fixed a small set of sizes and a common corner fitting. The twenty-foot box became so widespread that the capacity of ports and ships is still measured in "twenty-foot equivalent units".

The consequences reached far beyond the docks. Because loading became faster and cheaper, the cost of shipping a product across an ocean shrank to a small fraction of its price. Manufacturers could place factories wherever labour and materials were cheapest and ship parts back and forth during production. Ports that invested in giant cranes and deep berths boomed, while many old harbours in city centres, with no room to expand, lost their trade. Dock work, once one of the largest sources of jobs in port cities, shrank dramatically.

Historians therefore caution against describing the container simply as an invention. Its effects depended on agreements, investments, and the decisions of governments, unions, and companies over many years. The box was simple; the system built around it was not.
