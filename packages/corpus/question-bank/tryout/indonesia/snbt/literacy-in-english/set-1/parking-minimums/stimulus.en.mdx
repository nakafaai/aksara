In many cities, zoning codes require every new building to come with a fixed number of parking spaces: perhaps two for each apartment, or one for every few hundred square feet of shop floor. These rules, known as parking minimums, were introduced with good intentions. Planners feared that without them, drivers would circle the block in search of a space, clogging streets and frustrating residents. Decades later, however, the case for scrapping most of these requirements has become hard to ignore.

The first problem is cost. Parking is expensive to build, especially in underground garages, where a single space can cost as much as a small car. Developers pass that cost on to tenants and buyers, including those who do not own a car at all. In effect, minimums require households without cars to subsidise those with them.

The second problem is land. A space for one car, together with the lanes needed to reach it, takes up more room than most people imagine. When every building must supply its own parking, shops end up separated by asphalt, distances grow, and walking or cycling becomes less practical. The rule meant to accommodate cars thereby increases the need for them.

Critics of reform warn that removing minimums will leave neighbourhoods short of parking. But abolishing a requirement is not the same as banning parking. Developers would still build spaces where buyers want them; they would simply no longer be forced to build a number chosen by a formula, often copied from suburbs with very different travel habits. Where street parking becomes scarce, a city can respond with resident permits or pricing rather than by mandating more garages.

Several cities that have relaxed or removed their minimums report that some new housing projects included fewer spaces than the old rules would have required, and that some projects which had been financially unworkable went ahead. These early results do not prove that reform suits every place. A town with little public transport may find that most buyers still want two spaces per home. That, however, is precisely the point: the number of spaces should reflect local demand, not a regulation written for someone else's town.
