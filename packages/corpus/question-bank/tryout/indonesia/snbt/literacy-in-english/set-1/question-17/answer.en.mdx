To weaken the author, you need a finding that makes the Board's demand look **unfair rather than sensible**. The author defends the demand by its purpose: a second trial, an account of the workings and copies by others, because "a method only one man could build was no method for a navy that needed hundreds". The demand is reasonable only if the Board applied it as a real standard.

So imagine, as the question allows, that the Board had earlier accepted a rival's method after one voyage, with no account and no copies. Then the Board **asked of Harrison what it never asked of others**. That double standard makes Harrison's charge of prejudice much more believable and cuts the ground from under "not unreasonable".

- A costly, slow copy shows that copying was hard, which is exactly why a navy needing hundreds had reason to worry. It supports the author.
- A weaker second trial shows why one voyage wasn't enough, so it supports asking for a second trial.
- Cook's crew using lunar sightings fits the passage's point that ships used both methods. It says nothing about whether the demand was fair.
- Harrison's refusal to explain his clocks makes the demand for "a full account of how the watch worked" look even more sensible.

The finding that most weakens the author's argument is that the Board had earlier accepted a rival's method after a single voyage, without the account or the copies it demanded from Harrison.
