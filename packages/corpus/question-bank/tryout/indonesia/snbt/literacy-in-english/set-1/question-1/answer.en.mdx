# Asked

# Solution

Follow the author's reasoning across paragraphs <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />: the author exposes the **hidden assumption about comparing signals** while refusing to declare how rich or poor the shrimp's colour world actually is.

> The author undercuts the assumption that mantis shrimp combine receptor signals like humans, while leaving open how rich their colour world really is.

## Tracing the author stance across paragraphs

- Paragraph <InlineMath math="2" /> identifies the hidden premise: the popular claim <Highlight>quietly assumes that the shrimp uses its receptors the way we use ours</Highlight>.
- Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> shows the shrimp failing at fine wavelength gaps (<InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="25" /> nm), hitting that opponent-coding prediction hard.
- Paragraphs <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="5" /> limit the claim: a poor score <Highlight>does not show that the shrimp sees colour badly in every sense</Highlight>, and receptor counts alone cannot tell us how many colours an animal sees.

## Why the other options fail

- **Saying it perceives fewer colours** turns poor discrimination into a poor colour world, which paragraph <InlineMath math="4" /> explicitly refuses to do.
- **Claiming the extra receptors serve mainly ultraviolet** introduces an idea never stated in the passage.
- **Saying tests suggest opponent coding for most colours** reverses what the experiment actually indicates.
- **Blaming prematureness solely on testing one species** misplaces a limitation of the team's hypothesis onto the author's central objection about counting receptors.

# Answer

The author's position is that **It assumes the shrimp combines receptor signals as people do, and the tests undercut that without showing how rich its colour world is**.
