**Text 1**

When Westbrook School announced last spring that it would stop setting homework for pupils under <InlineMath math="11" /> and limit it to <InlineMath math="40" /> minutes a night for everyone else, the reaction at the school gate was close to relief. As a parent governor, I had heard for years that evenings in many homes had turned into a nightly negotiation, with worksheets finished at the kitchen table by tired children and, too often, by their parents.

The school did not act on feelings alone. Before changing the rule, it ran a pilot for a single term in which Years 3 to 6 received no homework except reading. Families in the pilot reported fewer arguments about schoolwork, and the share of pilot children who said they usually went to bed after nine o'clock fell from <InlineMath math="34" /> percent to <InlineMath math="21" /> percent. Most tellingly, the pupils' end-of-term scores in reading and mathematics were no lower than those of the same year groups the year before.

That last result should settle the debate. If removing homework costs nothing in attainment while giving children back their evenings, then the <InlineMath math="40" />-minute limit for older pupils is, if anything, too generous. Homework has become a tax that families pay for a school's anxiety about being seen as rigorous, and like many taxes it falls hardest on those least able to pay: children without a quiet room or a desk, or without a parent home before eight. Westbrook has made a brave start. It, and schools like it, should finish the job and drop homework at every age.

**Text 2**

I support most of what Westbrook has done, and for the youngest pupils I would go as far as the pilot did. Studies that compare children who do more homework with children who do less tend to find only a weak link with achievement in primary school and a clearly stronger one among older secondary pupils. Such studies cannot by themselves show that homework causes the difference, since keen students may simply do more of it, but the pattern by age is consistent enough to take seriously.

That is why I am uneasy when the pilot is used as a case for abolition at every age. It involved only Years 3 to 6, the group in which little effect was expected in the first place, and it lasted a single term. Scores that were "no lower" than last year's also tell us less than they seem to. A different group of children, sitting tests that count for nothing, makes a weak comparison, and a small loss could easily hide inside it.

Some colleagues worry in the opposite direction and argue that any limit teaches pupils that learning stops when a timer rings. I don't share that worry. A limit on minutes is a sensible guard against excess. What it cannot tell us is which minutes are worth having. Spending <InlineMath math="20" /> minutes recalling last week's algebra without notes may do more than an hour of copying out definitions. For older pupils facing exams, scrapping such practice altogether could hurt most those who have the least help at home to make up for it. With older pupils, the useful question is what kind of homework earns its place, and the answer will rarely be "none".
