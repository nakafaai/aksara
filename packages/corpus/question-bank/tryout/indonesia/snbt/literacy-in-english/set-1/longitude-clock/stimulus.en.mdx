At sea in the early eighteenth century, finding latitude was routine. A navigator measured the height of the sun at noon, or of the Pole Star at night, and worked out how far north or south the ship lay. Longitude was another matter, because the sky offers no such marker for east and west. The answer had been known in principle for a long time. Earth turns through <InlineMath math="360" /> degrees in <InlineMath math="24" /> hours, or <InlineMath math="15" /> degrees every hour, so the difference between local time, which a navigator could find from the sun, and the time at the home port at that same moment gives the difference in longitude. What was missing was a way to know the home-port time in the middle of an ocean. A degree of longitude spans about <InlineMath math="111" /> kilometres at the equator and shrinks towards the poles, where the meridians meet, so an error of a few minutes in the home-port time could put a ship many kilometres from where its crew believed it to be.

In 1714 the British Parliament passed the Longitude Act, offering up to <InlineMath math="\pounds 20{,}000" /> for a method that fixed a ship's longitude to within half a degree on a voyage to the West Indies. The Act did not say how this should be done. Astronomers favoured lunar distances: the moon moves steadily against the background stars, so measuring its angle from a bright star and comparing the result with printed tables could give the home-port time. The method needed only a good instrument and the tables, but it demanded clear skies and long, error-prone arithmetic. The alternative, a clock that kept home-port time throughout the voyage, sounded simpler and seemed hopeless. Pendulum clocks that kept good time on land faltered on a rolling deck, and changes in temperature altered the metal parts and the oil that every clock relied on.

John Harrison, a carpenter by training, spent decades proving the clock route possible. After three large sea clocks, he built H4, a watch about the size of a saucer, which sailed to Jamaica in 1761. On arrival it had lost only about <InlineMath math="5" /> seconds.

The Board of Longitude, which judged claims under the Act, did not hand over the prize. It asked for a second trial, for a full account of how the watch worked, and for copies made by other craftsmen, on the ground that a method only one man could build was no method for a navy that needed hundreds. Harrison saw prejudice, and pointed out that Nevil Maskelyne, an astronomer who championed lunar distances, sat on the Board. He received partial payments, and Parliament granted him a separate reward in 1773, but the Board never declared that he had won.

It is tempting to tell this as the story of a lone genius cheated by a jealous establishment. Yet the Board's demand was not unreasonable, and events partly bore it out. Larcum Kendall's copy of H4 went with James Cook on his second voyage, and Cook praised it, which showed that the design could work beyond its maker's hands. Even so, chronometers stayed costly for decades, and the Nautical Almanac, which Maskelyne launched in the 1760s, made lunar distances practical for ordinary navigators. Many ships came to use both methods, taking lunar sightings to check whether their chronometers had drifted.
