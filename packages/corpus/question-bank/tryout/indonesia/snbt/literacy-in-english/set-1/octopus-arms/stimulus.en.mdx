An octopus has roughly 500 million neurons, far more than most other invertebrates. What makes the animal unusual, however, is not the total but where those neurons sit. More than half of them are located not in the central brain but in the eight arms, where dense cords of nerve cells run the length of each arm and connect to the hundreds of suckers lining its underside.

This arrangement allows a surprising degree of local control. In laboratory studies, an arm that has been separated from the body can still produce a reaching movement when it is stimulated, and severed arms continue to grip objects that touch their suckers. Such behaviour suggests that much of the detailed work of reaching, bending, and grasping is computed within the arm itself rather than dictated move by move from the brain.

Why would evolution favour such a design? One explanation lies in the mechanics of a boneless body. A human arm has a limited number of joints, so the brain needs to coordinate only a few angles to place a hand in space. An octopus arm, by contrast, can bend at any point along its length and twist in any direction. Planning every possible configuration centrally would demand enormous processing capacity. Delegating routine decisions to the arms reduces that burden: the brain can issue a broad command, such as "reach there," and leave the arm to work out the details.

The suckers add a further layer. Each contains receptors for touch and for chemicals, so an octopus effectively tastes what it touches. Some researchers suspect that an arm probing a crevice can begin to respond to prey it detects before the central brain becomes involved, much as a person may jerk a hand away from a hot stove before consciously feeling pain.

None of this means that the arms behave like independent animals. Octopuses solve mazes, open jars, and appear to recognise individual human keepers, tasks that clearly require centralised learning. The emerging picture is one of cooperation between levels: the brain sets goals and combines information from the eyes, while the arms handle much of the execution. Some engineers designing soft robots have taken note, arguing that flexible machines may work best when control is spread across the body rather than concentrated in a single processor.
