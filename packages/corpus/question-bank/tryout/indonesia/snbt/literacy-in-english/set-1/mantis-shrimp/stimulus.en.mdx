Few animals have been praised as extravagantly as the mantis shrimp. Some shallow-water species carry up to <InlineMath math="16" /> kinds of light-sensitive cell in each eye, and <InlineMath math="12" /> of these are colour receptors, each tuned to its own narrow band of wavelengths between deep ultraviolet, at about <InlineMath math="300" /> nanometres, and far red, at about <InlineMath math="720" /> nanometres. Human eyes manage with <InlineMath math="3" /> kinds of colour receptor. From that contrast, a conclusion has spread through web comics and nature programmes almost unchallenged: an animal with so many more colour channels must see a world of colours that we cannot begin to imagine.

That conclusion quietly assumes that the shrimp uses its receptors the way we use ours. Human colour vision does not read a hue off any single receptor. The brain compares the outputs of receptors whose sensitivities overlap, so that a light which excites one kind of cone slightly more than its neighbour is seen as a slightly different shade. This comparison, known as opponent coding, is what lets people tell apart lights that differ only a little in wavelength, and on this model every extra channel should make the comparisons finer still.

In 2014, Hanne Thoen and her colleagues at the University of Queensland published a direct test of that prediction. They trained mantis shrimp of the species *Haptosquilla trispinosa* to strike at whichever of two optical fibres glowed in a rewarded colour, then moved the other fibre's colour closer and closer in wavelength until the animals could no longer pick the right one. The results were poor. Early findings reported at a conference put the shrimp's limit at a gap of about <InlineMath math="15" /> nanometres, while people, in the parts of the spectrum where they are most sensitive, can separate lights only <InlineMath math="1" /> or <InlineMath math="2" /> nanometres apart. Honeybees and butterflies, which have far fewer receptor types than the shrimp, also did better.

A low score of this kind does not show that the shrimp sees colour badly in every sense. What it shows is that the animal is not doing what the opponent model predicted. The team's proposal is that it largely skips the comparing. On this view, the <InlineMath math="12" /> receptor types work less like the overlapping cones of a human eye than like a row of tripwires: each one reports only that light in its own band has arrived, and the animal recognises a colour from which wire has been tripped, without weighing one signal against another. Because the colour receptors sit in a narrow strip across the middle of each eye, the shrimp would also need to sweep its eyes across a scene to build up a picture, and the researchers did observe such scanning movements. A system like this would give up fine distinctions in exchange for speed, a trade that may suit a predator on a crowded reef, where a flash of colour from a rival or from prey has to be read before it is gone.

The proposal remains a hypothesis, and the tests behind it have limits that its admirers sometimes pass over. They used one species, a handful of target colours, and single wavelengths shone into a tank, not the mixed colours of coral, fish and weed that the animal actually meets. Nor do they exclude a duller explanation: an animal asked to punch a glowing fibre for a snack may simply stop trying once the task gets hard, which would make its eyes look worse than they are. What the experiments do undercut is the inference with which the popular story began. A count of receptors, however striking, cannot by itself tell us how many colours an animal sees, because that depends on what the brain does with the signals, and that is exactly the part no count can reveal.
