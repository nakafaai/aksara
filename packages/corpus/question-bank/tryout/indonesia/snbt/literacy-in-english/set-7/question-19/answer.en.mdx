# Given

Even after nineteen umbrellas return with caring notes and neighbours visit to check on Mrs Okonjo, the father remarks that a sign would have worked faster. The narrator closes by saying she does not think he **ever understood what the umbrellas had been for**.

# Asked

Why does the narrator believe her father never understood what the umbrellas had been for?

# Solution

> My father said the sign would have worked faster. I don't think he ever understood what the umbrellas had been for.

Contrast the **two standards of success** running through the whole story: the father's ledger of returned objects versus Mrs Okonjo's quiet investment in neighbourly ties.

## Contrasting the father and Mrs Okonjo

The father measures the stand by cost and recovery: he calls it a <Highlight>leak</Highlight> and still thinks a reminder sign <Highlight>would have worked faster</Highlight> to bring umbrellas back.

Mrs Okonjo never puts up the sign, reads every note carefully, hands the umbrellas back out to visitors, and feels she <Highlight>had all she needed</Highlight> because lending gave her **genuine ties with her neighbours**.

The distractors miss one side of this **central contrast**. Arguing merely about hospital timing still judges by returns, and Mrs Okonjo was not saving money for herself because none of the umbrellas were hers.

Simply keeping the street dry cannot explain the notes, visits, and renewed giving, and it was the father, not the narrator, who claimed the sign would have been faster.

# Answer

The narrator thinks this **because he judged the lending by how many umbrellas came back, while for Mrs Okonjo the point was the ties the lending built with her neighbours**.
