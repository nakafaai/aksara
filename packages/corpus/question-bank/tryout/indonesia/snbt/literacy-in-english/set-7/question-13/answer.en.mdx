# Given

Comparing **injuries per million kilometres cycled** gives <InlineMath math="6" /> for Eastvale and <InlineMath math="3" /> for Riverton, which suggests that each kilometre cycled in Riverton is safer.

# Asked

Which fact, if true, would most weaken the conclusion that a kilometre cycled in Riverton is safer than one in Eastvale?

# Solution

> Its headline read "Eastvale twice as safe for cyclists as Riverton", because Eastvale recorded <InlineMath math="60" /> cycling injuries and Riverton <InlineMath math="120" />.

A rate comparison between two cities breaks down if **both cities do not count the same injuries** in the numerator.

## Checking measurement bias

If Riverton counts only <Highlight>hospital-treated injuries</Highlight> while Eastvale also counts milder injuries treated by <Highlight>family doctors</Highlight>, Eastvale's numerator is inflated by broader reporting. Part or all of the gap between <InlineMath math="6" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> could come from different counting rules rather than safer roads in Riverton.

The other options fail to weaken the **per-kilometre comparison**. Riding mostly on separated lanes supports Riverton's safety rather than weakening it. Population size affects total injuries, not injuries per kilometre. Riding four times as far is already divided out inside Riverton's <InlineMath math="120 \div 40 = 3" /> rate, and bicycle ownership says nothing about injury risk per kilometre ridden.

# Answer

The fact that would most weaken the conclusion is that **Riverton records only cycling injuries treated in hospital, while Eastvale also records injuries treated by family doctors**.
