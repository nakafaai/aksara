**Text 1**

Every autumn I watch the same pattern. The lecture hall is full in the first week and half empty by the fifth, and by exam time the students I stopped seeing are the ones asking for extensions. I now think universities should make attendance at lectures a condition of sitting the final exam: present at <InlineMath math="80\%" /> of sessions, with documented exceptions for illness, caring duties and paid work.

The evidence on attendance is unusually consistent. A 2010 review that pooled the results of dozens of studies of college students found that class attendance predicted grades more strongly than admissions test scores or high school grades did. If attendance predicts grades better than anything else we measure, a rule that raises attendance is the cheapest lever we have.

The usual reply is that recordings make the room unnecessary. Last year I asked the students in my course who had said they would catch up from the recordings whether they had done so. Fewer than half had watched a single lecture to the end. A recording is a promise to learn later, and later rarely comes.

I do not claim that a register turns a bored student into a curious one. A student can sit in the third row and think about lunch. But skipping is the first and easiest step in falling behind, and a rule removes that step at almost no cost. Students who are present can at least be reached, while students who are absent cannot. Freedom matters, but first-year students are not yet free in any useful sense, because they do not know what they are missing.

**Text 2**

I agree with my colleague about one thing: students who come to lectures tend to do better. The review she cites is careful work, and I do not doubt its numbers. What it describes, though, are students who chose to come. Those students differ from absentees in ways no register can change. They tend to be more motivated, they hold fewer paid jobs, and they found the course useful enough to return. The link tells us how attenders differ from absentees. It cannot tell us what happens when we march the absentees in.

My department tried. In the year before we introduced a rule, about <InlineMath math="55\%" /> of registered students came to a typical lecture. In the year after, about <InlineMath math="85\%" /> did. The average mark on the final exam stayed within a point of where it had been. Had our marks risen after the rule, I would not have counted that as proof either, since the exam was rewritten that same year and the two intakes arrived with different school grades. One department's before-and-after comparison can settle very little.

A signature on a sheet is not attention, and my colleague concedes as much. What a rule does buy is a full room of people who would rather be elsewhere, and it takes away the empty seats that used to tell a lecturer something was wrong.

I would make one exception. Attendance should be required in small first-year tutorials, where an absence is noticed at once and a struggling student can be helped early. For lectures, the better policy is to make the hour worth turning up for.
