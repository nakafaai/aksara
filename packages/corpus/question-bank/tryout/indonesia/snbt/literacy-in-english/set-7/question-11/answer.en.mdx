# Given

The table gives **cycling injuries and distance cycled** in two cities last year: Eastvale had <InlineMath math="60" /> injuries across <InlineMath math="10" /> million kilometres cycled, while Riverton had <InlineMath math="120" /> injuries across <InlineMath math="40" /> million kilometres cycled.

# Asked

How many injuries per million kilometres cycled did each city record, and what does that comparison show?

# Solution

> Traffic engineers usually compare the safety of cycling in different places by the number of injuries per million kilometres cycled.

Compute each city's **injury rate per million kilometres**, then identify what that rate can and cannot prove.

## Comparing injury rates per kilometre

Divide total cycling injuries by millions of kilometres cycled:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Eastvale} &= \frac{60}{10} = 6 \text{ injuries per million km} \\ \text{Riverton} &= \frac{120}{40} = 3 \text{ injuries per million km} \end{aligned}" />

| City | Injuries | Million km cycled | Injuries per million km |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Eastvale | <InlineMath math="60" /> | <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| Riverton | <InlineMath math="120" /> | <InlineMath math="40" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |

Since <InlineMath math="3" /> is half of <InlineMath math="6" />, a kilometre cycled in Riverton carried **half the risk** of a kilometre cycled in Eastvale. A per-kilometre rate does not tell us risk per resident (where Eastvale is lower, <InlineMath math="3" /> vs <InlineMath math="4" /> per <InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> residents), and observational rates alone cannot prove that Riverton's bike lanes caused the lower risk.

# Answer

The rates are **Eastvale <InlineMath math="6" /> and Riverton <InlineMath math="3" />, so a kilometre cycled in Riverton carried half the risk of one in Eastvale**.
