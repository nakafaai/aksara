# Given

Suppose Eastvale builds bike lanes and the next year records **<InlineMath math="20" /> million kilometres cycled** and **<InlineMath math="80" /> injuries** (with <InlineMath math="200{,}000" /> residents), while Eastvale previously had <InlineMath math="60" /> injuries over <InlineMath math="10" /> million km and Riverton has <InlineMath math="3" /> injuries per million km and about <InlineMath math="133" /> km per resident.

# Asked

Which statements would be true in that hypothetical next year?

# Solution

> Traffic engineers usually compare the safety of cycling in different places by the number of injuries per million kilometres cycled.

Compute Eastvale's **new injury rate** and **new distance per resident** first, then compare both numbers with Eastvale's old values and Riverton's figures.

## Comparing the new Eastvale year to Riverton

Divide Eastvale's new injuries by its new distance, and divide its new distance by its residents to find both **updated figures**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{New Eastvale injury rate} &= \frac{80}{20} = 4 \text{ injuries per million km} \\ \text{New Eastvale km per resident} &= \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{200{,}000} = 100 \text{ km per resident} \end{aligned}" />

| Statement | Numerical comparison | True |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate falls from <InlineMath math="6" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> | Old <InlineMath math="6" /> down to new <InlineMath math="4" /> | **Yes** |
| Rate stays higher than Riverton's | <InlineMath math="4 > 3" /> | **Yes** |
| Eastvale is now safer than Riverton | Higher injury rate means less safe (<InlineMath math="4 > 3" />) | **No** |
| Total injuries rise even though rate fell | Injuries rise <InlineMath math="60 \to 80" /> while rate drops <InlineMath math="6 \to 4" /> | **Yes** |
| Eastvale residents cycle more per person than Riverton | <InlineMath math="100" /> km is still below Riverton's <InlineMath math="133" /> km | **No** |

Notice the key **rate-versus-total insight**: doubling the distance cycled causes raw injuries to climb from <InlineMath math="60" /> to <InlineMath math="80" /> even as each individual kilometre becomes safer (<InlineMath math="6" /> down to <InlineMath math="4" />).

# Answer

The statements that would be true are:

- Eastvale's rate would fall from <InlineMath math="6" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> injuries per million kilometres.
- Eastvale's rate would still be higher than Riverton's.
- Eastvale's number of injuries would rise even though its rate fell.
