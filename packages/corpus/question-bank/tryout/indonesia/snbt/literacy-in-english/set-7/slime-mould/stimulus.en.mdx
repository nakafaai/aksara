The yellow slime mould *Physarum polycephalum* looks like a spill of mustard on a forest floor. It is not a plant, an animal, or a fungus, and it has no brain or nerves. For much of its life it is a single enormous cell containing millions of nuclei, which spreads out in a network of branching tubes as it searches for food.

In 2000, Japanese researchers placed a slime mould in a small plastic maze, with pieces of food at the entrance and the exit. Within hours, the organism had pulled back from the dead ends and left a single thick tube along the shortest route between the two food sources. Ten years later, a team arranged oat flakes in the pattern of the cities around Tokyo. The slime mould grew a network linking them that resembled the region's railway system in its efficiency and in how well it kept working when one link was cut.

How does a brainless cell manage this? Part of the answer lies in its tubes. Tubes that carry more nutrients thicken, while those that carry little shrink and disappear. Over time, this simple rule strengthens efficient routes and removes wasteful ones, without any part of the organism "knowing" the overall layout.

The slime mould also leaves a trail of slime wherever it has been, and it tends to avoid moving onto that trail. In experiments, this helps it avoid searching the same empty area twice. Some researchers describe the trail as an external memory: information stored in the environment rather than inside the organism.

These findings are sometimes reported as if slime moulds were planning railways. They are not. The organism is simply following rules that happen to produce efficient networks. That is exactly why engineers find it interesting: if simple local rules can produce a good network, the same rules might help design networks for computers or traffic.
