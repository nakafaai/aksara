# Given

The passage presents the **mood explanation**: the original gain came from feeling briefly more alert and cheerful, not from anything unique to Mozart's music.

# Asked

Which hypothetical finding would most weaken the explanation that mood, rather than Mozart, produced the effect?

# Solution

> Students who listened to lively, upbeat music did better on the spatial task than students who listened to slow, sad music, whether or not the lively piece was by Mozart.

To undermine "mood, not Mozart", a finding must **hold mood equal** while showing that Mozart still outperforms another equally lively composer.

## Isolating Mozart from mood

If students who heard Mozart outperformed students who heard an equally lively piece by another composer even when both groups felt <Highlight>equally alert and cheerful</Highlight>, mood can no longer explain the score gap. Something specific to Mozart would still be producing an extra gain.

The other choices do not weaken the **mood hypothesis**. Beating silence is the <InlineMath math="1993" /> result that mood already explains. Outperforming coffee still leaves room for Mozart to cheer listeners up more than coffee. Lively Mozart beating slow, sad Mozart directly supports the mood hypothesis, and a small average effect does not identify the cause.

# Answer

The finding that would most weaken the explanation is that **students who heard Mozart did better than students who heard an equally lively piece by another composer, even though both groups felt equally alert and cheerful**.
