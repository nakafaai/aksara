To weaken "mood, not Mozart", you need a result where **mood is held equal** and Mozart still makes a difference.

The finding that both groups felt equally alert and cheerful, yet the Mozart group still did better, does exactly that. Both groups heard lively music, and both "felt equally alert and cheerful", so mood can't explain a gap. If the Mozart group still did better, something about Mozart matters after all.

- Mozart beating silence is the original 1993 result. Mood explains it just as well, since music can lift mood and silence doesn't.
- Coffee was only offered as a guess ("perhaps"). And a bigger lift from Mozart than from coffee could simply mean Mozart cheered people up more, so mood isn't ruled out.
- Lively Mozart beating sad Mozart is exactly what the mood explanation predicts. The composer is the same, and only the mood differs.
- A small average effect says nothing about what causes it.

The finding that would most weaken the explanation is that students who heard Mozart did better than students who heard an equally lively piece by another composer, even though both groups felt equally alert and cheerful.
