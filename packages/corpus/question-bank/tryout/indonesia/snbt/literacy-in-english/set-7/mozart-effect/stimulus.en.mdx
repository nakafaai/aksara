In 1993 three researchers in California reported that college students who had just listened to ten minutes of a lively Mozart sonata did better on a spatial reasoning task, folding and cutting paper in their heads, than students who had sat in silence or listened to relaxation instructions. The improvement was modest, the equivalent of eight or nine points on an IQ-style scale, and it had faded within about fifteen minutes. The study involved thirty-six students.

Within a few years the finding had been retold as a claim that classical music makes babies smarter. Shops sold Mozart recordings for infants, and in 1998 the governor of one American state proposed giving a classical CD to the parents of every newborn. None of the original researchers had tested babies, and none had claimed a lasting effect.

Attempts to repeat the study gave mixed results, and a later review that pooled dozens of them found the average effect to be small. One line of work offered an explanation for why it appeared at all. Students who listened to lively, upbeat music did better on the spatial task than students who listened to slow, sad music, whether or not the lively piece was by Mozart. In another experiment, students who preferred a story read aloud did as well after hearing the story as other students did after hearing Mozart. When the researchers measured how alert and cheerful the students felt, the differences in mood accounted for the differences in scores.

If that explanation is right, the original result was real but was not about Mozart. Anything that leaves a listener briefly more alert and in a better mood, such as a favourite song, an interesting story or perhaps a cup of coffee, should help a little with a puzzle taken straight afterwards. That is a far less exciting finding, and it was never going to sell recordings for babies.
