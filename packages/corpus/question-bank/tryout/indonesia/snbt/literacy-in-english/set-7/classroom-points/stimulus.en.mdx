Walk into many classrooms today and you will find a leaderboard. Pupils earn points for finishing exercises, badges for streaks of correct answers, and a place at the top of a ranked list for the week's best effort. Supporters call this gamification, and they point out, correctly, that pupils often work harder when points are on offer. The question is what happens when the points go away.

A well-known experiment from 1973 offers a warning. Researchers gave preschool children markers and paper. Some children were told they would receive a certificate for drawing, and they did. Others drew without any promise of reward. Days later, when markers were simply left out during free play, the children who had expected the certificate spent less time drawing than the others. An activity they had once enjoyed for its own sake had become something they did for a prize.

Classroom points risk the same effect. A pupil who reads to collect badges may stop reading once the badges stop. Leaderboards add a second problem: they reward the few pupils who are already ahead, while those near the bottom learn mainly that they are behind.

None of this means games have nothing to teach schools. What makes a good game absorbing is rarely its points. It is the immediate feedback after every move, the freedom to fail and try again without penalty, and challenges that grow harder just as the player grows more skilled. These features can be built into ordinary lessons without a single badge.

Points are easy to add, and their effects show up quickly. The features that actually make games worth playing take more work to design. Schools that want pupils to keep learning after the term ends should invest in the second rather than settle for the first.
