As a teenage worker in Pittsburgh in the 1850s, Andrew Carnegie could not afford books. A local man, Colonel James Anderson, opened his private library to working boys on Saturdays, and Carnegie later said that those borrowed books shaped his life. Decades afterwards, having become one of the richest men in the world through the steel industry, Carnegie gave away much of his fortune. A large share went to public libraries.

Between 1883 and 1929, Carnegie's money paid for more than 2,500 library buildings, most of them in the United States and the rest mainly in Britain, Canada, and other English-speaking countries. Many small towns that had never had a public library received their first one this way.

Carnegie did not simply hand out buildings. A town that wanted a grant had to provide the land and promise to pay for running the library every year, usually about a tenth of the building's cost, through local taxes. Carnegie believed that a community which paid for its own library would value it and keep it open. A gift that cost the town nothing, he feared, might be neglected once the novelty wore off.

Not everyone welcomed the money. Some critics pointed out that Carnegie's fortune had been built partly on long hours and low wages in his steel mills, and a few towns refused his grants for that reason. Others objected to paying the yearly upkeep he required.

Many of the buildings still stand. Some remain libraries, while others have become museums, offices, or community centres. Their survival is partly a result of the condition that Carnegie insisted on: towns that had to keep paying for their libraries also built the habit of keeping them.
