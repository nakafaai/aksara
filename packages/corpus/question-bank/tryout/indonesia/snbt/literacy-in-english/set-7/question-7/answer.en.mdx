# Given

**Text A** praises a school phone ban and links it to calmer lessons, fewer school-hours bullying reports, and a <InlineMath math="9" />-point rise in maths pass rates. **Text B** accepts calmer lessons and supports a ban, but questions the pass-rate claim and the missing evening bullying data.

# Asked

Which of the five statements would **both writers** accept (**Yes** or **No**)?

# Solution

> I don't doubt that the school feels calmer, and keeping phones out of lessons seems sensible to me.

A statement earns **Yes** only when **both Text A and Text B** agree with it. Check each writer's position side by side.

## Comparing agreement across both texts

Verify every statement against the explicit claims of both passages.

| Statement | Text A position | Text B position | Both accept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <Highlight>lessons are calmer</Highlight> | <Highlight>I don't doubt that the school feels calmer</Highlight> | **Yes** |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | Credits the ban for the rise | <Highlight>could come from any of the three changes</Highlight> | **No** |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | Urges every school to ban phones | <Highlight>keeping phones out of lessons seems sensible</Highlight> | **Yes** |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | Does not claim evening bullying rose | Says <Highlight>nobody counted that</Highlight> | **No** |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | Does not predict a specific rise elsewhere | <Highlight>would not promise parents a nine-point rise</Highlight> | **No** |

Watch Statement <InlineMath math="4" /> carefully because it is a classic **burden-of-proof trap**: Text B asks whether bullying moved to the evening and stresses that nobody measured it, so neither writer claims it actually happened.

# Answer

- Statement <InlineMath math="1" />: **Yes**
- Statement <InlineMath math="2" />: **No**
- Statement <InlineMath math="3" />: **Yes**
- Statement <InlineMath math="4" />: **No**
- Statement <InlineMath math="5" />: **No**
