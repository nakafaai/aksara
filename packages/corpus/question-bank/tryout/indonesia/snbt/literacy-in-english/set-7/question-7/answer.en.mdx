A statement gets "Yes" only if **both writers** hold it. If only one writer says it, or neither does, the answer is "No."

**Raising attendance would raise grades.** Text 1 says a rule "is the cheapest lever we have" because attendance predicts grades. Text 2 rejects that step: the link "cannot tell us what happens when we march the absentees in." Only Text 1 holds it. No.

**Seats without attention.** Text 1: "I do not claim that a register turns a bored student into a curious one. A student can sit in the third row and think about lunch." Text 2: "A signature on a sheet is not attention, and my colleague concedes as much." Both agree. Yes.

**Recordings are a poor substitute.** Only Text 1 argues this, with its "promise to learn later" line. Text 2 never mentions recordings. No.

**First-year students should be required to attend some teaching.** This one is easy to miss. Text 1 wants lecture attendance required and says "first-year students are not yet free in any useful sense." Text 2 makes one exception: "Attendance should be required in small first-year tutorials." They pick **different kinds of teaching**, but both require first-years to attend something. Yes.

**One department's figures can show whether a rule works.** Text 2 says the opposite: "One department's before-and-after comparison can settle very little." Text 1 doesn't make this claim either. No.
