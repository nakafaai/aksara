# Given

The table supplies total injuries, millions of kilometres cycled, and population for **Eastvale** (<InlineMath math="60" />, <InlineMath math="10" /> million km, <InlineMath math="200{,}000" />) and **Riverton** (<InlineMath math="120" />, <InlineMath math="40" /> million km, <InlineMath math="300{,}000" />).

# Asked

Which conclusions can be validly drawn from the passage and the table?

# Solution

> Traffic engineers usually compare the safety of cycling in different places by the number of injuries per million kilometres cycled.

Test each candidate conclusion with **its own ratio or causal check** rather than relying on intuition.

## Checking all five candidate conclusions

Calculate the key ratios and Eastvale's projected injuries at <InlineMath math="40" /> million kilometres:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Km per resident:} &\quad \text{Riverton } \frac{40{,}000{,}000}{300{,}000} \approx 133 > \text{Eastvale } \frac{10{,}000{,}000}{200{,}000} = 50 \\ \text{Injuries per } 10{,}000 \text{ residents:} &\quad \text{Eastvale } \frac{60}{20} = 3, \quad \text{Riverton } \frac{120}{30} = 4 \\ \text{Eastvale rate over } 40 \text{ million km:} &\quad 6 \times 40 = 240 \text{ injuries} \end{aligned}" />

| Candidate conclusion | Data check | Valid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More km per Riverton resident | About <InlineMath math="133" /> km vs <InlineMath math="50" /> km | **Yes** |
| Fewer injuries per Eastvale resident, but not half | <InlineMath math="3" /> vs <InlineMath math="4" /> per <InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> (<InlineMath math="3 \neq 2" />) | **Yes** |
| Eastvale rode a quarter as far with half as many injuries | <InlineMath math="10 / 40 = 1 / 4" /> and <InlineMath math="60 / 120 = 1 / 2" /> | **Yes** |
| Eastvale over <InlineMath math="40" /> million km would have <InlineMath math="120" /> injuries | At Eastvale's rate of <InlineMath math="6" />, it gives <InlineMath math="240" />, not <InlineMath math="120" /> | **No** |
| Bike lanes encouraged more Riverton residents to cycle | Table shows correlation, not causation | **No** |

Notice why the last two choices fail **basic data reasoning**: projecting Eastvale's rate of <InlineMath math="6" /> over <InlineMath math="40" /> million kilometres gives <InlineMath math="240" /> injuries (while <InlineMath math="120" /> uses Riverton's rate of <InlineMath math="3" />), and the table cannot prove why Riverton residents cycled further.

# Answer

The conclusions that can be drawn from the passage and the table are:

- On average, each Riverton resident cycled more kilometres than each Eastvale resident.
- Measured per resident, Eastvale had fewer injuries, but not half as many.
- Eastvale's cyclists rode a quarter as far as Riverton's in total and had half as many injuries.
