Mrs Okonjo kept a stand by her front door with a dozen umbrellas in it, none of them hers. She lent them to anyone caught in the rain on our street: the postman, the boy from the chip shop, strangers waiting for the bus. Hardly any came back. Every few months she bought more at the market, cheap black ones that turned inside out in the first strong wind.

My father called it a leak. "She is paying for half the street to stay dry," he said, and when I offered to put up a sign asking people to return them, he said it was a good idea. Mrs Okonjo thanked me and said she would think about it. The sign never went up.

That winter she had a fall and spent three weeks in hospital. When she came home, there were nineteen umbrellas in the stand. People had brought them back while she was away, some with notes tied to the handles. One note said only, "Sorry it took so long. Hope you're mending." Another, in a child's writing, was from the boy at the chip shop.

She read every note at the kitchen table, and then she gave the umbrellas out again, one by one, to the people who came to the door to ask how she was. She never bought another umbrella. She said she had all she needed.

My father said the sign would have worked faster. I don't think he ever understood what the umbrellas had been for.
