New Caledonian crows live only on the islands of New Caledonia in the southwest Pacific, and they are the only animals apart from humans known to make hooked tools in the wild. In 1996 the biologist Gavin Hunt described two kinds: twigs trimmed into hooks, and strips cut in steps from the barbed edges of pandanus leaves. Nobody now doubts that these birds make tools. The open question is whether a crow making one is thinking ahead.

For years the most quoted answer came from a captive crow at Oxford named Betty. In a study published in 2002, she faced a small bucket of food at the bottom of a vertical tube, with only a straight piece of wire to work with. She bent the wire into a hook and lifted the bucket out, and she did so again in trial after trial, so the bending was no accident. Because bending was thought to be new to the species, the result was read as invention: a bird that grasped what the task required and built something it had never needed before. Few objects in the study of animal minds have been asked to carry as much as that short length of wire.

Later work has taken some of that weight off. A 2016 study led by Christian Rutz concluded that bending tools is part of the species' natural behaviour. If so, Betty may have applied a familiar routine to an unfamiliar material rather than worked out a new solution. None of this shows that she was not clever. It shows that the part of the case that rested on novelty was weaker than it looked. A laboratory study of <InlineMath math="17" /> captive crows, published in 2021, found that some of them did bend tools but that trial and error explained most of their progress.

Other work explains why hooks matter to the birds at all. In controlled tests with wild-caught crows, the deeper a tool's hook, the faster the birds pulled bait out of holes in logs. A payoff like this tells us why a habit of hook making would persist in a population. It tells us nothing about what an individual bird pictures before it starts.

In 2018 researchers gave <InlineMath math="8" /> wild-caught crows a puzzle in which food sat out of reach and no single piece on offer was long enough to get it. Of these birds, <InlineMath math="4" /> fitted pieces together into a tool long enough to reach the food, and one of them built tools from <InlineMath math="3" /> and even <InlineMath math="4" /> pieces. The team was careful to add that the mental processes behind these feats had not been established.

What, then, would count as evidence of planning? Some researchers treat a fast success on a first attempt as a sign that the animal simulated the outcome in advance. Speed on its own is weak evidence, since a bird that has learned general rules, such as "longer reaches farther", can succeed quickly without imagining anything. A stronger case would need three things at once: an action that is not part of the species' ordinary behaviour, materials whose properties the bird has had no chance to learn by handling them, and the right choice made before the first attempt. Studies that meet all three are rare, and Betty's, once taken to be one of them, now fails the first.
