# Given

The passage separates **verified elements of Gage's record** (surviving the iron rod, Harlow's short <InlineMath math="1868" /> note quoting "no longer Gage") from embellishments added by later textbooks.

# Asked

Which statements accurately describe ways the famous story departed from the evidence?

# Solution

> But Harlow's description is short, and many of the later details, such as fights and heavy drinking, appear in no account written by anyone who knew him.

Separate details that **invented or stretched the record** from facts that genuinely come from the historical sources.

## Separating departures from documented facts

| Statement | Supported by record | Departure from evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Added fights and heavy drinking | No (<Highlight>appear in no account written by anyone who knew him</Highlight>) | **Yes** |
| Treated early changes as lifelong | No (<Highlight>stretched to fit the rest of his life</Highlight>) | **Yes** |
| Claimed he survived an iron rod through his head | Yes (confirmed <InlineMath math="1848" /> event) | **No** |
| Claimed he could never hold a job again | No (drove a stagecoach in Chile for years) | **Yes** |
| Quoted Harlow's "no longer Gage" | Yes (written by Harlow in <InlineMath math="1868" />) | **No** |

Only the three **unsupported or contradicted claims** count as departures from the evidence. Surviving the iron rod and quoting Harlow's actual phrase reflect the genuine historical record.

# Answer

**The ways the famous story departed from the evidence are**:

- It included details, such as fights and heavy drinking, that appear in no account by anyone who knew him.
- It treated changes seen soon after the accident as lasting for the rest of his life.
- It said he could never hold a job again, although he later worked for years as a coach driver in Chile.
