Carbon dioxide that dissolves in seawater does not simply sit there. It reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions, and many of those ions then combine with carbonate ions already in the water to form bicarbonate. The sea therefore becomes slightly more acidic and, less obviously, poorer in carbonate. Since the start of the industrial era, the average pH of surface seawater has fallen by about <InlineMath math="0.1" /> units. Some headlines have turned this into a forecast that the oceans will soon support no shelled life at all.

The lost carbonate is what worries biologists most. Corals, oysters, mussels, sea urchins and the tiny swimming snails called pteropods all build shells or skeletons from calcium carbonate. Chemists measure how easy that is with the saturation state. When it is above <InlineMath math="1" />, the water holds more than enough carbonate for shell material to stay solid. When it falls below <InlineMath math="1" />, unprotected shell material tends to dissolve. Shells are not equally at risk. Aragonite, the form used by corals and pteropods, dissolves more readily than calcite, and many molluscs, oysters among them, build their first larval shell from aragonite before switching largely to calcite as adults.

Much of the evidence comes from laboratory tanks. A typical experiment raises the carbon dioxide in the water to a level expected late in this century and holds it there for a few weeks. Under these conditions, many shell-builders grow more slowly, and some larvae develop deformed shells. Yet results vary. Some species grow normally, a few grow faster, and two experiments on the same species have sometimes disagreed.

Some commentators read this disagreement as evidence that the danger has been exaggerated. That reading confuses variation with safety. A tank differs from the sea in ways that cut both ways. The change in a tank is abrupt, whereas the real ocean changes over decades, giving populations time to acclimatise and, over many generations, perhaps to adapt. In some experiments, however, the animals are fed generously, and a well-fed animal can spend more energy controlling the chemistry of the fluid in which its shell forms. A tank also usually changes one factor at a time, while animals in the sea may face warmer water and falling oxygen as well. Coastal water also swings between day and night as algae take up carbon dioxide in daylight and release it in darkness, so an animal that copes with a steady level in a tank may still suffer during the worst hours of a real night.

To study whole communities, researchers visit volcanic vents where carbon dioxide bubbles from the seafloor. Near such vents the water is more acidic than the surrounding sea, and divers typically find fewer corals, snails and urchins and more seagrass and fleshy algae. These sites have their own weakness. They are open to the surrounding ocean, so larvae spawned in normal water elsewhere can drift in and settle.

None of this makes the future precise. What the evidence supports is narrower than the alarm and firmer than the dismissal: as carbonate becomes scarcer, building a shell costs more energy, and species whose early life stages depend on aragonite are likely to feel that cost first. Whether a given population pays it through slower growth, fewer offspring or local disappearance will depend on food, temperature and how quickly the chemistry shifts.
