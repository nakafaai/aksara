In September 1848 a railway foreman named Phineas Gage was preparing a blast in Vermont when the charge went off early and drove an iron rod more than a metre long up through his left cheek and out of the top of his head. He survived, and within minutes he was talking. His doctor, John Harlow, cared for him for months.

Gage became one of the most famous patients in the history of brain science. For more than a century, textbooks described the accident as having turned a steady, likeable man into a rude and unreliable drifter who could never hold a job again, and they offered his case as proof that the front of the brain governs personality.

The record is thinner than that story suggests. Harlow did write, in 1868, that Gage's friends said he was "no longer Gage", that he swore freely and could not keep to his plans. But Harlow's description is short, and many of the later details, such as fights and heavy drinking, appear in no account written by anyone who knew him. Later research also turned up what happened next. Gage worked for some years as a stagecoach driver in Chile, a job that meant handling a team of horses on mountain roads to a fixed timetable, before he died in 1860.

Some researchers now suggest that Gage recovered more than the famous story allows, perhaps because the steady routine of his later work helped him relearn how to manage himself. If so, the best-known lesson drawn from his case, that the injury changed him for good, was taken from the first months after the accident and stretched to fit the rest of his life.
