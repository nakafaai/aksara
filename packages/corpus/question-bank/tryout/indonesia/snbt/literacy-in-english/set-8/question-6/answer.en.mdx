# Given

**Text A** cites an <InlineMath math="18\%" /> overall drop in cars entering the city centre to argue that the congestion charge works. **Text B** agrees with the charge but breaks down that <InlineMath math="18\%" /> figure using exempt ride-hailing cars.

# Asked

How does Text B respond to Text A?

# Solution

> They now make up a third of the cars entering the centre. That means private cars are down by about <InlineMath math="45\%" />, not <InlineMath math="18\%" />. Meanwhile ride-hailing cars spend much of their time driving empty between passengers, so average speeds in the centre have fallen almost back to where they were before the charge.

Trace Text B's **stance and argument structure**: it backs the policy itself while reinterpreting what the headline <InlineMath math="18\%" /> decline actually conceals.

## Tracing support and critique in Text B

Text B explicitly states <Highlight>I support the charge</Highlight>, yet argues that the <InlineMath math="18\%" /> total drop hides a much steeper <InlineMath math="45\%" /> fall in **private cars**. That steeper drop is masked by exempt <Highlight>ride-hailing cars</Highlight>, which drive empty between fares and have slowed centre speeds almost back to pre-charge levels.

This rules out the four distractors cleanly. Text B blames ride-hailing cars rather than Text A's new bus routes. The <InlineMath math="18\%" /> understates (not overstates) the private-car drop because ride-hailing cars are inside the count. Text B wants ride-hailing cars charged rather than private cars exempted, and it supports rather than rejects the charge.

# Answer

Text B **supports the charge, but argues that the <InlineMath math="18\%" /> hides a much larger fall in private cars, offset by exempt ride-hailing cars that have slowed traffic again**.
