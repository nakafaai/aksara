In 1325, a twenty-one-year-old from Tangier in Morocco set out on the pilgrimage to Mecca. Ibn Battuta did not return home for almost twenty-four years. By the end of his travels he had crossed North Africa, the Middle East, East Africa, Central Asia, India, and parts of Southeast Asia and China, covering a greater distance than any other known traveller of his time.

His training made this possible. Ibn Battuta had studied Islamic law, and in many Muslim communities a trained judge was valued and well paid. In Delhi he served the sultan as a judge for several years, and in the Maldives he held the same role. Wherever he went, his learning opened doors to rulers, scholars, and generous hosts.

On his way to China in the 1340s, he stopped at Samudra Pasai on the northern tip of Sumatra. He described a Muslim sultan who received him warmly, debated religious questions with scholars, and provided him with a ship for the rest of his voyage.

After he finally returned to Morocco, the sultan of Morocco asked him to record his journeys. Ibn Battuta dictated his memories to a young scholar, Ibn Juzayy, who shaped them into a book usually called the *Rihla*, meaning "the journey". Because Ibn Battuta had lost most of his notes on the way, much of the book was told from memory years after the events.

Modern historians treat the *Rihla* with both respect and caution. Many of its descriptions match other sources and remain valuable records of fourteenth-century life. Yet some passages appear to borrow from earlier travel writers, and a few journeys, such as parts of his account of China, are described so briefly or confusingly that some scholars doubt he made them as told.
