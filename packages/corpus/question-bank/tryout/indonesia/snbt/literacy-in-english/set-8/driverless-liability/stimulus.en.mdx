**Text 1**

When a person drives a car, the law has a ready answer to the question of who pays after a crash: the driver who made the mistake, or the driver's insurer. A car that drives itself breaks that answer. If the steering, braking and judgement all come from software, the person in the seat is no more in control than a passenger on a train, and the victim of a crash deserves compensation from whoever really was in control.

The fairer rule is to make the manufacturer liable whenever the self-driving system is engaged. Makers are best placed to prevent crashes, since only they can change the code and only they can read the full record the car keeps of its own decisions. They are also best placed to bear the cost: a maker can spread the expense of compensation across every car it sells, while a single owner could be ruined by one serious claim. Most importantly, a company that knows it will pay for its system's errors has a constant reason to make the system safer.

Some insurers argue that owners should still pay because they chose to buy the vehicle and to switch the system on. But choosing a product is not the same as controlling it. Nobody blames a homeowner when a newly installed lift falls because of a design fault.

The one case that needs care is the handover, when the system asks the human to take back control. A maker that designs a car to hand over with only a few seconds' warning, to a person it has encouraged to read or watch films, should not be able to shift the blame back to that person when the handover fails.

**Text 2**

Arguments for making manufacturers liable for self-driving crashes usually begin from the victim's point of view, and rightly so. Someone hit by an automated car should be paid quickly, without having to prove what went wrong inside a computer. That goal, however, points to a different rule from the one usually proposed.

If victims must claim from the maker, they face a company with far more lawyers and far more information than they have, and every claim becomes a technical dispute over logs and code. A simpler design keeps the owner's compulsory insurance as the first payer. The insurer compensates the victim at once and then, if the crash was caused by a fault in the system, recovers the money from the manufacturer. The maker still ends up paying for its defects, so its incentive to improve the software survives, but the victim does not have to wait for that argument to be settled.

Nor should the owner vanish from the picture. An owner can fail to install a safety update, ignore repeated warnings, or switch the system on in conditions it was never approved for, such as heavy snow. These are choices, and an insurance premium that rises with them gives owners a reason to make them carefully.

Finally, a rule that puts every cost on the maker from the first day could raise prices enough to slow the spread of systems that may, in some settings, already crash less often than human drivers. If that happens, fewer such cars on the road would make no one safer.
