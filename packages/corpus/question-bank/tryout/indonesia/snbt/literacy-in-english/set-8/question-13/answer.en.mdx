# Given

Only <InlineMath math="7{,}000" /> out of <InlineMath math="50{,}000" /> guests (about **one in seven**) completed the optional survey, and a manager reported that guests who had complaints were **less likely to answer** afterwards.

# Asked

Which statement best explains why <InlineMath math="92\%" /> cannot be treated as the satisfaction share of all guests?

# Solution

> One of the chain's managers pointed out that guests with a complaint usually raise it at the front desk before they leave, and that, in her experience, those guests are less likely to answer the survey afterwards.

Combine **low participation** with **non-response bias**: when most guests skip the survey and the missing guests skew unhappier, the answered sample leans toward satisfied guests.

## Explaining the sample bias

Just <InlineMath math="7{,}000" /> of <InlineMath math="50{,}000" /> guests (<InlineMath math="14\%" />, roughly one in seven) replied. Because dissatisfied guests are <Highlight>less likely to answer the survey afterwards</Highlight>, the respondents are not representative of everyone who stayed at the hotels.

The other choices contain clear flaws. Low response alone cannot prove the true number is "far below <InlineMath math="92\%" />". Front-desk complaints are excluded from online survey totals. Station's higher response rate explains weighting between hotels rather than all-guest bias, and combining Harbour (<InlineMath math="95\%" />) with Station (<InlineMath math="91.5\%" />) pulls the figure **down**, not up.

# Answer

The best explanation is that **only about one guest in seven answered, and the manager's remark suggests that unhappy guests were less likely to answer, so the answers lean towards satisfied guests**.
