Meat grown from animal cells in steel tanks, rather than taken from slaughtered animals, has moved from laboratories into a few shops and restaurants. Singapore became the first country to approve the sale of such cultivated meat in 2020, and the United States approved two chicken products in 2023. Other governments have gone the opposite way. Italy passed a law banning its production and sale in 2023, and some American states have since done the same.

Supporters of the bans give two main reasons. The first is safety: the technology is new, and no one has eaten it for decades. The second is tradition: farmers and chefs argue that meat is part of a culture and a rural economy that a factory product could undermine.

Neither reason justifies a ban. Safety is exactly what food regulators exist to judge. A cultivated product can be required to pass the same kind of review as any new food ingredient, and it can be kept off shelves if it fails. Banning the product before any review takes place does not make food safer. It only prevents the evidence from being collected.

The argument from tradition is more understandable but no stronger. Many foods that are now ordinary were once new, and consumers who value traditional meat will remain free to buy it. If a cultivated product is clearly labelled, no one will eat it by mistake.

There are fair questions about cultivated meat. It is still expensive, and it is not yet clear whether it can be produced at a large scale with less energy than farming. Those questions are best answered by allowing careful, labelled sales and studying the results. A ban answers them by refusing to ask.
