# Given

The passage concludes that the textbook lesson from Gage's case was **taken from the first months after the accident and stretched to fit the rest of his life**, ignoring his later years of demanding work.

# Asked

Which scenario illustrates a lesson "stretched" in the exact same way?

# Solution

> If so, the best-known lesson drawn from his case, that the injury changed him for good, was taken from the first months after the accident and stretched to fit the rest of his life.

A parallel case requires **three matching ingredients**: an early short-term observation, a textbook claim that the effect is permanent, and later evidence of functioning that contradicts permanence.

## Matching all three parts of the stretch

When a patient forgets new names <Highlight>in the first weeks after an operation</Highlight>, and a textbook labels that deficit **permanent** even though the patient later returns to a demanding job, the textbook repeats the exact error made in Gage's story.

Each other option misses one of the **three parts**. Forgetting names for years genuinely supports calling it permanent. Calling it temporary matches the return to work. Confirming permanence with later job tests grounds the claim in evidence, and a doctor's short contemporary note simply records the early weeks without stretching them.

# Answer

The matching case is: **a patient forgets new names in the first weeks after an operation, and a textbook describes this as permanent although the patient later returns to a demanding job**.
