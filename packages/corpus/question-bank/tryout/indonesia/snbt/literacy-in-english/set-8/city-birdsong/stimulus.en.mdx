For a songbird, a song is a tool. Males sing to defend a territory and to attract a mate, and the song only works if it is heard. In cities, that has become harder. Traffic produces a constant low rumble that can mask the lower notes of a song, much as a conversation becomes difficult beside a busy road.

Birds respond to this in several ways. Many simply sing louder in noisy places. Some also change their pitch. In the early 2000s, researchers in the Netherlands found that great tits in noisy parts of cities sang with higher lowest notes than great tits in quiet woods. Since traffic noise is concentrated at low frequencies, raising the bottom of the song keeps more of it above the noise.

Others change their timing. A study of European robins in a British city found that robins in places with loud daytime noise were more likely to sing at night, when the streets are quieter. Night singing had often been blamed on street lighting, but in this study daytime noise predicted it better than light did.

These adjustments may come at a cost. In some experiments, female great tits responded more strongly to low-pitched songs than to high-pitched ones, so a male that sings higher to be heard may be heard as less attractive. Singing louder or at night also uses energy and time that could be spent feeding.

An unusual chance to test these ideas came in 2020, when pandemic lockdowns emptied the streets of San Francisco. Recordings of white-crowned sparrows showed that, with the traffic gone, the birds sang more softly while their songs could still be heard at roughly twice the distance as before. Within weeks, the birds had adjusted to a quieter city.
