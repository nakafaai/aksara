The last bus home left the town centre at eleven, and on Thursdays I was always on it. That Thursday a man in a grey coat got on two stops after me and sat across the aisle. He did not look at his phone. He did not look out of the window. Every time I glanced up, he was watching the door, as if he were counting the stops along with me.

When I got off at the bottom of Mill Lane, he got off too. The lane is long and has a single streetlight about halfway up. I walked faster. So did he, though he kept the same distance behind me the whole way, never closer and never further, like a dog that has been told to heel. By the time I reached our gate I had my keys between my fingers.

At the door I turned round. He was standing under the streetlight with his hands in his pockets, and he came no nearer. He waited until I had the door open, lifted one hand, and walked back down the lane towards the main road.

My mother was in the kitchen. She asked whether Mr Lin had seen me home all right. I did not know anyone called Mr Lin. She said he was the night porter at the hospital where she worked, that his shift ended at half past ten, that he lived two streets past ours, and that she had asked him, since he took the same bus anyway, to keep an eye on me as far as the gate.

"He doesn't talk much," she said. "I told him he didn't need to."

I put my keys back in the bowl. I thought about the grey coat under the streetlight, and about which way he had walked when he left.
