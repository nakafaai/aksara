To weaken "tests mostly measure coaching", you need evidence that scores track **something else**, once money and schooling are held equal.

The first finding does exactly that. It compares applicants **from the same school** and **with similar family incomes**, so the means to pay for coaching is held equal. If scores still predict first-year grades, they're measuring ability the university cares about, not mainly what a family can afford.

- Better-funded schools behind higher scores fits Text A's view that money shapes scores. It strengthens the claim.
- Scores rising with free coaching shows coaching **does** raise scores, which also fits Text A.
- A grade gap between the groups is Text B's point about the standard. It says nothing about what the scores themselves measure.
- Coaching improving essays weakens the case for dropping tests, but it doesn't show that scores measure anything besides coaching.

The finding that would most weaken the claim is that, among applicants from the same school and with similar family incomes, test scores still predicted first-year grades.
