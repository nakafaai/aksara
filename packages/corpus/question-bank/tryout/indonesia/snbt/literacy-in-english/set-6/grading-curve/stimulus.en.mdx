In many universities, final marks are "curved". Instead of grading each student against a fixed standard, teachers rank the class and give the top 15 percent an A, the next 35 percent a B, and so on down the list. Supporters say curving prevents grade inflation and corrects for tests that turn out harder or easier than intended. Those are real concerns. A strict curve, however, solves them at too high a price.

The first cost is to cooperation. Under a curve, one student's success lowers everyone else's chances. Explaining a difficult problem to a classmate becomes a risk, since that classmate might then move above you in the ranking. Students learn this lesson quickly, and study groups become rarer precisely in the courses where they would help most.

The second cost is to meaning. A grade should tell a future employer or teacher what a student can do. Under a curve, a B means only that a student did better than some classmates and worse than others. In an unusually strong class, a student who has mastered the material may still receive a C. In a weak class, a student with serious gaps may receive an A.

A better approach is to set clear standards in advance and grade against them. If a test proves much harder than intended, a teacher can adjust the standard for that test openly and explain why, rather than ranking students against one another. This keeps the protection that supporters value without turning classmates into rivals.

Curving is attractive because it is simple: the share of each grade is fixed before a single paper is read. But simplicity for the teacher should not come at the cost of what students learn to value.
