For three years I went to Mrs Varga every Thursday, and for three years she told me exactly what was wrong with my playing. Too heavy in the left hand. The second phrase rushed. A wrong note in bar twelve that I had played so often it had become, she said, "part of the furniture".

The spring I turned fifteen, she began to praise me. I played the Schubert badly one week, badly enough that I stopped in the middle, and she nodded and said, "Good. Much better." I took it as a kindness and was annoyed by it, the way you are annoyed when someone lets you win.

Other things changed too, though I did not put them together. She moved her chair from beside the piano to the end of the keyboard, where she could see my hands. She asked me to play the slow movement louder, then louder again, until it was not slow at all. The metronome, which had ticked on top of the piano for three years, now sat on the music stand with its little arm swinging in front of my eyes, and she watched the arm rather than me. When the telephone rang in the hall, she did not move.

In June she told my mother that she had taught me everything she could and that I should go to Mr Hollis at the conservatory. My mother was delighted. I was not. I had wanted to stay until I played the Schubert well enough for Mrs Varga to say something was wrong with it.

I went to Mr Hollis for two years. He was a good teacher. The first week, he stopped me at bar twelve.
