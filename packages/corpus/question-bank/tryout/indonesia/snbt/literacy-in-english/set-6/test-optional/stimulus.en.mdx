**Text A**

Three years ago our university stopped requiring entrance-test scores. Applications rose by <InlineMath math="40\%" />, and the share of admitted students from low-income families rose from <InlineMath math="12\%" /> to <InlineMath math="19\%" />. Students admitted without scores have earned first-year grades almost as high as those who sent them in. Entrance tests mostly measure how much coaching a family can afford. Dropping them has widened the door without lowering the standard.

**Text B**

I share the goal, and the rise in low-income admissions is welcome. But "almost as high" hides a gap. Students admitted without scores averaged <InlineMath math="2.9" /> in their first year, against <InlineMath math="3.1" /> for those with scores, and that gap appeared among students the university had already chosen after weighing everything else it knew about them. Research at several universities has found that scores, read against the results of an applicant's own school, help admissions officers spot strong students from weak schools whose other records look ordinary. Without scores, a university may lean more on essays and activities, which coaching also improves. I would keep tests optional for now, but I would ask the university to report each applicant's score beside the results of their school, and to track how students admitted each way do after their first year.
