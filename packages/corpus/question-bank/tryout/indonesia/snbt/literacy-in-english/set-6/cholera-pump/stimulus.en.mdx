In late August 1854 cholera broke out around Broad Street in Soho, London, and some five hundred people died within ten days. The physician John Snow, who suspected that cholera spread through contaminated water rather than foul air, marked the deaths on a street map. They clustered around a public water pump on Broad Street. On 8 September, at his urging, the local board removed the pump's handle.

The handle has become a symbol of public health, and the story is often told as though the outbreak stopped because of it. Snow himself was more careful. Deaths had already fallen sharply before the handle came off, partly because many residents had fled. The map was suggestive, too, but not decisive: people who live close to a pump also share the same air, so a cluster around the pump fitted the bad-air theory almost as well as Snow's. More telling were the exceptions. A workhouse near the pump, with its own well, had few deaths among hundreds of inmates. Workers at a nearby brewery, who drank beer and water from the brewery's own supply, escaped almost entirely. And a widow who lived miles away, but had water fetched from Broad Street because she liked its taste, died of cholera.

Snow's strongest evidence came from elsewhere in London. Two companies supplied water to the same districts south of the Thames, often to houses in the same street. The Southwark and Vauxhall company drew its water from the river at Battersea, below the city's sewage outlets. The Lambeth company had moved its intake upstream, beyond them, in 1852.
