Until the nineteenth century, every town set its clocks by the sun. Noon was the moment the sun stood highest in the sky, and because the Earth turns from west to east, that moment arrives a little later the farther west you go. In Britain, Bristol's noon came about ten minutes after London's. For most of history this hardly mattered. A journey between the two cities took the better part of two days, and nobody could tell the difference in their clocks.

The railways changed that. A train could now cover the distance in a few hours, and a timetable had to say when it would leave and arrive. If each station kept its own local time, a single journey involved several different clocks, and passengers missed connections that existed only on paper. In 1840, the Great Western Railway began using London time at all its stations, and by the end of the decade most British railways had done the same. The public called it "railway time".

Not every town welcomed the change. Some kept local time for everyday life while the station followed London, and a clock on Bristol's Corn Exchange still has two minute hands, one for each time. Only in 1880 did Parliament make Greenwich time the legal time throughout Great Britain, decades after most people had begun using it.

In the United States, the problem was larger, because the country spans several hours of sunlight from east to west. Railway companies, each with its own time, were using dozens of different standards. On 18 November 1883, the railroads agreed to use four time zones, each one hour apart. Newspapers called it "the day of two noons", because many towns saw noon twice as their clocks were reset. The government did not make the zones law until 1918.

In both countries, the pattern was the same. A new technology made local time impractical, private companies agreed on a common standard, and governments followed years later.
