Hide a treat under one of two upturned bowls, point at the right one, and most pet dogs will walk straight to it. The trick looks trivial, yet chimpanzees often ignore a human's pointing finger in the same test. For over <InlineMath math="20" /> years, the argument over why dogs find it so easy has kept returning to one awkward comparison: wolves.

In 2002 Brian Hare's team reported that wolves raised by people did poorly on pointing tasks, whereas dog puppies only a few weeks old, including some with little human contact, did well. The natural reading was that domestication had built the skill into dogs. In 2008 a University of Florida team that included Clive Wynne complicated that reading. Wolves that met people daily followed a distant pointing finger without training, and some dogs, notably shelter dogs, did worse. Earlier comparisons, the team argued, had tested wolves outdoors, sometimes from behind a fence, and dogs indoors. On their account, domestication is not a prerequisite for the skill, and an animal's own experience of people matters at least as much.

The obvious reply is that adults have had years to learn. An inherited skill should show up before learning has had much time to work, and that is the logic of a 2021 study led by Hannah Salomons at Duke University. Her team compared <InlineMath math="44" /> dog puppies with <InlineMath math="37" /> wolf puppies aged <InlineMath math="5" /> to <InlineMath math="18" /> weeks. The wolf pups had been hand-fed and given almost round-the-clock human care from a few days after birth. The dog pups, bred to become assistance dogs, lived with their mothers and littermates and saw far less of people. Yet <InlineMath math="17" /> of the <InlineMath math="31" /> dog pups tested on pointing chose the right bowl consistently, while none of the <InlineMath math="26" /> wolf pups tested did better than chance. On tasks measuring memory and self-control, the two groups performed alike.

A second study that year, led by Emily Bray, tested <InlineMath math="375" /> puppies about <InlineMath math="8" /> weeks old from the same assistance-dog programme. It estimated that more than <InlineMath math="40\%" /> of the variation in how well those puppies followed a point could be traced to genetic differences between them.

Neither study has ended the debate. Critics of the 2021 comparison point out that the two groups of pups differed in more than their genes. They were reared in different ways, and the dog pups were far more willing to approach a stranger: by the team's own measure, <InlineMath math="30" /> times more likely to do so. A pup that keeps its distance from the person who points may fail for reasons that have little to do with understanding the gesture. On this view, the wolf pups' failure may be a story about nerves rather than minds. That fits an older idea: domestication mainly selected for a calm temperament, and losing fear let a social skill already present in the dogs' ancestors come to the surface.

The two camps are closer than the headlines suggest. Hardly anyone now argues that experience is irrelevant, since some shelter dogs with little human company do poorly on these tasks. Few would claim either that a wolf, however tame, starts life as tuned to people as a dog pup does. What remains in dispute is how large each share is, and whether passing a pointing test measures understanding or only a willingness to engage with the person doing the pointing.
