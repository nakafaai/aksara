For most animals, sleep is a risky business. A sleeping animal cannot watch for predators, and an animal that must keep moving, such as a dolphin that has to surface to breathe, faces an obvious problem. Some species have solved it in an unusual way: they sleep with only half of the brain at a time.

In this kind of sleep, one hemisphere shows the slow, regular brain waves of deep sleep while the other remains awake. The animal can keep one eye open and continue to swim, fly, or keep watch. Dolphins rest this way, letting each half of the brain sleep in turn. Fur seals show an interesting difference: in the water they sleep mainly with one hemisphere at a time, but on land, where they are safer, they usually sleep with both.

A well-known experiment with mallard ducks showed that the behaviour can be adjusted to danger. Researchers placed ducks in a row and filmed them as they slept. Ducks at either end of the row, the most exposed positions, spent far more time sleeping with one eye open than ducks in the middle. The open eye was usually the one facing away from the other ducks, towards where a predator might come from.

The ability is also useful on long journeys. When researchers fitted frigatebirds with small devices that recorded brain activity, they found that the birds slept while flying over the ocean, often with one hemisphere at a time. In flight they slept for less than an hour a day, far less than the many hours they sleep on land.

Humans cannot sleep with half a brain, but we may have a faint version of the same system. Studies of people sleeping in an unfamiliar place for the first time found that one hemisphere stayed somewhat more alert than the other and responded more strongly to unusual sounds. This may help explain why many people sleep badly on their first night in a hotel.
