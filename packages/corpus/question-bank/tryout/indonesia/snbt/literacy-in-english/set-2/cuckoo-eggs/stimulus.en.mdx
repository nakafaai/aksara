The common cuckoo never raises its own young. A female waits until a pair of smaller birds, often reed warblers, leave their nest unguarded, then removes one of their eggs and lays one of her own in its place, a visit that can take as little as ten seconds. The cuckoo chick usually hatches first, and within a day or two it pushes the warblers' eggs over the rim of the nest one by one. The warblers then spend weeks feeding a single chick that soon outweighs both of them together.

For a long time the textbook account was that the hosts are simply fooled. Cuckoo eggs often match the colour and speckling of the host's own, and each female cuckoo belongs to a lineage, called a gens, that specialises in one host species. Seen this way, the relationship is an arms race that the cuckoo is winning: every sharpening of the host's eye is answered by a better forgery.

Field experiments complicate this picture. When researchers place painted model eggs in warbler nests, many hosts do reject them, either by pushing the odd egg out or by deserting the nest and starting again. But rejection is not free. A warbler trying to eject an egg sometimes cracks one of its own, and a pair that deserts loses a week or more of a short breeding season. Rejection also has a cost when the warbler is wrong: a host that rejects too readily will sometimes throw out one of its own eggs that happens to look unusual. Warblers have been found to reject far more often when they have just seen a cuckoo near the nest than when they have not. The hosts are not blind. They weigh the evidence against the price of a mistake.

This changes what the "failure" of a host means. Where cuckoos are rare, a warbler that accepts an odd-looking egg is usually accepting its own, so a habit of tolerance costs little. Where cuckoos are common, the same habit can cost a whole season's brood. In one English reed bed, researchers who tested warblers with model eggs over more than a decade found that rejection became steadily less common as local cuckoo numbers fell. The change came too quickly to be the work of natural selection alone. Individual birds seem to adjust their caution to the risk around them.
