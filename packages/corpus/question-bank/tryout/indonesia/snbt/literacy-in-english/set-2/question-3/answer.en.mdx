Each option names an idea and a reason, and both halves must be right.

**Which idea?** Quotation marks around one word often mean "so-called": the writer uses the word but doubts it. Calling an accepting host a "failure" belongs to the view in the second paragraph that "the hosts are simply fooled", the arms race "that the cuckoo is winning".

**On what grounds?** The same paragraph answers at once: "Where cuckoos are rare, a warbler that accepts an odd-looking egg is usually accepting its own, so a habit of tolerance costs little." Acceptance can be **a sensible bet**, not a failure.

- The idea is right, but the reason isn't. Hosts reject "far more often" after a sighting, not every time.
- The passage never doubts that rejection is costly. It uses those costs to explain acceptance. The brood loss is real, but quotation marks don't add emphasis.
- The passage never says egg matching is poor. "Cuckoo eggs often match" stands.
- Nothing doubts that the chick pushes out the eggs, and the passage never says most hosts reject cuckoo eggs.

The quotation marks question the idea that accepting hosts are simply fooled, on the grounds that acceptance can be a sensible bet where cuckoos are rare.
