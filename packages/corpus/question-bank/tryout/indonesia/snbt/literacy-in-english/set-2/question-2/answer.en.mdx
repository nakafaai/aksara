Statement 1. The third paragraph opens with "Field experiments complicate this picture": many hosts "do reject" model eggs, and "The hosts are not blind." The experiments argue against the textbook view, not for it. No.

Statement 2. Warblers "reject far more often when they have just seen a cuckoo near the nest than when they have not". "Far more often" isn't "always", and the trigger is a sighting, not how common cuckoos are in the area. A warbler that hasn't seen one may still keep an odd egg. Yes.

Statement 3. Egg matching belongs to the textbook account in the second paragraph, which the passage then **complicates**. The evidence for weighing risks is in the third paragraph: the costs of rejecting and the effect of seeing a cuckoo. No.

Statement 4. The third paragraph says a host that rejects too readily throws out "one of its own eggs that happens to look unusual". The last paragraph says that where cuckoos are rare, an odd egg is "usually" the host's own, so tolerance "costs little". Together, the risk of losing its own eggs is what makes keeping odd eggs pay there. Yes.

Statement 5. In that reed bed, rejection fell as cuckoos fell, and the change "came too quickly to be the work of natural selection alone" because "individual birds seem to adjust their caution to the risk around them". Birds that adjust to risk should adjust back when the risk rises, which takes years, not generations. Yes.

So statements 2, 4 and 5 are Yes, and statements 1 and 3 are No.
