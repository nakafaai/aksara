**Text B**

Nobody should doubt that the crash counts at the camera sites went down. What the counts cannot tell us is how much of the fall the cameras caused. Sites are not chosen at random. A road usually gets a camera because it has just had an unusually bad run of crashes, and the count at any one spot rises and falls from year to year largely by chance. A site picked straight after a bad stretch will therefore tend to have fewer crashes afterwards even if nothing is done, a pattern statisticians call regression to the mean. When studies in other countries allowed for it, the effect of fixed cameras remained real but came out at roughly half the raw before-and-after fall.

There is a second gap. Some drivers brake hard for a camera and speed up once they are past it, so a share of the crashes may simply move to nearby streets, where a count of camera sites alone will never find them. None of this is an argument against cameras. It is an argument for measuring them properly: count crashes within a kilometre of each site, and compare the camera sites with similar sites that had the same bad run in the same years but have not yet been given a camera.
