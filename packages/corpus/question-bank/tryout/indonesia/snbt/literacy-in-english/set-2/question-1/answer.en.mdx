# Given

In the hypothetical scenario, a reed warbler can eject any egg **without ever cracking its own**. The reed bed is a place where cuckoos are rare.

# Asked

Why might this warbler still do better by keeping odd-looking eggs?

# Solution

Remove the physical breakage cost and look for the remaining **cost of misjudgement** in paragraphs <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="4" />.

> Even a warbler that never cracks an egg during ejection still loses its own young whenever it mistakes an unusual host egg for a cuckoo egg.

## Evidence from the passage

Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> identifies a second cost of rejection: <Highlight>Rejection also has a cost when the warbler is wrong: a host that rejects too readily will sometimes throw out one of its own eggs that happens to look unusual</Highlight>.

Paragraph <InlineMath math="4" /> connects this directly to rare-cuckoo habitats: <Highlight>Where cuckoos are rare, a warbler that accepts an odd-looking egg is usually accepting its own, so a habit of tolerance costs little</Highlight>.

Combining both claims, an odd egg in a low-cuckoo reed bed is **usually the warbler's own**, so routine rejection would mostly destroy its own eggs even with flawless ejection technique.

## Why the other options fail

- **Deserting a nest** is a different rejection method, and the passage never links losing a week to places where cuckoos are rare.
- **Egg matching** restores the outdated fooled-host view, whereas the passage states <Highlight>The hosts are not blind</Highlight>.
- **Cuckoo chick behaviour** stays fatal whenever a cuckoo hatches, while rarity lowers how often it happens, not the outcome.
- **Natural selection** is explicitly ruled out as the sole cause in the English reed bed because the shift happened too quickly.

# Answer

**Because an odd egg there is usually its own, so a habit of rejecting would mostly destroy its own eggs**.
