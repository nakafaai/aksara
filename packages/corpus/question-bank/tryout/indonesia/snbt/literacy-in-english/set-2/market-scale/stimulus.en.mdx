Everyone at the Thursday market knew that Mrs Adeyemi's scale was not to be trusted. The fish seller said so with a wink, the baker said so to anyone queuing for bread, and my mother said so to me on my first morning at my grandmother's rice stall, adding only, "Watch her hands, not the needle."

So I watched her hands. Grandma scooped rice into paper cones, set each cone on the tray and called out the weight before the needle had stopped swinging. A woman in a nurse's uniform paid for a kilo and left. A schoolboy with a note from his mother paid for half a kilo and left. Whatever her hands were doing, I could not catch them at it.

Around noon Grandma went to the back to fetch change. I lifted the empty tray, set it down again and watched the needle settle. It came to rest a hair below zero.

"It is honest for some," Grandma said behind me, "and generous for others." She had not gone for change at all.

I said nothing. I was ashamed for her, and then, when she turned the scale to the light and showed me the small brass screw at the back, I was ashamed of myself, though it took me the rest of the afternoon to work out why. A man in a good coat stopped at the stall soon after. Before she weighed his rice, Grandma gave the screw a quarter turn, and the needle sat exactly on zero. When the nurse's daughter came in later with a long list and a handful of coins, which she counted twice before she put them down, Grandma turned it back. "Prices go up every month," she said. "Wages go up when they remember."

At supper I told my mother that I had watched Grandma's hands.

"And?"

"They don't do anything. It's the screw."

My mother laughed, which I had not expected. "Thirty years," she said, "and the inspector has never once found that scale wrong. He comes on Mondays, when the market is shut."

The next Thursday I was the one who turned the screw. Twice I turned it the wrong way, and both times the customer went home with less rice than the needle had promised. Grandma made me run after them both with a second cone.
