# Given

At two reed beds, **X** (tall, thick reeds) and **Y** (low-cut reeds with wide visibility), cuckoos are **equally common** and researchers place the **same model egg** in nests. Warblers accept <InlineMath math="70%" /> of model eggs at X and <InlineMath math="20%" /> at Y.

# Asked

Which explanation of the difference between X and Y fits the passage best?

# Solution

Eliminate the factors **held constant** by the experiment (egg mimicry quality and cuckoo density), leaving **visibility of approaching cuckoos** as the lone variable.

> When cuckoo density and model-egg appearance are identical, open reeds increase rejection simply because warblers spot approaching cuckoos near their nests far more often.

## Matching the experimental contrast to the text

Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> provides the exact mechanism tied to sightlines: <Highlight>Warblers have been found to reject far more often when they have just seen a cuckoo near the nest than when they have not</Highlight>.

Because the reeds at Y are cut low, approaching birds are visible from far away. Warblers at Y are therefore **more likely to have seen a cuckoo near the nest**, triggering the much lower <InlineMath math="20%" /> acceptance rate compared with <InlineMath math="70%" /> in thick cover at X.

## Why the other options fail

- **Sharper eyesight** is never proposed in the passage, while hosts weigh sighting evidence, not optical acuity differences.
- **Natural selection from reed cutting** lacks support and conflicts with the passage's point that behavioural shifts happen too rapidly for selection alone.
- **Better forgeries at X** contradicts the prompt, which uses the same painted model egg at both sites.
- **More cuckoos at X** directly contradicts the prompt stating cuckoos are equally common at both reed beds.

# Answer

**Warblers at Y are more likely to have seen a cuckoo near the nest, and a warbler that has seen one rejects far more often**.
