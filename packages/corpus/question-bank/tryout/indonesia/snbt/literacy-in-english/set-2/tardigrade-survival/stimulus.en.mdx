Tardigrades, often called water bears, are eight-legged animals about half a millimetre long that live in films of water on moss, lichens, and soil, as well as in lakes and oceans. They have become famous as the toughest animals on Earth. Laboratory reports describe tardigrades surviving temperatures close to absolute zero, doses of radiation hundreds of times higher than a human could endure, and even several days in the vacuum of space aboard a satellite. Such claims are accurate, but they are often misread.

An active tardigrade is not especially hardy. Crawling through a damp cushion of moss, it can be killed by conditions that would not trouble many other small invertebrates. Its extraordinary survival depends on a single strategy: when its surroundings dry out, it loses almost all of its body water, pulls in its legs, and curls into a compact, barrel-shaped form called a tun. In this state its metabolism falls so low that it can barely be detected. Tardigrades do not so much resist harsh conditions as wait them out. When water returns, a tun can rehydrate and resume normal activity within hours, sometimes after years in a dried state.

How a body survives without water is still being worked out. Many animals that tolerate drying, such as brine shrimp and some nematode worms, fill their cells with a sugar called trehalose, which helps preserve cell structures. Many tardigrades, however, produce little of it. Instead, researchers have identified proteins unique to tardigrades that have no fixed shape in solution. As a cell dries, these proteins appear to form a glass-like solid that locks other molecules in place, preventing them from unfolding or sticking together until water returns.

A different protein seems to protect genetic material. One tardigrade species produces a protein that binds to DNA and shields it from damage. When scientists inserted the gene for this protein into human cells grown in the laboratory, the cells suffered noticeably less DNA damage after exposure to X-rays than ordinary cells did.

These findings have attracted interest well beyond zoology. Vaccines and some medicines must be kept cold, which makes them difficult to deliver in places without reliable refrigeration. If the way tardigrade proteins stabilise molecules during drying could be copied, it might become possible to store such products dry at room temperature. That goal remains distant, but it illustrates how studying an obscure animal can lead to practical questions no one expected to ask.
