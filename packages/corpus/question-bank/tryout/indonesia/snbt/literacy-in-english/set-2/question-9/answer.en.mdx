# Given

**Text A** measures the cameras' success by the gap between the <InlineMath math="40%+" /> fall at the <InlineMath math="40" /> camera sites and the <InlineMath math="8%" /> city-wide fall. **Text B** explains how sites are chosen and why counts fluctuate.

# Asked

Based on Text B, what is wrong with treating that gap as a measure of what the cameras achieved?

# Solution

Apply Text B's **selection-bias argument** to the comparison between the <InlineMath math="40" /> chosen roads and the rest of the city.

> Because camera sites are selected right after an abnormal spike while the whole city is not, the sites naturally drop faster than city roads even without cameras, inflating the gap.

## Applying Text B critique

Text B states <Highlight>Sites are not chosen at random. A road usually gets a camera because it has just had an unusually bad run of crashes</Highlight> and concludes that such a spot <Highlight>will therefore tend to have fewer crashes afterwards even if nothing is done</Highlight>.

The city as a whole was not selected after an extreme spike. Comparing spike-selected roads against the entire city therefore mixes **regression to the mean** into the gap, attributing natural rebound to the cameras.

## Why the other options fail

- **City-wide figure is less reliable** reverses Text B: random fluctuation hits counts "at any one spot", making individual sites noisier than the city total.
- **Moved crashes should enlarge the city fall** is backwards: crashes shifting to nearby streets stay inside the city total, leaving the city-wide change roughly intact.
- **Average versus percentage** misreads Text A, which converts the site averages into a percentage ("more than <InlineMath math="40%" />") before comparing.
- **Excluding later events** is never proposed anywhere in Text B.

# Answer

**The camera sites were picked after an unusually bad run, so they would be expected to fall more than the city's roads even without cameras, and part of the gap is not the cameras' doing**.
