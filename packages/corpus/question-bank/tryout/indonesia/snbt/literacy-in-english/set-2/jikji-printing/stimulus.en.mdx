In 1377, monks at Heungdeok Temple in Cheongju, in what is now South Korea, printed a collection of Buddhist teachings using movable type cast in metal. The book is usually known by its short name, *Jikji*. It was produced about seventy-eight years before Johannes Gutenberg printed his famous Bible in Mainz, and it is now recognised as the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. In 2001, UNESCO added it to its Memory of the World Register.

The word "surviving" matters. Of the two volumes of *Jikji*, only the second still exists, and its history is unusual. A French diplomat acquired it in Korea in the late nineteenth century, and it eventually passed to the National Library of France in Paris, where it remains. For decades it sat in the collection without attracting attention, until a Korean librarian working there identified its printing date and method in the 1970s.

*Jikji* may not even be the earliest book of its kind. A Korean writer of the thirteenth century recorded that a collection of ritual texts had been printed with metal type in about 1234, but no copy is known to exist. Historians therefore face a familiar problem. They can say with confidence which example is the oldest to survive, but a claim about the very first can change whenever new evidence comes to light, or remain uncertain forever if that evidence has been lost.

The idea of movable type is older still. In the eleventh century, a Chinese craftsman named Bi Sheng made individual characters from baked clay, a method described by the scholar Shen Kuo. Clay type, however, was fragile, and woodblock printing, in which an entire page was carved into a single block of wood, remained the usual method in China for centuries.

Why, then, is Gutenberg so often treated as the inventor of printing? Part of the answer lies in what followed. In Korea, metal type was used mainly by the royal court and Buddhist temples to produce limited numbers of important texts, and written Korean at the time relied largely on Chinese characters, which run into the thousands. In fifteenth-century Europe, by contrast, printers worked with an alphabet of a few dozen letters and sold their books to a growing market. Within a few decades, printing shops had opened in many European cities.
