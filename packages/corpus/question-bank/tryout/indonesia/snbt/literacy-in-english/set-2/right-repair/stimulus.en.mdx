When a phone screen cracks or a washing machine stops draining, the cheapest fix is often to replace a single part. Yet many owners discover that the part cannot be bought, that the repair manual is kept secret, or that the device refuses to work properly after a new part is fitted by anyone other than the manufacturer's own technicians. Laws establishing a "right to repair" aim to change this, and they deserve wide support.

The practices that block repair take several forms. Some companies sell spare parts only to authorised service centres. Others glue batteries in place or use unusual screws that ordinary tools cannot turn. Increasingly, manufacturers also use a technique known as parts pairing: each major component carries a digital code that is registered to one particular device, so a replacement screen or camera taken from an identical model may trigger warning messages or lose some functions until the company's software approves it.

The costs of these practices fall on several groups at once. Owners pay more or replace devices that could have been repaired. Independent repair shops, which are often small local businesses, lose work they are perfectly capable of doing. And the environment pays as well, because a discarded phone or appliance becomes electronic waste, much of which is never properly recycled.

Manufacturers respond that unauthorised repairs can be unsafe. A poorly installed battery can overheat, and a tampered device may expose its owner's data. These concerns are real, but they do not justify blanket restrictions. Safety can be supported by publishing proper repair instructions, which reduce botched repairs rather than encourage them. Security can be protected with systems that verify a part is genuine without requiring the manufacturer's own approval for every repair. A company that truly cares about safe repairs has every reason to make the correct information available.

Some governments have already begun to act. The European Union, for example, has required makers of certain household appliances to keep spare parts available for a number of years after a model is sold. Rules of this kind do not force anyone to repair a product. They simply make sure that when an owner chooses to repair rather than replace, that choice is a real one.
