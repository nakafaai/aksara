The last two paragraphs explain acceptance by **risk**, not by blindness, and they separate the cost of a habit from the cost of one accepted cuckoo egg. Judge each option on its own.

- **Cannot tell eggs apart**. The passage says the hosts "are not blind" and that many reject model eggs. High acceptance is explained as caution matched to risk. Not inferable.
- **Low risk rather than inability**. "Where cuckoos are rare, a warbler that accepts an odd-looking egg is usually accepting its own, so a habit of tolerance costs little." A population that accepts a lot may simply face few cuckoos, and the hosts aren't blind. Inferable.
- **Shaped by natural selection over generations**. In the English reed bed the change "came too quickly to be the work of natural selection alone". The passage points to individual adjustment, so "must" is wrong. Not inferable.
- **Loses little when the egg is a cuckoo's**. This is the trap. "Costs little" describes the *habit* of tolerance where most odd eggs are the host's own. An egg that really is a cuckoo's still produces a chick that pushes out the warblers' eggs, so that bird loses its brood. Not inferable.
- **Acceptance could fall if cuckoos became common**. Warblers reject far more often after seeing a cuckoo, and in the English reed bed rejection moved with cuckoo numbers. More cuckoos should mean more rejection, so acceptance could fall. Inferable.

The inferable statements are that its high acceptance may reflect a low risk of meeting cuckoos rather than an inability to spot odd eggs, and that its acceptance could fall if cuckoos became more common nearby.
