# Given

We consider a population of reed warblers that **accepts many cuckoo eggs**, evaluated against paragraphs <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="4" />.

# Asked

Which statements can be inferred about this warbler population? Select all that apply.

# Solution

Keep two distinctions sharp: acceptance tracks **local risk rather than blindness**, and the low cost of a **tolerant habit** differs from the catastrophic cost of a single accepted cuckoo egg.

> High acceptance in a warbler population points to low local cuckoo pressure and flexible individual caution, not inability to tell eggs apart or painless cuckoo parasitism.

## Option by option check

| Option | Verdict | Evidence from the passage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cannot tell own eggs from other eggs | **False** | Paragraph <InlineMath math="3" /> states <Highlight>The hosts are not blind</Highlight> and many reject model eggs. |
| High acceptance may reflect low cuckoo risk | **True** | Paragraph <InlineMath math="4" /> explains <Highlight>Where cuckoos are rare, a warbler that accepts an odd-looking egg is usually accepting its own, so a habit of tolerance costs little</Highlight>. |
| Must be shaped by natural selection over generations | **False** | Paragraph <InlineMath math="4" /> says the English shift <Highlight>came too quickly to be the work of natural selection alone</Highlight>. |
| Loses little when an accepted egg is a cuckoo's | **False** | Paragraph <InlineMath math="1" /> shows an accepted cuckoo chick pushes out all host eggs, costing a whole brood, while "costs little" applies only to the habit where odd eggs are rarely cuckoos'. |
| Acceptance could fall if cuckoos became common | **True** | Individual birds adjust caution to local risk, and warblers reject far more often when cuckoos are sighted nearby. |

# Answer

- **Its high acceptance may reflect a low risk of meeting cuckoos rather than an inability to spot odd eggs**.
- **Its rate of acceptance could fall if cuckoos became more common nearby**.
