A honeybee that has found rich flowers often returns to the hive and dances on the vertical comb in near darkness. She makes short straight runs, shaking her abdomen, and loops back between them. Karl von Frisch argued that this "waggle run" is a set of directions. Its angle from the vertical matches the angle between the food and the sun as seen from the hive, so a run tilted <InlineMath math="40^\circ" /> right of straight up means "fly <InlineMath math="40^\circ" /> right of the sun". The longer the run lasts, the farther away the food lies.

The distance in that code is not measured in metres. A bee judges how far she has flown from how much of the scene streams across her eyes, so the closer and more detailed her surroundings, the more distance she registers for each metre actually flown. Bees trained to collect sugar at the end of a narrow, patterned tunnel danced as if the food lay far beyond the tunnel's real end. The code still works because a recruit that sets off along the same route sees the same scenery and measures with the same flexible ruler.

In the 1960s the biologist Adrian Wenner challenged von Frisch, arguing that recruits find food mainly by smell, using the floral scent on the dancer's body and the odour of the site itself. Recruits also took far longer to arrive than a bee simply following a bearing should need. His deeper point was this: that bees dance, and that a dance contains information, does not show that other bees use it.

James Gould set dance and scent against each other. In the dark a dancer orients to gravity, but she may orient to a visible light instead, treating it as the sun. Gould painted over the dancers' ocelli, small light-sensitive eyes on top of the head, so they kept dancing to gravity, while recruits with untouched ocelli read the same runs against a lamp. Dancer and recruit therefore disagreed by a predictable angle, and most recruits turned up at feeding stations along the bearing they had read, not at the feeder whose scent the dancers carried. Gould did not dismiss smell, though: under some conditions, he reported, recruits rely on odour alone.

Three decades later, a team at Rothamsted Research in England tracked recruits by radar. Most flew fairly straight to the area of the advertised feeder, then searched around it for a long time, which explains the delay that had troubled the critics. Recruits released up to <InlineMath math="250" /> metres from the hive flew the bearing the dance had given, as though still starting from home, and so missed the feeder.

Popular accounts often treat the radar study as proof that colonies depend on the dance. It settled the question Wenner had asked, not the one those accounts went on to answer on its behalf. When Gavin Sherman and Kirk Visscher laid hives on their sides, the dances lost their direction information, although the bees kept dancing. Those colonies gathered clearly less food than normal colonies in winter, slightly less in summer and no less in autumn. Anna Dornhaus and Lars Chittka found that location information helped colonies in a tropical habitat but not in two temperate ones. The dance may pay off most where food comes in rich, short-lived patches, and least where flowers are widespread and long-lasting.
