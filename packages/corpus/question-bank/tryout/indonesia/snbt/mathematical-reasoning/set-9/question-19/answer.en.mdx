On a surface net, the shortest route for a fixed sequence of faces becomes a **straight segment**. Points <InlineMath math="P" /> and <InlineMath math="Q" /> are midpoints of vertical edges of height <InlineMath math="2\text{ cm}" />, so each lies <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" /> from either the top or the bottom face.

The four side faces form a band of length <InlineMath math="2(4+8)=24\text{ cm}" />. The two points are opposite on that band at the same height, so a route using **only side faces** has length <InlineMath math="4+8=12\text{ cm}" />.

For a net through the top face, take the top as a <InlineMath math="4\times8" /> rectangle. The bottom face gives the same three lengths.

- The two faces attached to the edges of length <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> place the points at <InlineMath math="(-1,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(5,8)" />, giving <InlineMath math="\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10" />.
- The two faces attached to the edges of length <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" /> place them at <InlineMath math="(0,-1)" /> and <InlineMath math="(4,9)" />, giving <InlineMath math="\sqrt{4^2+10^2}=\sqrt{116}" />.
- Two adjacent side faces place them at <InlineMath math="(-1,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(4,9)" />, giving <InlineMath math="\sqrt{5^2+9^2}=\sqrt{106}" />.

The complete set of candidate lengths is <InlineMath math="12,10,\sqrt{116},\sqrt{106}" />. The **minimum** is <InlineMath math="10" />, so the shortest surface route from <InlineMath math="P" /> to <InlineMath math="Q" /> is <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />.
