Both stairs have to use exactly the same steps. So the plan: **first find a height <InlineMath math="R" /> that fits both gaps at once**, then pick the narrowest tread that's still allowed, and finally count the treads.

# The shared step height

The lower stair has <InlineMath math="n_1" /> steps and the upper stair <InlineMath math="n_2" />. Since the heights match, <InlineMath math="R=\tfrac{288}{n_1}=\tfrac{272}{n_2}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{n_2}{n_1} &= \frac{272}{288} = \frac{17}{18} \\
n_1 = 18k,\ n_2 &= 17k \\
R &= \frac{288}{18k} = \frac{16}{k}
\end{aligned}" />

The second line holds because <InlineMath math="17" /> and <InlineMath math="18" /> share no factor other than <InlineMath math="1" />, so <InlineMath math="n_1" /> has to be a multiple of <InlineMath math="18" />. Here <InlineMath math="k" /> is a positive whole number. The rise rule asks for <InlineMath math="15\le R\le18" />. For <InlineMath math="k=1" />, <InlineMath math="R=16" /> works, while <InlineMath math="k=2" /> already gives <InlineMath math="R=8" />, which is too low. So **the only height that fits both stairs is <InlineMath math="16\text{ cm}" />**, with <InlineMath math="n_1=18" /> and <InlineMath math="n_2=17" />.

# The narrowest tread

The shortest stairs use the narrowest treads. The step rule with <InlineMath math="R=16" /> gives a lower limit for <InlineMath math="T" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2\cdot16+T &\ge 62 \\
T &\ge 30
\end{aligned}" />

The upper limit is <InlineMath math="T\le66-32=34" />, so <InlineMath math="T=30" /> is allowed and **it's the narrowest tread for both stairs**.

# Treads and total length

The top step of each stair has no tread, because **the floor above doesn't count as a tread**. So each stair has one tread fewer than it has steps.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{treads} &= (18-1)+(17-1) = 33 \\
\text{total length} &= 33\cdot30 = 990\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

Check the sizes. The heights <InlineMath math="18\cdot16=288" /> and <InlineMath math="17\cdot16=272" /> match both gaps, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot16+30=62" /> sits exactly on the lower limit of the step rule.

# Why the other options are wrong

Each other option **forgets the same-size condition or miscounts the treads**.

- <InlineMath math="810\text{ cm}" /> comes from designing each stair on its own: the lower stair with <InlineMath math="16" /> steps of <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="T=26" /> (<InlineMath math="390\text{ cm}" />), and the upper stair with <InlineMath math="16" /> steps of <InlineMath math="17\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="T=28" /> (<InlineMath math="420\text{ cm}" />). Those sizes don't match.
- <InlineMath math="858\text{ cm}" /> comes from giving <InlineMath math="33" /> treads a width of <InlineMath math="26\text{ cm}" />, but that width is only allowed for <InlineMath math="R=18" />.
- <InlineMath math="1{,}020\text{ cm}" /> comes from counting the floor above one stair as a tread, which gives <InlineMath math="34" /> treads.
- <InlineMath math="1{,}050\text{ cm}" /> comes from treating the number of treads as the number of steps, <InlineMath math="35" />.

So the shortest total horizontal length of the two stairs is <InlineMath math="990\text{ cm}" />.
