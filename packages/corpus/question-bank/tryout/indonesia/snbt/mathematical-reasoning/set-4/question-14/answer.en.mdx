We use work rates. Let the three rates be <InlineMath math="v_A" />, <InlineMath math="v_B" />, and <InlineMath math="v_C" />. A person who completes one job in <InlineMath math="t" /> days completes the fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{t}" /> per day.

Based on the problem, we can set up three combined rate equations:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_A + v_B = \frac{1}{12}" />
  <BlockMath math="v_A + v_C = \frac{1}{15}" />
  <BlockMath math="v_B + v_C = \frac{1}{20}" />
</MathContainer>

Add all three equations at once. Each variable then appears twice.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(v_A + v_B) + (v_A + v_C) + (v_B + v_C) = \frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{15} + \frac{1}{20}" />
  <BlockMath math="2(v_A + v_B + v_C) = \frac{5}{60} + \frac{4}{60} + \frac{3}{60}" />
  <BlockMath math="2v_{\text{total}} = \frac{12}{60} = \frac{1}{5}" />
</MathContainer>

From the equation above, we get their total combined work rate:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{5} \div 2 = \frac{1}{10}" />
</MathContainer>

Since the total work rate is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{10}" /> of the job per day, the time required to complete <InlineMath math="1" /> full job is the reciprocal.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t = \frac{1}{v_{\text{total}}} = 10" />
</MathContainer>

Thus, they need <InlineMath math="10\text{ days}" />.
