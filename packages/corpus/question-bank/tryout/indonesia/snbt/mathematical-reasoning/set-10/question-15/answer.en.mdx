You can judge every statement with one tool: **turn concentration into an amount of stock A**, that is, concentration divided by <InlineMath math="200" />, times the volume. Amounts of stock can be added and subtracted, concentrations can't. After that, divide by the final volume again.

# Mixing two solutions

Find the stock A in each solution, add them up, then divide by the volume of the mixture.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{stock A} &= \frac{1{,}600}{200}\cdot50+\frac{1{,}100}{200}\cdot150=400+825=1{,}225\text{ ml}\\
\text{concentration of the mixture} &= \frac{1{,}225}{200}\cdot200=1{,}225\text{ ppm}
\end{aligned}" />

The value <InlineMath math="1{,}225\text{ ppm}" /> lies in the range <InlineMath math="1{,}150" /> to <InlineMath math="1{,}250\text{ ppm}" />. The plain average <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1{,}600+1{,}100}{2}=1{,}350" /> is misleading, because **the solution with the bigger volume counts more**. The first statement is true.

# The week-one solution

A <InlineMath math="200" />-litre solution at <InlineMath math="600\text{ ppm}" /> holds <InlineMath math="600\text{ ml}" /> of stock A. After one daily change, both the volume and the stock drop.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{concentration} &= \frac{600-25}{200-8}\cdot200=\frac{115{,}000}{192}=598.95\ldots\text{ ppm}
\end{aligned}" />

The concentration actually goes down. Here the stock drops by <InlineMath math="\tfrac{25}{600}" />, a little over <InlineMath math="4\%" />, while the water drops by only <InlineMath math="\tfrac{8}{200}=4\%" />. **The direction of the change depends on the starting concentration**: the solution only gets stronger above <InlineMath math="\tfrac{25}{8}\cdot200=625\text{ ppm}" />. The second statement is false.

# Adding stock to a solution

The <InlineMath math="100\text{ ml}" /> of stock spreads over <InlineMath math="160" /> litres, so each litre gets <InlineMath math="\tfrac{100}{160}\text{ ml}" /> of stock A.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{rise in concentration} &= \frac{100}{160}\cdot200=125\text{ ppm}
\end{aligned}" />

The rise isn't <InlineMath math="100\text{ ppm}" />, because **the volume is smaller than** <InlineMath math="200" /> litres. The third statement is true.

# Diluting for week three

<InlineMath math="150" /> litres at <InlineMath math="1{,}200\text{ ppm}" /> hold <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1{,}200}{200}\cdot150=900\text{ ml}" /> of stock A. Clean water adds no stock, so once the tank is full there are still <InlineMath math="900\text{ ml}" /> in <InlineMath math="200" /> litres.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{concentration} &= \frac{900}{200}\cdot200=900\text{ ppm}
\end{aligned}" />

That's **exactly the week-three target**. The fourth statement is true.

So the first statement is **true**, the second is **false**, the third is **true**, and the fourth is **true**.
