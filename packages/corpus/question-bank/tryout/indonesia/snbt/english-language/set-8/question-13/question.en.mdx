Jonas was translating a welcome sign that would appear at both a neighbourhood meal and a formal council meeting. He began with the greeting used in his family. It sounded warm when spoken across a kitchen table, but on the council sign it could suggest a closeness that the organisers had not yet established. A more formal alternative was respectful in public and strangely distant at the meal.

Rather than force one version into both settings, Jonas kept the two translations and added a short note describing where each was appropriate. He asked family members, event organisers, and another translator to review the distinction. Their responses refined the note but did not erase the difference between the settings.

The organisers selected the formal version for the council sign and the warmer version for spoken greetings at the meal. Jonas's choice made the ambiguity open to review and gave each translation a clear scope. It also showed why faithful translation may require more than replacing one word with another.

In his final note, Jonas used *connotation* to mean the social meaning and level of formality carried by a word or phrase beyond its basic reference. Both greetings could mean “welcome,” but their connotations suited different relationships. Recording that distinction was more accurate than labelling one version correct and the other wrong.
