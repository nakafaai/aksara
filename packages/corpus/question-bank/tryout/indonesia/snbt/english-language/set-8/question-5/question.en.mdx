An engineering class built paper bridges to test whether a triangular truss would increase the load carried before collapse. Every model used the same mass of paper and crossed the same span. Students added identical washers at the marked centre point, one at a time, so load placement would not vary between bridges.

| Condition | Mean result |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 24 |
| With the change | 39 |
| Comparison | 25 |

The triangular-truss models held a mean of 39 washers. The baseline and comparison models, which did not use the triangular fold, held means of 24 and 25. The gap supports an association between the truss design and a larger load capacity in these paper models.

The models were not perfectly interchangeable, however. Small differences in paper fibres, crease position, and fold sharpness could affect where a bridge failed. The class therefore proposed cutting sheets from one paper batch with a template, folding them around the same guide, and testing more bridges.

The report defined a *truss* as a framework of connected triangles that distributes forces. That definition explains the structural idea being tested. The washer count measures how each model performed, while the construction variation limits how confidently the result can be generalised.
