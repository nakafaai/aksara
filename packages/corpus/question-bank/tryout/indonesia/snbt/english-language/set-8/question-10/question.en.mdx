A multilingual youth translation club noticed that the same wording problem was being debated at every meeting. Students often found an accurate dictionary equivalent, only to learn from a family member or community interpreter that it sounded too formal, too intimate, or unfamiliar in another region. During an initial week, a mean of 19 translations were accepted after the first peer review.

Students, families, teachers, and community interpreters then built a shared glossary. Each entry included an example from a participant and a note about the situation in which the wording was used. The club alternated sessions with and without the glossary. First-review acceptance reached a mean of 31 in the trial sessions and 20 in the comparison sessions, while meeting time and reviewer availability remained stable.

| Stage | Mean value |

The club agreed to keep testing the glossary, but not to present it as a complete authority. A wording common in one family, age group, or region may carry a different meaning elsewhere. New entries therefore needed a named context and a way for participants to challenge or refine them.

In the report, *register* meant the situation and purpose that shape the most suitable wording. The concept explains why a translation can be accurate in one conversation and unsuitable in a public notice. It also explains why the glossary needs examples rather than a single universal equivalent.
