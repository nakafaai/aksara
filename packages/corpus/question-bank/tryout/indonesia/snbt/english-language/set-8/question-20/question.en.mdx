At a multilingual welcome desk, Mei kept turning a blank card between her fingers. One greeting came naturally from her grandmother's kitchen: warm, familiar, and suited to someone already treated like family. The other belonged to public meetings, where respect did not assume a close relationship. Printing only one would make every visitor enter through the same social doorway.

Mei wrote the home greeting on one side of the card and the public greeting on the other. When an elder from her community arrived, she showed the familiar side and spoke the words aloud. When a first-time visitor approached for official information, she turned the card and used the public version.

Neither side cancelled the other. Turning the card became a small act of choosing how to enter a relationship. The object therefore works as a doorway: language carries Mei from one social setting into another while preserving the difference between them.

That comparison is a *metaphor*, which treats one thing as another to create meaning. The card is not literally a doorway, but Mei's repeated turning of it supports the comparison. The meaning comes from her context-based choices, not from the physical card alone.
