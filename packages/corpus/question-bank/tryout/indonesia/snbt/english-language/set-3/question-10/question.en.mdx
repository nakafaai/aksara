Three bus stops on the same neighbourhood street served different routes, yet their information boards looked almost identical. Passengers often joined the wrong queue and then asked a driver to redirect them. Rather than adopting the loudest suggestion, the organisers first observed the stops for one week. During each observation period, a mean of 28 passengers chose the intended stop without asking for help.

The team next added a small map showing the usual walking time between each stop and nearby landmarks. On matched trial periods, the mean rose to 44; periods using the original board reached 30. The same routes, timetables, and staffing levels were maintained, and the boards were switched between stops so that one location would not always receive the new design.

| Stage | Mean passengers choosing the intended stop without asking |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 28 |
| Trial | 44 |
| Comparison | 30 |

The result supported a limited extension, not an immediate permanent rollout. A map labelled with a single walking time could still mislead someone who moved more slowly, avoided stairs, or needed a step-free route. Passengers and drivers therefore recommended showing distance and accessibility information alongside time. In the report, *wayfinding* meant information that helps a person understand where they are and how to continue, a broader aim than merely making the board more noticeable.
