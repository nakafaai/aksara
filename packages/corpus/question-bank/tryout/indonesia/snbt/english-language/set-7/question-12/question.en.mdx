Priya brought the first flood-warning draft to a residents' workshop. It included rainfall estimates, drainage notes, three phone numbers, the history of the warning system, and the safe route. Every detail had a reason to be there, yet the route was buried halfway down the page. People who knew the plan could find it; a new reader might not.

She rewrote the warning in two sentences. The first named the street and expected water depth. The second gave the safe route and the time residents should leave. Priya then handed it to a neighbour who had not attended the planning meeting and asked him to explain what he would do next.

The neighbour identified the route without asking for clarification, but he also pointed out that one landmark was difficult to recognise at night. Priya added a clearer reference point and scheduled another test. The exchange replaced assumed clarity with evidence and showed that shortening the message did not end the review process.

In her final note, Priya used *cognitive load* to mean the mental effort required to hold and process information at one time. Removing details that did not change the immediate action reduced what a reader had to process before locating the route. The lesson was not that detail is unimportant, but that urgent information must make the next action easy to find.
