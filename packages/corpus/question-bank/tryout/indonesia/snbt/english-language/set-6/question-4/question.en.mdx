A biology class used a colour-changing reaction as an indirect measure of enzyme activity. The students wanted to know whether holding the reaction mixture at <InlineMath math="37^{\circ}\text{C}" /> would change the score recorded after five minutes. They kept sample volume, pH, reaction time, and the amount of enzyme solution the same across conditions.

| Condition | Mean colour-change score |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | <InlineMath math="18" /> |
| With the change | <InlineMath math="31" /> |
| Comparison | <InlineMath math="19" /> |

The <InlineMath math="37^{\circ}\text{C}" /> condition produced a mean score of <InlineMath math="31" />, while the baseline and comparison means were <InlineMath math="18" /> and <InlineMath math="19" />. This supports an association between that temperature and a higher colour-change score under the classroom conditions. It does not show that <InlineMath math="37^{\circ}\text{C}" /> would give the same result for every enzyme or experimental system.

The largest measurement weakness came from the scale itself. Students matched each sample to a printed colour chart by eye, so two observers could assign slightly different scores to the same sample. The class proposed repeating the comparison with more samples and an instrument that measures colour intensity numerically.

The report defined an *enzyme* as a biological catalyst that speeds a reaction without being consumed. That definition identifies the process represented by the colour change. It does not turn an eye-estimated score into a precise concentration or prove that temperature alone caused the difference.


What role does the definition of *enzyme* play in the passage's reasoning?
