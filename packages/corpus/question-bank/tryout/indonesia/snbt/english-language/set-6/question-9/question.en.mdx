At a community repair café, volunteers often began a job and then had to leave the workbench to find a missing tool. Visitors waited, shared tools became difficult to track, and some repairs were not completed before closing. During an initial week, the organisers recorded a mean of 14 repairs per session that were completed without a return trip for a missing tool.

Visitors, volunteer repairers, tool lenders, and venue staff helped design a short preparation card. Before starting, a repairer selected the type of repair and checked the listed tools. The organisers alternated sessions using the card with sessions using the previous process. The trial mean was 25 completed repairs without a missing-tool return, compared with 15 in the comparison sessions. Staffing and schedules remained stable.

| Stage | Mean value |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 14 |
| Trial | 25 |
| Comparison | 15 |

The figures supported a limited extension of the card, but they did not justify a permanent rule yet. Replacing a loose plug and rebuilding a damaged appliance require very different time, skill, and tools. A longer trial would need to group results by repair difficulty rather than treat every object as equivalent.

In the report, *product-life extension* meant keeping a product useful for longer through maintenance or repair. The term explains why completed repairs matter, but it does not show that every attempted repair extends a product's life by the same amount.
