A biology class used a colour-changing reaction as an indirect measure of enzyme activity. The students wanted to know whether holding the reaction mixture at 37°C would change the score recorded after five minutes. They kept sample volume, pH, reaction time, and the amount of enzyme solution the same across conditions.

| Condition | Mean result |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 18 |
| With the change | 31 |
| Comparison | 19 |

The 37°C condition produced a mean score of 31, while the baseline and comparison means were 18 and 19. This supports an association between that temperature and a higher colour-change score under the classroom conditions. It does not show that 37°C would give the same result for every enzyme or experimental system.

The largest measurement weakness came from the scale itself. Students matched each sample to a printed colour chart by eye, so two observers could assign slightly different scores to the same sample. The class proposed repeating the comparison with more samples and an instrument that measures colour intensity numerically.

The report defined an *enzyme* as a biological catalyst that speeds a reaction without being consumed. That definition identifies the process represented by the colour change. It does not turn an eye-estimated score into a precise concentration or prove that temperature alone caused the difference.
