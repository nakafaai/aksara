Researchers examined the evolutionary history of <InlineMath math="1{,}311" /> species of songbirds found across sub-Saharan Africa and the Palaearctic region, which includes Europe and much of northern Asia. They asked whether movement between regions with different pathogen communities was related to the evolution of genes involved in immune recognition.

Their phylogenetic analysis found that colonization from sub-Saharan Africa, where pathogen diversity is generally higher, into the Palaearctic occurred up to <InlineMath math="20" /> times more often than movement in the opposite direction. Long-distance migration also evolved <InlineMath math="3" /> times more often from African resident lineages than from Palaearctic resident lineages.

The researchers then compared diversity in major histocompatibility complex class I, or MHC-I, genes. These genes help the immune system recognize pathogens. Species that had colonized the Palaearctic from Africa, as well as African lineages that evolved long-distance migration to breed in the Palaearctic, had lower MHC-I diversity.

The authors proposed an evolutionary trade-off. Greater MHC-I diversity may allow recognition of a wider range of pathogens, but it may also increase the risk of harmful immune reactions. Moving into a region with a different pathogen community could therefore alter the balance between those benefits and costs over many generations.

The study concerns comparative evolutionary patterns. It does not show that an individual bird immediately loses immune genes when it migrates, nor does it establish health advice for humans.
