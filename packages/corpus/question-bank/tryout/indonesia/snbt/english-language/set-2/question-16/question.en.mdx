Open-plan offices remove many walls and doors, often with the aim of making communication easier and using space more flexibly. Enclosed offices provide more visual and acoustic privacy but may create stronger physical boundaries between coworkers. Each design solves some problems and creates others, so the best choice depends on how people use the space.

One study analyzed a large occupant-survey database across five office layouts, from private offices to open rooms with few partitions. Private offices received the highest overall workspace-satisfaction ratings. In open-plan settings, noise and loss of privacy were major sources of dissatisfaction, and the reported ease of interaction did not offset those disadvantages.

A second study measured what happened when two corporate headquarters changed to more open layouts. Using wearable sensors and electronic communication records, the researchers found that face-to-face interaction fell by about <InlineMath math="70\%" /> in both cases, while email and instant-message activity increased. The result challenged the assumption that removing walls automatically produces more face-to-face collaboration.

These studies do not prove that one layout is best for every organization. They examine particular workplaces and outcomes. A sound design decision should consider the tasks people perform, their need for concentration and privacy, how teams communicate, and evidence collected after the space is occupied.
