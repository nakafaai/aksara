An environmental science class built a channel model to test a removable filter for floating plastic fragments. The students compared the usual mesh with a mesh that had smaller openings. Water volume, fragment size, flow rate, and collection time were kept constant, and the outcome was the percentage of target fragments recovered at the end of each run.

| Condition | Mean result |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 38 |
| With the change | 71 |
| Comparison | 40 |

The smaller mesh collected a mean of 71% of the fragments, compared with 38% at baseline and 40% in the comparison condition. This is evidence that the smaller openings improved collection in the model. It does not establish how the filter would behave in a river or drain.

Every test fragment was clean, similarly shaped, and made from one type of plastic. Environmental debris can include leaves, grease, sand, fibres, and plastics of different densities. A finer mesh might collect the target fragments yet also clog quickly or trap material that should pass through.

The report used *selectivity* to mean the ability to collect one group of particles while allowing others to pass. The next trial would use controlled mixtures of target plastic and non-target debris, record both collection and blockage, and retain the original flow controls. That would test real selectivity rather than collection of one material alone.
