At a night market, stall permits explained how food and general waste should be separated, but their symbols did not match the symbols printed on the collection bins. Stallholders could follow one instruction correctly and still deliver a bag to the wrong stream. During an initial week, a mean of 36 bags per observation period reached the intended collection stream.

Stallholders, visitors, cleaners, and waste collectors helped redesign the signs. The team placed the same symbol on a stall's permit, its preparation-area guide, and the destination bin. It then alternated evenings using the matched system with evenings using the existing materials. The trial mean was 54 correctly sorted bags, compared with 37 in the comparison condition. Staffing and collection schedules remained stable.

| Stage | Mean value |

The result justified further testing, not permanent adoption after a few evenings. Visitor numbers and the kinds of food sold changed from night to night, which also changed the amount and composition of waste. The follow-up therefore needed more evenings and separate records for different market conditions.

In the report, *system alignment* meant designing different parts of a process to work together consistently. Matching symbols matter because the disposal decision begins at the stall and continues through collection. Improving the final bin alone would leave the earlier conflicting instruction in place.
