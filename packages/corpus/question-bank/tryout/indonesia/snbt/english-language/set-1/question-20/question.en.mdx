Marine and coastal ecosystems support communities through many kinds of benefits. To examine those benefits in one place, researchers collaborated with Hui Aloha Kīholo at Kīholo Bay in Hawaiʻi. They combined ecological surveys with year-long surveys of fishing activity, catch, and the movement of seafood from fishers to other people.

The study estimated an annual catch of about <InlineMath math="7{,}353\text{ kg}" />. That amount could provide roughly <InlineMath math="30{,}500" /> meals and had a market-equivalent value of <InlineMath math="\$78{,}432" />. Most of that value supported household consumption and other noncommercial uses.

The distribution data reveal why sales alone give an incomplete picture. About <InlineMath math="58\%" /> of the catch was kept, <InlineMath math="33.5\%" /> was given away, and only <InlineMath math="8.5\%" /> was sold. About <InlineMath math="20\%" /> of the seafood was used for social or cultural events, linking the fishery to food security and social cohesion.

The authors argue that participatory research can reveal nutritional, economic, social, and cultural values that conventional commercial records overlook. They present the method as useful for other resource-dependent communities, while the findings from one bay do not prove that every reef fishery has the same catch or distribution pattern.
