Yoga usually combines physical postures, breathing techniques, and meditation or relaxation. Styles vary in intensity and purpose, so research does not support treating yoga as one uniform practice or as a cure for every condition.

Studies suggest that yoga may support general wellness by helping people manage stress and by improving sleep, balance, and mental or emotional health. It may also ease some kinds of pain, including low-back and neck pain. However, the size of the benefit and the quality of the evidence differ across outcomes.

Physical postures can train flexibility, strength, and balance. These gains may support daily movement, but yoga has not been shown to prevent every disease, and its effects can be similar to those of other forms of exercise. A health claim should therefore match the evidence for the specific condition and population.

For mental health, yoga may help some people manage anxiety symptoms or depression, especially as an addition to established care. Evidence is weaker for diagnosed anxiety disorders and several other conditions. Yoga should not replace assessment or treatment by a qualified mental health professional.

Yoga is generally considered safe for healthy people when performed properly, but injuries can occur. Beginners should learn from a qualified instructor and avoid extreme practices. Older adults, pregnant people, and those with health conditions may need modifications and should discuss their needs with a health-care provider and instructor.

*(Adapted from the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health's review of [yoga effectiveness and safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yoga-effectiveness-and-safety).)*
