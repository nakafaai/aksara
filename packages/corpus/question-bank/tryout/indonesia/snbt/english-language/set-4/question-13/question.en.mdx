Omar's accessibility map looked complete while it lay flat on his desk. At the community sports centre, however, two arrows disappeared behind a noticeboard and a drinks machine at the busiest corners. He could have adjusted the drawing from memory and called the task finished. Instead, he admitted that the map had not yet been tested from the position of the people expected to use it.

Omar asked Amina, a wheelchair basketball player who visited the centre each week, to test the route with him. As they moved through the building, she marked each point where a sign vanished from her line of sight. Omar checked the same locations from wheelchair height. Their notes did not settle every accessibility question, but they turned a general concern into two observable failures.

The planning team moved both signs before the final map was printed. Omar still had to explain the evidence and document what remained untested; asking Amina to participate did not transfer that responsibility to her. He concluded that a map's accuracy on paper matters less if its intended reader cannot act on the information in the building.

In his final note, Omar used *co-design* to mean designing with people rather than merely designing for them. The term is supported by Amina's role in identifying failures and shaping the revision. It would not be supported if she had only approved a finished map after every decision had already been made.


Which conclusion follows by combining at least two details without going beyond the evidence?
