At a local-history display, visitors often repeated a caption's technical terms but could not describe the object in front of them. Curators, teachers, and accessibility advisers proposed different remedies. Before changing the display, the team observed it for one week and found that a mean of 17 visitors per period could accurately describe one object after reading the existing caption.

The next phase alternated two conditions. Trial captions first named a visible feature and then identified the curator's interpretation; comparison periods retained the original text. A mean of 29 visitors described an object accurately during trial periods, compared with 18 during comparison periods. Opening hours, object placement, and staffing remained unchanged.

| Stage | Mean visitors accurately describing one object |
| --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 17 |
| Trial | 29 |
| Comparison | 18 |

The figures justified extending the trial, but not claiming that the revised captions worked equally well for every visitor. No screen-reader user had been included, and the test measured immediate descriptions rather than later recall. The report used *plain language* for wording that can be understood without specialist knowledge. It did not use the term to mean that historical uncertainty or technical detail should be removed.


Which conclusion follows by combining at least two details without going beyond the evidence?
