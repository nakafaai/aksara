Beside a carved wooden tool in a local museum stood a blank caption card. The curator had removed a dense technical label but delayed replacing it, worried that a shorter opening would make the object seem less significant. Visitors passed the empty card without stopping, so the attempt to protect the tool's importance had left it with no accessible point of entry.

Eli suggested one sentence: “Look at the worn edge where a hand held this tool.” He placed the card beside the detailed label instead of treating the two forms of explanation as competitors. The invitation asked visitors to begin with visible evidence before reading the specialist account.

Visitors began pointing to the worn edge and discussing how the tool might have been held. Several then read the longer label to test their guesses. The curator kept both captions and asked Eli to draft another observation prompt. What had been a sign of hesitation became an invitation to look closely.

This change concerns *tone*, the attitude a speaker or writer conveys toward a subject. The technical label addressed readers from an expert distance, while “Look at” directly welcomed them into an act of observation. The warmer tone did not simplify the evidence itself; it changed how readers were invited to approach it.
