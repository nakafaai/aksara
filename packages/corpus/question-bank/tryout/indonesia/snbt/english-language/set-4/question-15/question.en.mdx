Omar entered a community sports centre intending to review an accessibility map. The plan had seemed manageable from a distance, but one problem appeared immediately: the printed map looked clear from a desk but failed at two crowded corners. For a moment, abandoning the task felt easier than exposing what was not working.

Instead of hiding the difficulty, Omar tested the route at wheelchair height and marked every place where a sign disappeared from view. This step did not solve the entire project. It made the next decision visible and allowed another person to respond to it.

As a result, the planning team moved two signs before printing the final version. The experience changed how Omar understood responsibility and support. The reflection was simple: a map is useful only when its reader can act on it in the real setting.
