(1) In a sports-equipment loan room, return shelves were marked only with letter codes, so balls, pads, and rackets were often placed in the wrong locations.

(2) During the first seven days, a mean of 24 items per session were returned to the correct shelf.

(3) The result could depend on the types of equipment borrowed or on staff assistance, so the team formed only a hypothesis about label clarity.

(4) During trial sessions, each shelf displayed a photograph of the equipment alongside its existing letter code.

(5) During comparison sessions, the shelves displayed only the old codes.

(6) Opening hours, staff numbers, and transaction volume were kept as similar as possible.

(7) The mean number of correctly returned items reached 33 in trial sessions, compared with 24 at baseline and 26 in comparison sessions.

(8) Several borrowers also said that the photographs were quicker to understand, but the team did not record whether they were new or returning users.

(9) The benefit of photo labels therefore could not yet be generalised beyond the short trial.

(10) The team planned a longer test that would separate new and returning borrowers under the same measurement rules.


Consider the following four summary sentences, which have been placed out of order.

(1) In that trial, a mean of 33 items were returned correctly, above the comparison mean of 26.

(2) However, because the trial was brief, the team planned a longer repetition under the same measurement rules.

(3) On the basis of that hypothesis, the team tested photo labels.

(4) The baseline produced a value of 24 and was used to form a hypothesis, not to establish a cause.

Which order forms the most coherent summary?
