(1) During an open laboratory tour, many visitors watched demonstrations without speaking because the general invitation to “ask anything” felt too broad.

(2) During the first seven days, a mean of 18 visitors per session asked at least one question.

(3) The number of visitors asking a question could depend on the demonstration topic, group size, and presenter style, so the baseline did not establish a cause.

(4) During trial sessions, every table displayed a card with two opening questions specific to its demonstration.

(5) During comparison sessions, presenters kept using the previous general invitation.

(6) Demonstrations, presenters, visit length, and group size were kept the same in both conditions.

(7) Visitors asking a question reached a mean of 27 in trial sessions, compared with 18 at baseline and 20 in comparison sessions.

(8) Presenters found the questions more focused, but the count did not measure question quality or visitor understanding.

(9) The team therefore limited its conclusion to the higher number of visitors asking a question during that short tour.

(10) A follow-up will classify question types and assess understanding while retaining the same group-size rules.

(1) In that trial, a mean of 27 visitors asked a question, above the comparison mean of 20.

(2) However, that count did not measure question quality or understanding, so the team planned to classify question types and assess understanding under the same group-size rules.

(3) On the basis of that hypothesis, the team tested question cards for each demonstration table.

(4) The baseline produced a value of 18 and was used to form a hypothesis, not to establish a cause.
