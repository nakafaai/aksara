# Given

In the growth-mindset passage, the author counters the claim that the intervention is useless because its effect is small with **two objections**: it **"ignores cost"** (taking under <InlineMath math="1" /> hour) and **"averages hide spread"** because gains are larger for some of the most lagging students.

# Asked

The flaw in concluding that growth-mindset interventions are useless merely because their average effect is small.

# Solution

Combine **both objections from paragraphs three and five** so neither cost-effectiveness nor subgroup spread is left out.

> Dismissing the intervention from a small average effect makes two errors at once: judging benefit without weighing low cost, and treating one average as if it applied identically to every student group.

## Evaluating the five candidate flaws

Checking **both cost weighting and subgroup variation** isolates the complete answer.

| Candidate flaw | Match with the author's reasoning | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Judges benefit without weighing cost and treats the average effect as uniform across every student group | Captures both of the author's objections | **Correct** |
| Ignores that the effect is huge for all students | False, the author agrees the average effect is small | **Incorrect** |
| Questions the number of studies in the meta-analysis | False, the author accepts the meta-analysis as evidence | **Incorrect** |
| Assumes it can replace costly reforms | Reversed, the author warns against using it as a replacement | **Incorrect** |
| Blames false growth mindset | False, that concerns classroom misuse, not small-effect critiques | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

The flaw is that **the conclusion judges benefit without weighing cost and treats the average effect as if it applied equally to every group of students**.
