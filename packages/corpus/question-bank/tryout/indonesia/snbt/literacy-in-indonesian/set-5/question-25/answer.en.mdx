Read the statement to be weakened carefully. The writer doesn't say nothing came from Portugal. The writer says keroncong "sebagai jenis musik, dengan susunan alat dan pola iringannya yang khas, tidak dikenal di Portugal" (as a kind of music, with its own line-up and accompaniment pattern, isn't known in Portugal). What's denied is that **the whole kind of music** existed there.

So a weakening finding has to show exactly what's denied: keroncong's line-up and accompaniment pattern already existing in Portugal. A 16th-century Portuguese manuscript describing an ensemble with the same line-up and accompaniment pattern does just that. If the manuscript is right, the kind of music existed before it reached the archipelago, and the writer's statement wobbles.

The other findings only touch things **the writer already grants**, so they weaken nothing.

- The cavaquinho still being played in Portugal is about the instrument. The writer says the plucked instrument came from the Portuguese.
- Portuguese creole words in Tugu songs fit the second paragraph, which says the community kept "bahasa kreol Portugis" (Portuguese creole). The language of the lyrics isn't the line-up or the accompaniment pattern.
- Similar plucked instruments on other islands are again about the instrument spreading. That even fits the idea that the same instrument gives different music in different places.
- The Tugu residents' ancestral traditions say nothing about music in Portugal.

The trap here is thinking every Portuguese trace weakens the writer. The writer has already separated the origin of the instrument from the origin of the kind of music, and only a finding about the kind of music hits the target.

So what weakens the statement most is a 16th-century manuscript from Portugal describing an ensemble whose line-up and accompaniment pattern match keroncong's.
