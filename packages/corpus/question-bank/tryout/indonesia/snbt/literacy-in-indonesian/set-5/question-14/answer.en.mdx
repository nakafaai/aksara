# Given

In paragraph three of **Text A**, the author argues crowding endangers hikers because "most hypothermia cases last year were recorded on the busiest days." This compares only **raw case counts**, ignoring that **far more hikers** are on the mountain on busy days.

# Asked

The finding that, if true, most weakens Text A's safety argument.

# Solution

Compare **per-hiker risk (cases per thousand hikers)** between peak days and quiet days.

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{200} = \frac{5}{1{,}000} \qquad \text{equals} \qquad \frac{8}{1{,}600} = \frac{5}{1{,}000}" />

> If hypothermia cases per thousand hikers are no higher on peak days than on quiet days, higher raw totals merely reflect more hikers on the trail rather than extra danger from crowding.

## Checking the five candidate findings

Testing **whether each finding addresses raw count versus per-capita risk** identifies the direct rebuttal.

| Finding | Targets raw-count vs rate flaw | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypothermia cases per thousand hikers on busiest days are no higher than on quiet days | Yes, directly undercuts crowding as an added danger | **Weakens most** |
| Rescue team once handled <InlineMath math="3" /> evacuations in one day | One event does not prove steady capacity | **Incorrect** |
| Hikers wore inadequate jackets | Adds another factor without ruling out cold-weather queues | **Incorrect** |
| Fog delayed hikers on quiet days | Shows another hazard, not that crowding is harmless | **Incorrect** |
| Litter dropped sharply | Supports the ecological case for a cap | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

Text A's safety argument is most weakened by the finding that **the number of hypothermia cases per thousand hikers on the busiest days is no higher than on quiet days**.
