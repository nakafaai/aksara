First pin down the safety argument exactly. In Text A's third paragraph, the writer says that "justru pada hari-hari terpadat itulah sebagian besar kasus hipotermia tahun lalu tercatat" (it was on the most crowded days that most of last year's hypothermia cases were recorded). From that the writer concludes that crowding puts hikers in danger.

The argument has a weak point: it only counts **cases**, without looking at **how many hikers there were**. On the most crowded days there are far more people on the mountain. It's only natural for there to be more cases too, even if the danger to each hiker is the same.

Compare two made-up days. On a quiet day there are <InlineMath math="200" /> hikers and <InlineMath math="1" /> case. On a crowded day there are <InlineMath math="1{,}600" /> hikers and <InlineMath math="8" /> cases.

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{200}=\frac{5}{1{,}000}\qquad\frac{8}{1{,}600}=\frac{5}{1{,}000}" />

The crowded day records <InlineMath math="8" /> times as many cases, yet the danger per hiker is exactly the same. So if cases per thousand hikers on the most crowded days are no higher than on quiet days, crowding hasn't been shown to add danger. That finding weakens the writer's argument most.

The other findings don't hit that weak point.

- Handling <InlineMath math="3" /> evacuations in one day only touches the sentence about the team's capacity, and one occasion doesn't prove the team can do it every time.
- Inadequate jackets add another cause, but don't show that queueing in the cold plays no part.
- Being held up by fog on a quiet day shows a different danger. The danger from crowding can still exist.
- Less litter is about the ecological argument, and it actually supports the quota.

So what weakens the argument most is the finding that hypothermia cases per thousand hikers on the most crowded days were no higher than on quiet days.
