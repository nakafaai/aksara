# Given

The anchoring passage states three key facts: 1974 participants **thought the number was random** yet it still pulled their guesses, the wheel study **could not distinguish** the two explanations, and among honey vendors the gap was **much smaller yet did not vanish**. It also uses qualified words (*strongest*, *often*), never *only* or *certainly*.

# Asked

All statements that can be validly concluded from the passage.

# Solution

Test each statement while watching closely for **absolute qualifiers** (*only*, *certainly*) that push beyond what the passage states.

> Three statements follow directly from the passage, while the two absolute claims using *only* and *certainly* overstep the text's careful wording.

## Checking all five statements against the text

Matching **every claim to exact wording** isolates the three valid conclusions.

| Statement | Passage evidence | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A number believed to be random can still shift estimates | 1974 participants "thought the number was random" yet it "still pulled their guesses" | **Valid** |
| The wheel experiment cannot show which explanation is right | Both predict the same direction so it "cannot distinguish between them" | **Valid** |
| Among honey vendors, the effect is weaker yet still present | The gap was "much smaller than among students, but did not disappear" | **Valid** |
| Anchoring happens only to people who do not know market prices | False, text says "strongest," and vendors were still affected | **Invalid** |
| Whoever states a high number first is certain to get a higher price | False, text says "often" and gives a way to counter anchors | **Invalid** |

# Answer

The valid conclusions are that **a number believed to be random can still shift estimates**, that **the wheel experiment cannot determine which explanation is right**, and that **among honey vendors the effect is weaker yet still present**.
