This question asks you to apply the logic of the design in the fourth paragraph to a new situation: residents of the comparison clusters start seeing a doctor more often, whatever causes their fever.

The fourth paragraph separates two ways of comparing. The first counts recorded cases. The second compares **the share of fever patients who test positive for dengue**. The text says a change in how often people seek care **"mengurangi pasien demam yang positif dan yang negatif sekaligus, sehingga proporsinya tidak banyak bergeser"** (reduces positive and negative fever patients together, so the share barely shifts). The same logic works when people seek care more often.

Now apply it to the new clinic:

1. In the comparison clusters, more fever patients come in. The dengue-positive patients rise with them, so recorded dengue cases there go up.
2. If you compare case counts, the gap with the release clusters gets wider. The benefit of the releases looks bigger than it really is.
3. Negative patients rise too, so the positive share stays about the same. The comparison of shares is hardly affected.

Why the other options are wrong:

- The share doesn't rise, because positive and negative patients increase together.
- Case counts in the comparison clusters go up, so the benefit of the releases is overstated, not understated.
- The comparison of shares is designed precisely to withstand differences in care-seeking like this.
- Randomisation happened before the clinic opened, so it can't balance a change that comes afterwards. The case-count comparison is still distorted.

So the new clinic makes the case-count comparison overstate the benefit of the releases, while the comparison of positive shares stays fairly safe.
