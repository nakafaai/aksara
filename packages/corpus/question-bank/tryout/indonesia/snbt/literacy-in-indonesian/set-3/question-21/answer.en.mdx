The author's conclusion rests on one step: the line runs to areas producing sugar, coffee and tobacco and ends at a port, so it mainly served **export goods** and their owners. The strongest weakener has to show that the line's direction doesn't reflect who actually used it.

If most of the line's traffic was **ordinary passengers and their goods**, the line was in fact mainly used by residents, not plantation produce. The direction may have been planned for exports, but the users turned out to be different. So who benefited most can't be read from the direction alone.

The other options are wrong.

- Being built by a private company is already in the text and doesn't bear on who benefited most.
- High prices in Europe strengthen the conclusion, by showing how much export goods earned.
- Carrying plantation company staff adds to the plantations' benefits, so it strengthens the author's conclusion.
- The author already grants, in paragraph 4, that towns along the line grew, as a benefit **"tidak direncanakan sebagai tujuan utama"** (not planned as the main aim). This doesn't refute that the biggest gains went to plantation owners.

So the finding that weakens it most is that in the first years most of the line's traffic was ordinary passengers and their goods, not plantation produce.
