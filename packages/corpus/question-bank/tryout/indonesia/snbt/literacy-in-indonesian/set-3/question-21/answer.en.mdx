The author's conclusion rests on one bridge: **the direction of the line reflects who gained most**. The line followed export goods, so plantation owners and trading firms are taken to have gained most. A weakening finding has to break that bridge, **and** its reason has to explain how.

If most of the early freight was actually ordinary passengers, a line pointing at plantation country was mostly used by other people. The direction of the line no longer shows who gained most. Both the finding and the reason hold.

The other options fail on the finding or on the reason.

- Same finding, wrong reason. The text says a private company built the line, and freight doesn't prove who built it.
- Towns growing along the line is something the author already **grants** in the fourth paragraph, without dropping the conclusion. It weakens nothing.
- High prices in Europe would help plantation owners. The reason contradicts the finding.
- Who built the line doesn't touch the question of who gained most from what it carried.

So the best pair is the finding that most early freight was ordinary passengers, with the reason that the line's direction doesn't reflect who used it most.
