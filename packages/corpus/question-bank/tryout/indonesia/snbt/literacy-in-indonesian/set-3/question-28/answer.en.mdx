The student's view rests on one assumption: Rp<InlineMath math="160{,}000" /> had an effect because it was taken as a **hint** about the price. The finding that weakens it most is one in which the number is clearly not a hint, yet the effect is still there.

If an extra group is told that Rp<InlineMath math="160{,}000" /> was drawn by lottery, they have no reason to treat it as a hint. If the prices they write are still almost the same as the second group's, the number's effect doesn't depend on its being taken as a hint. This is the same reasoning as the 1974 wheel experiment, where participants knew the number was random.

The other options are wrong.

- Buying honey at home is a difference between students. Since groups were assigned at random, such a difference isn't a strong explanation, and this option doesn't touch the hint assumption either.
- Prices falling after market information shows that real information has an effect. It doesn't test whether Rp<InlineMath math="160{,}000" /> was taken as a hint.
- Sellers being closer to the market price fits the text's point that anchoring weakens in people who know the real value. It doesn't weaken the student's view.
- A group that hears the number without the question and writes even higher prices doesn't rule out the number being taken as a hint. Those students could still think it was the real price.

So the finding that weakens the student's view most is that a group told Rp<InlineMath math="160{,}000" /> was drawn by lottery still wrote almost the same prices as the second group.
