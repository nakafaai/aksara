# Given

The 2015 meta-analysis finds choice overload most likely when **options are hard to compare**, people **do not yet know what they want**, the **decision is complex**, or people want **minimal effort**.

# Asked

The experimental design with the greatest chance of showing choice overload based on the 2015 meta-analysis.

# Solution

Count **how many 2015 triggers combine at once** in each proposed experiment to find the strongest design.

> Asking new workers unfamiliar with insurance to choose among <InlineMath math="5" /> or <InlineMath math="30" /> multi-term policies triggers three conditions together: hard comparison, high complexity, and unclear preferences.

## Comparing the meta analysis triggers across all five designs

Scoring **each experiment against the meta-analysis conditions** highlights the strongest setup.

| Experimental design | Hard to compare | Complex decision | Unclear preferences | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New workers unfamiliar with insurance choosing from <InlineMath math="5" /> or <InlineMath math="30" /> multi-term policies | Yes | Yes | Yes | **Best design** |
| Repeating the jam tasting | No (easy to taste) | No | Partial | **Weaker** |
| Identical-price, identical-cut T-shirts | No | No | No | **Weaker** |
| Experienced insurance agents picking complex plans | Yes | Yes | No (they know how to evaluate) | **Weaker** |
| Hurried shoppers picking nearly identical bottled water | No | No | Only low-effort pressure | **Weaker** |

# Answer

The strongest design is **asking new workers who do not yet understand insurance to choose from <InlineMath math="5" /> or <InlineMath math="30" /> policies that differ across many terms**.
