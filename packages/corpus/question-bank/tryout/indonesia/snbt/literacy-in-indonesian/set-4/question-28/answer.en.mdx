The 2015 meta-analysis names four conditions that make choice overload more likely: options are **hard to compare**, the person **doesn't yet know what they want**, the decision is **complicated**, or the person **wants to choose quickly**. Look for the case that meets the most of them.

The new employee choosing one of <InlineMath math="30" /> health insurance plans meets three conditions at once. Plans that differ in many terms are hard to compare, an insurance decision is complicated, and a new employee doesn't yet know which plan they need.

The other options are wrong.

- The coffee lover faces <InlineMath math="40" /> brands, but already knows what they want, their usual brand.
- Three packages differing only in price are easy to compare, and there are few options.
- Twelve colours at the same price and in the same style are easy to compare. The decision isn't complicated either.
- The mother buying her usual rice already knows what she wants, and there are no new options to compare.

So choice overload is most likely for the new employee choosing one of <InlineMath math="30" /> health insurance plans that differ in many terms at once.
