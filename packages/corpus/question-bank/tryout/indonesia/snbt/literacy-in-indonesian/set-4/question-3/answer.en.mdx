# Given

One year after deep wells at the port were closed, GPS still recorded **<InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" />** of sinking, leading a council member to call the closure a failure. Yet paragraph two explains that **clay releases water very slowly** so land keeps sinking for years at a slower rate, and previously the port sank <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> per year on average.

# Asked

The statement grounded in the passage that most weakens the council member's conclusion that the well closure failed.

# Solution

Compare the **sinking rate before and after closure** against the slow clay-compaction mechanism described in paragraph two.

> Sinking <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" /> in one year is slower than the prior <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> annual rate and matches slow clay compaction, so one year of data cannot prove failure.

## Evaluating the five responses to the council member

Checking **clay behavior and measurement tools** against the passage removes all four distractors.

| Statement | Alignment with the passage | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slower continued sinking (<InlineMath math="6 \to 4\text{ cm}" />) matches clay releasing water very slowly | Directly undercuts calling one-year data a failure | **Correct** |
| Ground will rebound once well levels recover | False, clay "hardly expands again" | **Incorrect** |
| GPS readings include sea-level rise | False, GPS measures ground motion, tide gauges mix both | **Incorrect** |
| Household wells extract more water per well | Reversed, factories and hotels extract far more per well | **Incorrect** |
| The east coast sinks only <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" /> | Different location, does not explain the port | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

The council member's conclusion is most weakened because **continued sinking at a slower rate fits slow clay compaction, so one year of data cannot yet show that closing the wells failed**.
