# Given

**The passage reports** the <InlineMath math="6" />-versus-<InlineMath math="24" /> jam experiment alongside later studies showing when choice overload appears or fades.

# Asked

Which statements can be concluded from the jam experiment and subsequent research.

# Solution

> Verify the small ratios directly and reject sweeping claims that choice never matters or that the original experiment was wrong.

Two statements need a small calculation, and the rest need careful reading of a key word.

- **Statement 1: Ya (Yes)**. Out of every <InlineMath math="100" /> passers-by, the <InlineMath math="24" />-flavour stand stops <InlineMath math="60" /> and the <InlineMath math="6" />-flavour stand <InlineMath math="40" />. Buyers are <InlineMath math="3\% \times 60 = 1.8" /> and <InlineMath math="30\% \times 40 = 12" />. More stop, but **fewer buy**.
- **Statement 2: Tidak (No)**. <InlineMath math="60\% : 40\% = 1.5" />. The <InlineMath math="24" />-flavour stand stops **one and a half times**, not twice, as many visitors.
- **Statement 3: Tidak**. The average was **"hampir nol"** (almost zero), but in some experiments more choice cut buying and in others it raised it. So choice still has an effect.
- **Statement 4: Ya**. Overload is more likely **"ketika orang belum tahu apa yang ia inginkan"** (when people don't yet know what they want). Conversely, people who already know are less likely to face it.
- **Statement 5: Tidak**. The author writes that the jam experiment is **"tidak keliru"** (not wrong), it just can't be made a general rule.

# Answer

The table below summarizes the verdict for each statement.

| Statement | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| More stop, fewer buy | Ya |
| Twice as many stop | Tidak |
| Choice never has an effect | Tidak |
| Knowing what you want makes overload less likely | Ya |
| The jam experiment was wrong | Tidak |
