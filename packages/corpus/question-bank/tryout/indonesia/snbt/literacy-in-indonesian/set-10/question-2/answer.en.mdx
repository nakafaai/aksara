# Given

An observation group at an estuary on Sumatra's east coast recorded two paired counts at different tides:

- **At high tide** on pond embankments: counts dropped from <InlineMath math="1{.}200" /> to <InlineMath math="450" /> birds.
- **At low tide** on the mudflat: counts shifted slightly from <InlineMath math="1{.}100" /> to <InlineMath math="1{.}050" /> birds, while mudflat area stayed unchanged.
- A sandbar across the estuary lies roughly <InlineMath math="6" /> kilometers away.

# Asked

The most defensible conclusion from comparing the high-tide and low-tide counts alongside the roosting explanation.

# Solution

Combine both tidal counts with the author's explanation of roosting energy costs.

- **Birds still feed at the estuary**. Low-tide feeding counts barely moved, so the birds have not abandoned the estuary as a feeding ground.
- **Roosting site shift**. The sharp drop appears only on the embankments at high tide, showing that **many birds still feed there but roost elsewhere**.
- **Energy cost**. The second paragraph notes that when a roost is far away, **"part of the newly stored reserves is spent flying back and forth"**. With a sandbar roughly <InlineMath math="6" /> kilometers away, that fat-reserve drain is a real risk.

Check why the other options fail:

| Distractor | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Population fell by more than half | Dropping from <InlineMath math="1{.}200" /> to <InlineMath math="450" /> reflects one roosting spot, not the overall population. |
| The road reduced mudflat prey | The text never states prey declined, and the mudflat area remained unchanged. |
| Disturbance causes no harm | It captures the low-tide count but ignores the flight cost to a distant roost. |
| Mudflats shrank like in the Yellow Sea | The passage explicitly states the estuary's mudflat area did not change. |

> A sharp drop at one high-tide roost does not prove population decline when low-tide feeding counts stay nearly unchanged.

# Answer

The safest conclusion is that **birds still feed at the estuary while many roost elsewhere**, which can **drain their fat reserves** if the new roost is far away.
