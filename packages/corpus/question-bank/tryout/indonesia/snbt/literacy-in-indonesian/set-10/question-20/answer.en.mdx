# Given

The question lists five candidate conclusions about **replacing subsidies** of Rp<InlineMath math="100" /> trillion in subsidies with Rp<InlineMath math="1{,}2" /> million cash transfers to the bottom two quintiles (costing Rp<InlineMath math="24" /> trillion), alongside the economist's warning about indirect effects beyond the table.

# Asked

All conclusions supported by the passage, the subsidy table, and the economist's warning.

# Solution

Test each conclusion against the **table calculations** and the **economist's caveat**:

| Conclusion | Verdict | Evidence from text and table |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom-quintile households receive more based on table figures | **Supported** | Cash aid of Rp<InlineMath math="1{,}2" /> million exceeds their lost Rp<InlineMath math="800{.}000" /> subsidy. The qualifier *based on table figures* matters because outside price effects exist. |
| Most of the budget remains | **Supported** | Cash aid spends Rp<InlineMath math="24" /> trillion out of Rp<InlineMath math="100" /> trillion, leaving Rp<InlineMath math="76" /> trillion. |
| Share of total subsidy alone cannot gauge burden | **Supported** | The bottom quintile gets only <InlineMath math="8\%" /> of total subsidy, yet that equals <InlineMath math="4\%" /> of its budget, tied for the highest relative burden. |
| Top quintile is unaffected | **Not supported** | They lose the largest per-household subsidy of Rp<InlineMath math="4" /> million and receive no cash transfer. |
| Every aid recipient is guaranteed to come out ahead | **Not supported** | The second quintile only breaks even on direct figures, before counting possible price increases on other goods. |

> Always pair table arithmetic with the economist's caveats so paper gains are not mistaken for guaranteed net welfare gains.

# Answer

The text and table support that **bottom-quintile households receive more on table figures**, **most of the budget remains**, and **total-subsidy share alone is insufficient to measure burden**.
