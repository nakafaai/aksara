The author's view separates two measures that are easy to mix up: **each person's willingness** and **the chance the victim gets help in the end**. The right option has to be right about both.

- **Each person's willingness**. The author accepts the 1968 result: participants who thought others were listening sought help **far less often**. So the presence of others can lower each person's willingness.
- **The chance the victim gets help**. On the camera footage, the more people there are, the more likely **someone** helps. The author ends firmly: the lesson is **"bukanlah bahwa kerumunan berbahaya bagi korban"** (not that crowds are dangerous for victims).

Three options share the same first part, so the second part decides.

- The first option draws its second part from the first, as if lower willingness in each person must mean the victim gets help less often. The author rejects exactly that leap.
- The author doesn't call the bystander effect a myth. What was exaggerated was the 1964 story, while the effect still shows up in the lab.
- The author doesn't say the effect happens only in the lab. The footage measures something else, whether someone helps in the end.
- The bystander effect weakens in **dangerous** situations, not in crowded places. The text also never says each person's willingness rises as people are added.

So the author's view is that the presence of others can lower each person's willingness to help, but a victim in a crowded place isn't necessarily helped less often.
