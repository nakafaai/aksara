Paragraph two says a student ranked tenth in a very strong class may master the subject better than a student ranked third in another class. That example only works if **classes differ in ability**. If every class had students of the same average ability, a student's position within the class would come close to measuring mastery, and the author's argument would lose its force.

That is the assumption the argument needs: the average ability of students can differ from one class to another.

- The author does not assume that marks mean nothing, and even compares the size of mark gaps between ranks.
- The argument does not depend on whether parents read subject marks. It criticises how parents read the ranking.
- Class size does not affect the comparison between two students from different classes.
- Teachers marking differently could be another reason, but the author's argument stands without that assumption.

So the argument in paragraph two depends on the assumption that average student ability can differ between classes.
