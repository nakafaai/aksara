The text names two things that affect how strong the bystander effect is. The right situation has to meet **both**.

- **Many other people are present**. The effect comes from responsibility being spread across many heads. Someone alone has nobody else to wait for.
- **The danger isn't clear**. The 2011 meta-analysis found the effect **"melemah dalam keadaan yang jelas berbahaya"** (weakens in clearly dangerous situations). So it's strongest when people aren't sure it's an emergency.

The person lying on a crowded platform meets both. There are many people, and they're **unsure** whether the person is ill or just asleep. Everyone can easily wait for someone else to act.

The other situations fail one condition.

- Thick smoke in a shopping centre is a **clear** danger. According to the meta-analysis, the effect weakens in situations like that.
- A pedestrian who is **alone** has nobody else to wait for, so no responsibility is spread.
- A public quarrel is like the incidents in the 2020 study, where someone stepped in in about nine out of ten cases.
- Someone shouting for help in a pool is clearly in danger, and only one lifeguard sees it, so responsibility isn't spread.

So the bystander effect is most likely to show strongly with the person lying on a crowded station platform, when the people around them aren't sure whether they're ill or just asleep.
