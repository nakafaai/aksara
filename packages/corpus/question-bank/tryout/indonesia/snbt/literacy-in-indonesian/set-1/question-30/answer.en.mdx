The text names two things that affect how strong the bystander effect is. A situation has to meet **both**, and more than one answer can be right.

- **Many other people are present**. The effect comes from responsibility being spread across many heads. Someone alone has nobody else to wait for.
- **The danger isn't clear**. The 2011 meta-analysis found the effect **"melemah dalam keadaan yang jelas berbahaya"** (weakens in clearly dangerous situations), because when the danger is real, people realise faster that it's an emergency.

**A person lying on a crowded platform: right**. There are many people, and they're unsure whether the person is ill or just asleep. Everyone can easily wait for someone else to act.

**A man slumped at a full bus stop: right**. A full stop means many people, and they **think he's just tired**, so the danger isn't clear yet. Both conditions hold.

**Thick smoke in a shopping centre: wrong**. The danger is clear. According to the meta-analysis, the effect weakens in situations like that.

**A pedestrian alone: wrong**. There's nobody else to wait for, so no responsibility is spread.

**Someone shouting in a pool: wrong**. The danger is clear, and only one lifeguard sees it, so responsibility isn't spread.

So the bystander effect is most likely to show strongly with the person lying on a crowded platform and with the man slumped at the full bus stop.
