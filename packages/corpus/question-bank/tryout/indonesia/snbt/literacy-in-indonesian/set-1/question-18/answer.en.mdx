# Given

**The table lists** student counts and 2023-2024 attendance percentages for four trial schools alongside non-programme schools.

# Asked

Which statements can validly be concluded from the text and table.

# Solution

> When combining percentages from schools of different sizes, bigger schools pull the combined average more strongly than smaller ones.

One statement needs a calculation, two need a comparison, and one names a cause. Judge each.

The statement about 2023 can be concluded. All four trial schools sit between <InlineMath math="85\%" /> and <InlineMath math="89\%" />, all below the other schools' <InlineMath math="92\%" />.

The statement about combined attendance in 2024 can be concluded, but it has to be worked out with the number of students as weights.

<BlockMath math="\frac{400(93)+100(90)+250(94)+250(91)}{1{,}000}=\frac{92{,}450}{1{,}000}=92.45\%" />

That's more than <InlineMath math="92\%" />. Careful: the plain average of the four percentages is exactly <InlineMath math="92\%" />, which is **not more than** <InlineMath math="92\%" />. The plain average is wrong because it counts a school of <InlineMath math="100" /> students the same as a school of <InlineMath math="400" />.

The statement that lunches raised Kalirejo's attendance by eight points can't be concluded. Kalirejo did rise eight points, but in the same semester the new transport route started passing through that village. The rise can't all be credited to the lunches.

The statement about Tegalsari and Watugede can be concluded. Both rose <InlineMath math="2" /> points, while schools without the programme rose <InlineMath math="1" /> point.

**The table below summarizes the verdict for each statement**.

| --- | --- |
| In 2023 every trial school was below the other schools | Ya |
| Combined attendance in 2024 is above <InlineMath math="92\%" /> | Ya |
| Lunches raised Kalirejo's attendance by eight points | Tidak |
