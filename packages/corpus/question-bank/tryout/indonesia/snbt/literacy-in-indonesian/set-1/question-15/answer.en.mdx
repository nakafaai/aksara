# Given

**Text B** proposes classroom phone lockers during lessons, whereas **Text A** backs an all-day ban from first bell to dismissal and highlights that students went back to talking during breaks instead of staring at screens.

# Asked

The objection the author of Text A is most likely to raise against Text B's classroom-locker proposal.

# Solution

Compare the **time scope of both policies** and check which supporting reason actually appears in Text A, since several options share a similar opening clause.

> Classroom lockers leave break time uncovered, erasing one of Text A's cited benefits: students talking with one another during breaks.

## Checking each objection against Text A

Verifying **the cited reason in each choice** reveals the only objection faithful to Text A.

| Proposed objection | Alignment with Text A | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lockers miss break time, yet Text A counts students talking during breaks as a benefit of the ban | Matches Text A's report that students resumed chatting during breaks | **Correct** |
| Exam gains came specifically from break time | Wrong, Text A ties exam gains to the overall ban | **Incorrect** |
| Notifications distract students during breaks | Wrong, Text A says notifications pull attention "from lessons" | **Incorrect** |
| Text A endorses turning phones off during class | Wrong, Text A says that compromise "fails in practice" | **Incorrect** |
| Text A only cares about phones in pockets during lessons | Wrong, Text A defends a full-day ban | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

Text A's author would most likely object that **classroom lockers do not cover break times, even though Text A counts students talking again during breaks as one of the ban's outcomes**.
