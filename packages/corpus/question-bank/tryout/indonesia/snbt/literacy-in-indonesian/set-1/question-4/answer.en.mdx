# Given

**Biocide is assumed** not to affect stone, so group I minus II isolates scrubbing and group II minus III isolates moss loss.

# Asked

Which conclusion the table best supports when comparing added wet-dry cycles and added surface loss from both effects.

# Solution

> Subtract groups that differ by just one step so the effect of scrubbing and the effect of losing moss stand out cleanly.

This needs four differences from the table, then one comparison. Scrubbing alone shows up in the gap between groups I and II, and lost moss alone in the gap between groups II and III, since the question assumes the biocide itself doesn't affect the stone.

| Effect | Extra cycles per year | Extra surface loss |
| --- | :---: | :---: |
| Scrubbing alone (I minus II) | <InlineMath math="110-85=25" /> | <InlineMath math="0.8-0.5=0.3" /> mm |
| Lost moss alone (II minus III) | <InlineMath math="85-60=25" /> | <InlineMath math="0.5-0.3=0.2" /> mm |

Both add **the same number** of cycles, <InlineMath math="25" /> a year. But scrubbing adds **more** surface loss, <InlineMath math="0.3" /> mm against <InlineMath math="0.2" /> mm. If the damage came only from wet-dry cycles, the extra damage should be about the same. So scrubbing seems to cause damage in another way too, for example the brush grinding the stone surface.

The other options are wrong.

- Scrubbing doesn't add more cycles. The increase is the same, <InlineMath math="25" /> a year.
- The same number of cycles doesn't guarantee the same damage. The table shows <InlineMath math="0.3" /> mm and <InlineMath math="0.2" /> mm.
- The fourth option flips the figures. Scrubbing, not lost moss, adds more surface loss.
- The group with the most cycles, group I, is also the most damaged. Cycles and surface loss fall in the same order across the three groups.

# Answer

So the best-supported conclusion is that scrubbing and lost moss each add <InlineMath math="25" /> cycles a year, but scrubbing adds more surface loss, so scrubbing seems to cause damage in another way too.
