This experiment was designed with only one difference between two groups of children. A supported conclusion has to **come from that difference**, not from anything else.

Both groups got the same drawing activity and the same promise. The only difference: for some children the promise was kept, for others it wasn't. Then the results differed sharply: about <InlineMath math="12" /> minutes of waiting against about <InlineMath math="3" /> minutes. Since the children weren't picked for their self-control, a gap that size is best explained by **having just been let down**. The fourth paragraph confirms it: waiting time "sebagian mencerminkan penilaian anak tentang seberapa dapat dipercaya dunia di sekitarnya" (partly reflects the child's judgement of how trustworthy the world around them is).

That conclusion says "not only", and that choice of words fits. The experiment doesn't say self-control plays no part. It shows something else also has a say.

The other conclusions go beyond the experiment.

- The experiment measured one treat test right after a disappointment. There's no data about "for good".
- Even the children who were let down still waited about <InlineMath math="3" /> minutes, and the other group waited about <InlineMath math="12" /> minutes. Children clearly can wait.
- The drawing materials were only what was promised before the treat test. The experiment didn't compare how much children liked drawing materials and treats.
- The experiment didn't measure intelligence.

So the best-supported conclusion is that how long a child waits can depend on the child's experience of whether adults' promises can be trusted, not only on the ability to hold back.
