# Given

In the **2013 experiment**, two groups of children did the same drawing activity and received the same promise with **only one difference**: adults kept their promise for one group and broke it for the other. Children whose promise was kept waited about **<InlineMath math="12" /> minutes**, versus about **<InlineMath math="3" /> minutes** for disappointed children.

# Asked

The conclusion best supported by the 2013 experiment.

# Solution

Draw a conclusion directly from **the single variable manipulated** (whether adult promises proved reliable), keeping within what a single lab session can show.

> How long a child waits can depend on their experience of whether adults' promises can be trusted, not solely on innate self-control.

## Checking the five conclusions against the experiment design

Testing **what was actually varied and measured** removes every overreach.

| Conclusion | Fit with the 2013 experiment | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wait time can depend on experience of whether adult promises are trustworthy, not only on self-control | Directly matches the single manipulated factor and careful "not only" framing | **Correct** |
| Disappointed children stop trusting promises forever | Goes beyond one short lab measurement | **Incorrect** |
| Disappointed children cannot wait at all | False, they still waited about <InlineMath math="3" /> minutes | **Incorrect** |
| Children prefer drawing supplies over treats | Preference between the two was never tested | **Incorrect** |
| Kept promises raise children's intelligence | Intelligence was not measured | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

The best-supported conclusion is that **how long a child waits can depend on their experience of whether adults' promises can be trusted, not solely on their ability to restrain themselves**.
