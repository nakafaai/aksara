The head of the tourism office looks at the falling occupancy rate. This question asks about something else: **the number of rooms occupied**. So first turn both occupancy rates into numbers of rooms, then work out the increase.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{Peak season 2023}&=1{,}000\cdot96\%=960\text{ rooms}\\
\text{Peak season 2024}&=1{,}500\cdot80\%=1{,}200\text{ rooms}
\end{aligned}" />

The increase is <InlineMath math="1{,}200-960=240" /> rooms. A percentage increase is always compared with **the starting state**, the <InlineMath math="960" /> rooms in 2023.

<BlockMath math="\frac{240}{960}=\frac14=25\%" />

This shows the heart of the passage: occupancy falls by <InlineMath math="16" /> points, yet occupied rooms rise by a quarter. The reason is that the number of rooms grows from <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> to <InlineMath math="1{,}500" />, a <InlineMath math="50\%" /> rise, faster than the occupied rooms grow.

The other answers come from the wrong denominator or the wrong measure.

- <InlineMath math="24\%" /> comes from dividing <InlineMath math="240" /> by the number of rooms in 2023, <InlineMath math="1{,}000" />, rather than the rooms occupied.
- <InlineMath math="20\%" /> comes from dividing <InlineMath math="240" /> by <InlineMath math="1{,}200" />, the end state.
- <InlineMath math="16\tfrac{2}{3}\%" /> is the relative fall in occupancy, <InlineMath math="16:96" />. That's a different measure, and it goes down.
- <InlineMath math="16\%" /> is the gap between the two occupancy rates in points, <InlineMath math="96-80" />.

So the number of rooms occupied per night in Karangwangi's peak season rose by <InlineMath math="25\%" />.
