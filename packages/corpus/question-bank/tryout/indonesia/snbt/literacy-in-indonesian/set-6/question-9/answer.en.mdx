The two kites are set against each other through **one thing only**: how each handles a wind that changes. So the meaning of the contrast has to come from how change is handled, not just from price or material.

The fibreglass dragon has a frame that "tidak mau melentur" (won't bend). When the wind turns, it "hanya tahu tegak atau jatuh" (only knows upright or falling). There's no middle way for it. The white bamboo kite is called "banyak maunya" (fussy), but when the wind changes, it "miring dulu" (tilts first). Tilting means bending for a moment, then finding a new balance. Kakek's advice completes it: "Jangan ditarik. Ikuti saja." (Don't pull. Just follow it.)

The contrast also lives inside the character. The narrator starts out like the dragon: sure, upright, and pulling hard when things change. By the end the narrator bends, changing a judgement and offering to hold the bamboo. So the two kites picture **two ways of facing change**: staying stiff until you fall, or bending and then adjusting.

The story doesn't support the other readings.

- The dragon is expensive, but it's the dragon that falls. The story doesn't let the expensive thing win.
- The story doesn't let technology win either. The fibreglass frame is what fails when the wind turns.
- The wind is only the test for both kites. What's contrasted is the kites, not the wind.
- Kakek isn't arrogant. Kakek praises the dragon with "Bagus" (nice) and doesn't mock it after it's lost.

So the contrast between the two kites pictures two ways of facing change: staying stiff until you fall, or bending and then adjusting.
