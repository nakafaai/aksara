First set Text B's worry out as a chain. A minimum wage has to be paid for every hour the app is on. So the company wants to control when and for how long the app may be switched on. As a result, numbers are capped and part-time drivers are pushed out. A weakening finding has to **break one link of that chain**.

The finding about that city breaks the chain in two places. First, the minimum wage there is counted only for the hours when drivers carry an order. Counted that way, the company bears no cost for an app left on without orders, so it has no reason to control hours or cap drivers. The second link collapses. Second, the feared result really didn't happen: the number of part-time drivers stayed the same. So a minimum wage doesn't have to end in the caps Text B fears.

Note that this finding doesn't weaken Text A. It only shows that there's a form of minimum wage that doesn't bring the result Text B fears.

The other findings don't touch the chain.

- Drivers working more than <InlineMath math="10" /> hours back Text A's complaint about long hours. That doesn't touch Text B's worry about caps.
- Drivers who are also teachers or employees fit Text B's writer's own story. If anything, it shows how many people could lose a place.
- Commission rising without notice is a problem both writers accept.
- Part-time drivers carrying fewer orders makes them look less profitable to the company. That strengthens Text B's worry.

So the finding that weakens it most is the city whose minimum wage is counted only for the hours drivers carry an order, where the number of part-time drivers stayed the same.
