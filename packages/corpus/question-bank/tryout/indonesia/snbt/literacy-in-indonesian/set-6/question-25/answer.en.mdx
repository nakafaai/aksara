The writer's view rests on one reason: living things "menanggapi cuaca yang benar-benar terjadi" (respond to the weather that actually happens). If some of those living things turn out **not to respond to the weather**, their signs won't shift, and the writer's view wobbles.

The finding about plants that sprout by day length hits that reason dead centre. Day length is set by the sun's cycle, just like the position of the stars. It doesn't change from year to year, however much rain falls. Plants like that will sprout at the same time every year, even if the rain comes late. So signs from those plants are as rigid as signs from the stars, and the writer's sorting in the fourth paragraph **doesn't hold for all living things**.

The other findings don't touch that reason.

- Young farmers not knowing the names of the seasons is about passing knowledge on, not about how living signs respond to weather.
- Official forecasts sometimes missing is about a different tool. It even fits the writer's advice that the two should check each other.
- The calendar also using signs from birds only adds another example. It doesn't say whether birds respond to the weather.
- Farmers' groups keeping records of signs fits the fifth paragraph and contradicts nothing.

So what weakens the view most is the finding that some plants used as signs sprout by day length, which doesn't change from year to year, rather than by rain.
