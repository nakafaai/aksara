# Given

In paragraph six, the author rejects local officials waiting for El Nino certainty before repairing irrigation channels or preparing short-cycle seeds because certainty arrives too late once drought is under way, and **those low-cost steps stay useful even in a normal dry season** (unlike costly, hard-to-reverse actions that merit stronger signs).

# Asked

The unstated assumption that must hold for the author's argument against waiting to work.

# Solution

Focus on the **cost-loss balance** underlying paragraph six: acting early before certainty makes sense only if **the cost of useful low-regret preparation is smaller than the loss from preparing too late**.

> The author's argument holds only if the cost of preparations that stay useful in a normal dry season is smaller than the losses incurred by waiting until severe drought arrives.

## Checking the five candidate assumptions

Testing **logical necessity and consistency with paragraph six** rules out all four distractors.

| Candidate assumption | Fit with paragraph six | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost of preparations useful in a normal dry season is smaller than losses from starting late | Logically required and matches the cheap-vs-costly boundary | **Correct** |
| El Nino forecasts rarely miss | Unneeded because those steps stay useful even in normal years | **Incorrect** |
| Every decision must be made as early as possible | Contradicts the exception for costly hard-to-reverse steps | **Incorrect** |
| Severe drought never happens without El Nino | Directly contradicted by paragraphs four and five | **Incorrect** |
| Certainty arrives early enough to act | That is the officials' hope, not the author's premise | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

The argument depends on the assumption that **the cost of preparations that remain useful during a normal dry season is smaller than the losses suffered when preparation begins only after a dry drought has arrived**.
