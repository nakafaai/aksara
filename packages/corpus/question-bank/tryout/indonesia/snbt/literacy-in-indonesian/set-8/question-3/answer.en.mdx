# Given

Responding to rice-mill workers who feel "used to" the noise, paragraph six suggests that **"losing the feeling of annoyance may actually be a sign that certain frequencies are no longer being heard."**

# Asked

The finding that, if true, most strongly supports the author's response to the workers' remark.

# Solution

Choose evidence comparing **workers who feel used to noise** against **workers still bothered by it** who share the **same tenure** (holding exposure duration equal), showing worse high-frequency thresholds among those who feel used to it.

> Among workers with identical tenure, finding worse high-frequency hearing thresholds in those who feel used to noise directly links perceived adaptation to hearing loss.

## Evaluating the five candidate findings

Checking **exposure control and direct link to perceived adaptation** isolates the strongest support.

| Finding | Links feeling used to noise with hearing loss | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Among workers with the same tenure, those feeling used to noise have worse high-frequency thresholds | Yes, controls tenure and directly backs the author's claim | **Strengthens most** |
| Veteran workers hear worse than new hires | Shows cumulative exposure, ignores feeling used to noise | **Incorrect** |
| Noise bothers workers again after a long vacation | Weakens the author (points to temporary psychological adaptation) | **Incorrect** |
| Complaints drop after adopting earplugs | Reflects lower noise entering the ear, not adaptation | **Incorrect** |
| Mill workers report more tinnitus than office staff | Confounded by noise exposure, misses adaptation | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

The strongest supporting finding is that **among workers with the same length of service, those who feel used to the noise actually have worse high-frequency hearing thresholds**.
