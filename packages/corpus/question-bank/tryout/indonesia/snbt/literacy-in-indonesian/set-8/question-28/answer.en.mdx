The framing effect needs two conditions: **exactly the same information**, differing only in **how it's stated**, for example as gain or loss, or as living or dying.

**<InlineMath math="90\%" /> chance of survival and <InlineMath math="10\%" /> risk of death: fits**. <InlineMath math="90\%+10\%=100\%" />, so both state the same thing from opposite sides. The text also gives this example.

**<InlineMath math="75\%" /> fat-free and <InlineMath math="25\%" /> fat: fits**. <InlineMath math="75\%+25\%=100\%" />. Same information, different frame.

**<InlineMath math="200" /> saved and <InlineMath math="400" /> die out of <InlineMath math="600" />: fits**. This is programmes A and C from the experiment.

**A <InlineMath math="95\%" /> vaccine and an <InlineMath math="80\%" /> vaccine: doesn't fit**. The figures differ, so the information differs too. That's not framing.

**A Rp<InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> cut and a <InlineMath math="10\%" /> cut: doesn't fit**. They're equal only if the price is exactly Rp<InlineMath math="100{,}000" />. Without the price, the information isn't necessarily the same.

So the pairs with the same information in different frames are the surgery's survival chance and risk of death, the meat's fat-free share and fat share, and the residents saved and dying under the same programme.
