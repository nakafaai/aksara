This asks for two measures with different divisors. **Total contributions** are divided by the pay of all <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> employees, while **average contribution per participant** is divided by the participants only. Since everyone earns the same, you can work in "percent of one person's pay".

**Total contributions as a percent of all pay**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{before}&=\frac{400\times6}{1{,}000}=2.4\%\\
\text{after}&=\frac{750\times3+150\times6}{1{,}000}=\frac{2{,}250+900}{1{,}000}=3.15\%
\end{aligned}" />

**Average contribution per participant**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{before}&=6\%\\
\text{after}&=\frac{3{,}150}{900}=3.5\%
\end{aligned}" />

Total contributions rise because participants go from <InlineMath math="400" /> to <InlineMath math="900" />, but each participant saves less on average.

The other options are wrong.

- The first option is right about the total, but the average per participant falls, it doesn't rise.
- The second option mixes up the two measures. <InlineMath math="6\%" /> and <InlineMath math="3.5\%" /> are the average per **participant**, not total contributions as a share of all pay.
- <InlineMath math="4.05\%" /> comes from assuming all <InlineMath math="900" /> participants save <InlineMath math="4.5\%" />, the midpoint of <InlineMath math="3\%" /> and <InlineMath math="6\%" />, then multiplying by <InlineMath math="900:1{,}000" />. But only <InlineMath math="150" /> people chose <InlineMath math="6\%" />, so the midpoint doesn't represent the participants.
- Total contributions do change, from <InlineMath math="2.4\%" /> to <InlineMath math="3.15\%" />. The rise in participants outweighs the fall per participant.

So total contributions rise from <InlineMath math="2.4\%" /> to <InlineMath math="3.15\%" /> of all pay, while the average per participant falls from <InlineMath math="6\%" /> to <InlineMath math="3.5\%" />.
