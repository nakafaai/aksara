Text B gives two ratios to turnover: wages about <InlineMath math="40\%" /> and net profit about <InlineMath math="5\%" />. To keep it simple, call the turnover <InlineMath math="100" /> units.

**Step 1**. Work out the extra wage cost.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{old wages}&=40\\
\text{extra wages}&=10\%\times40=4
\end{aligned}" />

**Step 2**. The extra cost comes out of profit, because turnover and other costs stay the same.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{new profit}&=5-4=1\\
\text{fall in profit}&=\frac{5-1}{5}=80\%
\end{aligned}" />

**Step 3**. To get profit back to <InlineMath math="5" />, turnover has to rise by <InlineMath math="4" /> units. Since the number of loaves sold stays the same, the extra turnover can only come from price.

<BlockMath math="\text{price rise}=\frac{4}{100}=4\%" />

The other options swap the base of comparison.

- A <InlineMath math="10\%" /> fall in profit treats the profit fall as equal to the wage rise. But profit is far smaller than wages, so the extra <InlineMath math="4" /> units hit profit much harder.
- A <InlineMath math="10\%" /> price rise treats the price rise as equal to the wage rise. Wages are only <InlineMath math="40\%" /> of turnover, so prices only need to rise <InlineMath math="4\%" />.
- A <InlineMath math="20\%" /> fall is <InlineMath math="1:5" />, the share of profit that's **left**, not the share lost.
- A <InlineMath math="40\%" /> price rise uses the wage share, not the extra cost.

So net profit falls <InlineMath math="80\%" />, and bread prices would need to rise <InlineMath math="4\%" /> to bring profit back.
