The key is in the second paragraph: inorganic mercury **"tidak mudah masuk ke jaringan hewan"** (doesn't easily get into animal tissue). The danger starts when bacteria at the **calm, oxygen-poor bottom** of a body of water turn it into methylmercury. So what matters isn't only how much mercury there is, but also where it settles.

- **The reservoir**. It receives mercury from the mine river, and its bottom is calm and oxygen-poor. That's where bacteria are most likely to make methylmercury, even though total mercury in its water is lower.
- **The fast river**. Total mercury is high, but most of it is still inorganic and the water isn't calm.
- **The clear lake**. It gets no water from the mine river, so it has far less mercury to start with.

Three options point to the reservoir, so the reason decides.

- Inorganic mercury doesn't get into animals easily. Lots of it in the fast river doesn't automatically contaminate the fish.
- The question says total mercury in the reservoir water is **lower** than in the river near the mine.
- Calm water alone isn't enough. The lake gets no mercury from the mine, and the text never says calm water always produces the most methylmercury.
- The question says nothing about the age or size of the reservoir fish.

So plant-eating fish are probably most contaminated in the calm reservoir, because its oxygen-poor bottom mud lets bacteria turn mercury into methylmercury.
