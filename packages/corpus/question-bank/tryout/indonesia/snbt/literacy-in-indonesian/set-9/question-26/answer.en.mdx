# Given

In the **Abraham Wald** example, armor was recommended for the areas with the fewest bullet holes (engines) because **"planes hit in the engine most likely never returned, so they were never recorded."**

# Asked

Why Wald advised armoring the least-holed sections, and which finding would most weaken his reasoning.

# Solution

Pair **Wald's survivor-bias reason (engine-hit planes fail to return)** with **evidence that directly breaks his core assumption** (downed planes also turned out to be hit mostly in the fuselage and wings, not the engines).

> Wald reasoned that planes hit in the engine rarely made it home, and that reasoning would weaken most if downed planes also had most hits on the fuselage and wings.

## Checking reason and counter finding across the options

Matching **both Wald's premise and the direct test on downed aircraft** selects the exact answer.

| Option | Accurate Wald reason | Finding truly weakens reason | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Planes hit in the engine tend not to return, weakened if downed planes were also hit mostly on fuselage and wings | Yes | Yes (undercuts where fatal hits occurred) | **Correct** |
| Total count of returning planes | Yes | Wrong (says nothing about fatal hit locations) | **Incorrect** |
| Claims enemies rarely hit engines | Wrong ("enemy fire does not aim that neatly") | - | **Incorrect** |
| More holes on returning fuselages | Yes | Actually matches Wald's original observation | **Incorrect** |
| Comparing armor weight on engines vs fuselage | - | Not discussed in the passage | **Incorrect** |

# Answer

Wald reasoned that **planes hit in the engine tended not to return**, and his reasoning would weaken most if **downed planes turned out to be hit mostly in the fuselage and wings as well**.
