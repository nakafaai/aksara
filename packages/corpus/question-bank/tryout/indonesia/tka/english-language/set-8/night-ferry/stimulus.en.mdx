The last ferry to Pulau Kecil left at nine. Rani and her grandmother reached the harbor at five minutes to nine, out of breath, with a basket of mangosteens and a sack of rice. The ferry was already full. A deckhand shook his head and began to untie the rope.

Rani's grandmother did not argue. She set down the basket, took out two mangosteens, and held them up to the deckhand. “For your children,” she said. “We will wait for the morning boat.” The young man hesitated, then laughed and took them.

Rani was annoyed. Her cousin's wedding was the next morning, and the morning boat would arrive only an hour before the ceremony. She sat on the sack of rice and watched the ferry's lights shrink across the black water.

Ten minutes later, a small wooden fishing boat bumped against the pier. The fisherman called out to her grandmother by name. He was the deckhand's father, and his son had phoned him from the ferry. “An old woman with a basket of mangosteens,” the son had said. “Don't let her sleep on the pier.”

The fishing boat was slow and smelled of diesel and salt. Rani held the rice sack between her knees and watched the stars. Her grandmother dozed against the side of the boat, smiling a little.

They reached the island before midnight. As Rani helped carry the basket up the beach, she noticed it was two mangosteens lighter. For the first time, she wondered whether her grandmother had given them away hoping for something in return, or simply because she always did, and whether that was exactly why it had worked.
