Before our nature club cleaned Karang Seruni Beach in March, we wrote our predictions on the bus. Almost everyone guessed that plastic bottles would top the list and that the stretch in front of the food stalls would be the dirtiest. Bu Ratih, our adviser, handed out tally sheets and said that nothing went into a sack until it had been counted. Fajar groaned that counting rubbish was "a job for people with nothing better to do" and signed up to carry sacks instead.

The beach is uneven, so our stretches were too: <InlineMath math="100\,\text{m}" /> at the river mouth in the north, <InlineMath math="300\,\text{m}" /> along the fishing harbour and <InlineMath math="200\,\text{m}" /> in front of the stalls at the southern end. Each team walked its stretch from the waterline to the sea wall and counted only pieces larger than a bottle cap.

The table shows the first day's final counts, and it broke both predictions. Since a longer stretch collects more simply by being longer, Bu Ratih made us compare the stretches for their length as well. That evening we settled on a neat explanation: visitors drop wrappers at the stalls, and the current carries them north until they pile up against the rocks at the river mouth.

The explanation did not survive the next morning. Pak Darto, a fisherman mending nets at the harbour, told us that from December to April the current along this shore runs south, away from the river mouth. He also pointed at the stringy weed wrapped around many of our sachets. It was water hyacinth, a river plant that cannot live in the sea. A fortnight later, after heavy rain in the hills had turned the river brown and fast, we counted the river-mouth stretch again and found <InlineMath math="783" /> items. The stall stretch, counted the same day, had barely changed.

Fajar surprised us more than the numbers did. On the first evening, while the rest of us waited for the bus, he noticed that two river-mouth sheets did not agree and stayed on the sand until dark, counting the sacks again. After Pak Darto's visit, he asked to lead the second count. We have sent both counts to the office of Lebakrandu, the first village upstream, where families with no rubbish collection leave their bags on the riverbank. A cleanup moves rubbish from sand to sacks. Only the count told us where it comes from.
