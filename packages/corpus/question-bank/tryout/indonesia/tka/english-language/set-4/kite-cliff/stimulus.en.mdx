The summer I turned twelve, I stayed with Grandfather in his village above the sea, where he made kites for the tourists who came to the beach. On my first day he gave me a lesson before he gave me a kite. "A kite always flies downwind of the hand that holds it," he said. "If the wind comes from the sea, your kite goes over the land behind you. Never forget it, or one day you will stand in the wrong place and lose your string."

The village itself was so still that afternoon that the washing hung straight down from the lines. Yet when we climbed to the cliff path, my kite jumped out of my hands and climbed at once. Grandfather laughed at my face. Every afternoon, he explained, the sea breeze runs up the face of the cliff, while the houses and the palms keep the village below in its own calm.

Two days later, under a perfectly clear sky, he told me to carry the drying kites inside. An hour later, rain came down so hard that the lane turned into a stream. I asked how he had known. He pointed at the swallows. "When they fly low over the fields, the rain is coming," he said. "They follow the insects, and the insects come down before a storm."

On the fifth day my string snapped, and my kite sailed off over the roofs. We found it that evening in Bu Tini's mango tree, tail tangled in the leaves, not even torn.

On the last afternoon, Grandfather took down the old red kite that had belonged to his brother, who had been dead for many years. The wind came off the sea from the west, as it did every afternoon, and the flags on the fishing boats below streamed towards the village. Grandfather let the red kite go. It rose, steadied, and hung far out over the water to the west, its long tail pointing back at us. The wind must have turned for a moment, I told myself. But the flags on the boats were still streaming towards the village. Grandfather did not say a word. He let the kite fly until the sun touched the sea, then wound in the string very slowly.
