The Egret Hide stands at the end of a wooden boardwalk in the Sedgewater Wetland Reserve, facing a shallow lagoon where herons, egrets, and kingfishers feed. The boardwalk is about two hundred meters long. Its last fifty meters are lined on both sides with screens of woven reed, so people walking toward the hide cannot be seen from the water. A small sign where the screens begin asks visitors to stop talking so that their voices do not disturb the birds.

The hide has two levels. The ground floor is a long, dim room with a wooden bench facing the lagoon. Instead of windows, its front wall has a row of narrow viewing slits at the eye level of a seated adult. Each slit has a hinged wooden flap. Visitors lift a flap only after they sit down and lower it again before they stand up, because birds on the water can notice movement through an open slit.

Beside the ground-floor door, a painted board shows the outlines of twelve birds that are often seen from the hide. A logbook lies on a shelf under the board. Visitors write down each bird they identify, the time, and how many they counted. Every Monday, a ranger copies the new entries into the reserve's records.

A wooden stair leads to the upper platform. The platform has no walls, only a waist-high rail, so visitors can see the whole lagoon and the mudflats beyond it. Large flocks arrive to feed in the first hour after sunrise, when the platform is busiest. Because birds can easily see anyone standing up there, visitors are asked to sit on the low benches and move slowly. The platform is closed whenever the wind is strong.

The ground floor is the most rewarding place for a first visit. Herons often hunt in the reeds directly in front of the slits, only a few meters from the bench.
