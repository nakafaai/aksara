Last semester, I joined the gamelan club at my school because my older sister had played in it. I was given a saron, a row of metal keys that you strike with a mallet. For two weeks, I practiced the melody at home every evening by tapping it on a book. By the first full rehearsal, I could play it from memory without a single wrong note.

Playing it with eleven other people was a different matter. Each time the music sped up, I was already ahead, and when it slowed down, I was still rushing. Twice the whole group had to stop because of me. I decided that the others were not keeping a steady beat, so I counted louder in my head and struck my keys even harder.

After the second stop, Bu Ratna, our teacher, gently took my mallet and asked me to watch Fajar, who played the kendang drum. In our club, the drummer chooses the speed and signals each change with his hands before the group follows. She told me to play the next piece without counting at all, only watching Fajar and listening for the big gong at the end of each long cycle.

It felt strange to stop trusting my own counting. During the piece, Fajar leaned forward and played a quick pattern. I slowed down with everyone else, and for the first time my notes landed together with the other sarons. Two weeks later, at the school's anniversary concert, we played three pieces without a single stop.

I still practice at home, but now I tap along to a recording of the whole group instead of practicing alone. Knowing my part was only half of the job. The other half was knowing whom to follow.
