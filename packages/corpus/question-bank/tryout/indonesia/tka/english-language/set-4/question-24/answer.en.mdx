The claim compares surfaces, so the supporting detail must show **the size of the shade effect on each surface**. A single temperature cannot do that. You need the difference between full sun and tree shade for each row of the table.

Subtract the shade value from the full-sun value for each surface, in degrees Celsius:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Asphalt road: } 54 - 35 &= 19 \\ \text{Concrete sidewalk: } 47 - 32 &= 15 \\ \text{Grass strip: } 36 - 29 &= 7 \end{aligned}" />

The two paved surfaces became <InlineMath math="19" /> and <InlineMath math="15" /> degrees cooler in the shade, while the grass became only <InlineMath math="7" /> degrees cooler. That pattern is exactly what the claim says: shade made the biggest difference on paved surfaces.

Check that the detail matches the claim and not just the table. The claim is about the difference that shade makes, so the detail must compare shade with full sun on more than one surface, and **only one option makes that comparison**.

The best support is that shade lowered asphalt by <InlineMath math="19" /> degrees and concrete by <InlineMath math="15" />, but grass by only <InlineMath math="7" />.

- "The measurements were taken at 1 p.m. on a single clear day in September." This tells when the data were collected. It says nothing about which surface changed most.
- "Asphalt in full sun was the hottest of all the surfaces that were measured." This is true, but it compares surfaces in the sun only, so it does not show how much shade cooled them.
- "Some large trees already grow along Jalan Melati, where the office took its measurements." This explains where the shade came from, not how large its effect was.
- "The concrete sidewalk measured <InlineMath math="32" /> degrees under the trees at the time of the test." A single shaded value cannot show a difference without the matching value in full sun.
