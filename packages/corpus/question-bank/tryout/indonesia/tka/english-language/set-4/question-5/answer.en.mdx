# Given

The **[GAP]** in paragraph three sits directly **between** the transport office's first trial of <InlineMath math="90" /> free minutes and the result sentence explaining that **busiest-district stations stood empty by mid-morning because bikes stayed out for hours**.

# Asked

Which numbered sentences could fill **[GAP]** without contradicting anything in the text.

# Solution

Think of **[GAP]** as a bridge linking policy to consequence. Any valid sentence must **explain how the <InlineMath math="90" />-minute allowance kept bikes out for hours and drained busy stations in the morning**.

> A gap-fill sentence must agree with both the experiment introduced before it and the exact outcome described immediately after it.

## Checking each candidate sentence

The sentence right after **[GAP]** says <Highlight>Stations in the busiest districts then stood empty by mid-morning, because bikes that should have come back after one trip stayed out for hours</Highlight>.

| Sentence | Verdict | Fit with surrounding text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Fits | Riders holding a bike all morning for errands directly explains why bikes stayed out for hours and emptied busy stations. |
| II | Contradicts | If riders barely used the extra time, bikes would not have stayed out for hours. |
| III | Fits | Taking a bike at breakfast until lunch is another clear example of morning bikes staying out for hours. |
| IV | Contradicts | Stations overflowing with returned bikes is the exact opposite of <Highlight>stood empty</Highlight>. |

## Why the other options fail

- **Options including II or IV** directly clash with empty stations and multi-hour bike use.
- **I only** overlooks that sentence III fits the exact same cause-and-effect bridge just as smoothly.

# Answer

The sentences that can fill **[GAP]** without contradicting the text are **I and III only**.
