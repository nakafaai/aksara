When a phone stops charging or a laptop screen flickers, most teenagers in Rantauwangi do one of two things: they pay for a repair they do not understand, or they buy a new device. Secondary schools should offer a third option. A short course in basic repair would save families some money, but its real value lies elsewhere: it would teach students to diagnose a problem before they spend anything on it.

The evidence from a pilot at SMA Harapan Bangsa is encouraging, though limited to one school and one semester. During that semester, <InlineMath math="64" /> students met for <InlineMath math="60" /> minutes every second Friday. Classmates and teachers brought them <InlineMath math="120" /> broken devices. The students repaired <InlineMath math="71" />, judged <InlineMath math="30" /> not worth repairing and passed the remaining <InlineMath math="19" /> to a licensed technician, because the pilot's rules did not allow them to touch those faults. In a survey at the end, most participants called the course the most practical lesson they had taken that year.

Critics raise two objections: the course is unsafe, and it takes time away from examination subjects. The first objection deserves a serious answer. Lithium batteries can catch fire when punctured, and some chargers carry mains voltage. In the pilot, however, a student could open a device only after a teacher had checked that its battery was not swollen, and no work was done on anything plugged into the wall. No injuries were recorded during the semester.

It is tempting to think that the course worked because students learned to swap parts, and most of the <InlineMath math="71" /> repairs were indeed part swaps that followed printed guides. The teachers who ran the pilot drew a different lesson: the <InlineMath math="30" /> devices left unrepaired had taught as much as the <InlineMath math="71" /> that were fixed. For each of those <InlineMath math="30" />, a student compared the price of a part with the value of the device and explained the decision to its owner. Learning when to stop, the teachers concluded, was the skill students were least likely to pick up anywhere else.

As for examination subjects, <InlineMath math="2" /> hours a month is a small price for a habit that lasts a lifetime. Schools that adopt the course should keep the pilot's two safety rules, and they should judge it by more than the number of devices fixed. A course that turns out confident students who open every swollen battery would be worse than no course at all.
