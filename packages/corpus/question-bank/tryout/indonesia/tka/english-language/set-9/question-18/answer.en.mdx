# Given

Bread rolls baked one evening turn out **small and heavy**, even though they were baked for the full **<InlineMath math="15" /> minutes at <InlineMath math="180" /> degrees** exactly as step five instructs.

# Asked

Which Roman-numeral details from the instructions could explain this outcome.

# Solution

Small, heavy rolls point directly to **insufficient rising**, so select only the instructions whose violation slows or kills yeast or cuts rising time short.

> Only steps affecting yeast activity or rising time explain heavy, dense rolls, never an already-correct baking step or equal portion sizing.

## Checking each instruction detail

| Statement | Explains heavy rolls? | Link to dough rising |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | **Yes** | Below <InlineMath math="24" /> degrees you must <Highlight>allow twice as long</Highlight>, so stopping at warm-room timing cuts the rise short. |
| II | **Yes** | Water above <InlineMath math="45" /> degrees <Highlight>kills the yeast</Highlight>, leaving nothing to lift the dough. |
| III | **Yes** | Direct salt contact with yeast <Highlight>slows it down</Highlight>, weakening the rise. |
| IV | No | The question states the rolls already baked for the full <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes at <InlineMath math="180" /> degrees. |
| V | No | Dividing into <InlineMath math="12" /> equal pieces controls even sizing between rolls, not whether the dough rises. |

## Why the other options fail

- **Options without I or III** leave out a direct cause of weak dough rise.
- **Options with IV** blame a baking step that the baker already followed accurately.
- **Options with V** treat uniform shaping weight as a cause of dense, unrisen dough.

# Answer

The details that could explain the small, heavy rolls are **I, II, and III only**.
