When Pak Hendra walked into Class 9B instead of Bu Lina, the room filled with whispers. He was young, wore a faded batik shirt, and carried a cardboard box instead of a bag. Everyone knew what a substitute teacher usually meant: copying pages from the textbook while the teacher looked at his phone.

Pak Hendra put the box on the desk and asked a question nobody expected. “Who here has ever been lost?” A few hands went up. Fikri, who sat at the back and rarely spoke, said he had once taken the wrong angkot and ended up at the other end of town.

“Good,” said Pak Hendra, and opened the box. Inside were old maps of the city, a compass, and a bundle of string. For the next two hours, the class worked in groups to plan routes between places on the map, measuring distances with the string and checking directions with the compass. Fikri's group, guided by his memory of that wrong angkot, found a shortcut that nobody else had noticed.

At the end of the lesson, Pak Hendra wrote the page numbers of the geography chapter on the board. “This is what you learned today,” he said. “Now you can read it and recognize it.”

Bu Lina came back the following week. On her desk she found a note from the class, signed by everyone, asking whether Pak Hendra's maps could stay in the classroom. Fikri was the one who had written it.
