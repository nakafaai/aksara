On the second Saturday of October, <InlineMath math="5" /> of us from the Rindang Shorebird Watch met on the sand spit at the mouth of the Rindang River for the club's yearly count of waders flying south. Our figures go to a regional survey that compares sites and years. Nadia Siregar, our leader, would not let anyone start until the incoming tide had covered the stone marker at the end of the spit, about <InlineMath math="2" /> hours before high water. Rising water pushes the birds off the outer mudflats, she explained, so they pass the spit in a stream narrow enough to follow.

Ilham and I counted birds flying south across the line between the marker and the old lighthouse, while Rara and Dimas counted birds flying north across it. In each pair, one person clicked and the other wrote. A flock bigger than about <InlineMath math="50" /> birds was counted in blocks of <InlineMath math="10" />, because a counter who clicks every bird in a large flock loses her place and almost always ends up too low. At the end of each hour, the writer read the figures aloud, Nadia copied them onto the master sheet, and only then did the partners swap jobs. The last rule was the one I hardly noticed: if counting stopped for more than <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes within an hour, that hour was marked incomplete. Its birds stay on the sheet but are not added to the day's total, because the survey compares only full hours.

The first hour was slow. Then, at 8:25, a flock of sandpipers came in low over the water, and I counted it in tens while Ilham wrote: <InlineMath math="380" /> birds, all heading south. For the rest of the morning, I was sure nothing would beat that hour.

At 9:20, a squall rolled in from the sea. Within a minute the lighthouse had vanished, and Nadia called us into the fishermen's shelter, where we drank tea and watched the rain hammer the flats. When it lifted at 9:45, birds poured south as if they had been waiting, and Ilham clicked faster than I had ever seen him. Nadia wrote "incomplete" beside the hour anyway.

Back at the clubhouse, Nadia subtracted the northbound birds from the southbound ones, since a flock that turns back and crosses the line again would otherwise be counted twice.
