Niko kept the final evening bus timetable pinned above his desk. His mother worked at a clinic across town, and the 9:20 bus was usually her way home. One Tuesday, a storm knocked out several traffic lights. At 9:45, she had not arrived and her phone battery was empty.

Niko read the timetable again as if the printed numbers could explain the delay. His neighbor, Mrs. Chen, reminded him that a timetable described a plan, not the bus's current position. She called the transit information line while Niko checked the official service page on her phone.

The page reported a road closure and said buses were turning around at Central Square. The message did not say when normal service would return. Niko wanted to cycle to the square, but the rain was heavy and part of the route had no streetlights. Mrs. Chen suggested calling the clinic's front desk first.

The receptionist confirmed that Niko's mother was safe and waiting with two coworkers. A hospital shuttle would take them to a station on another line. At 10:30, she called from a coworker's phone and told Niko where she was.

The next day, Niko did not take the timetable down. Instead, he added three notes beside it: live service page, transit phone number, and clinic front desk. The printed schedule still mattered, but it was no longer his only source. He understood that good information is not simply the first information available. During a disruption, it must also be current, specific, and safe to act on.

Which detail best supports Mrs. Chen's concern about Niko cycling?
