# Given

In the closing paragraph of the bird-count account, the writer moves from sulking over the dropped third hour to **accepting why consistent rules beat a bigger raw total** after reviewing last year's master sheet.

# Asked

Which reader responses to the bird-count account are supported by the text.

# Solution

Think of judging audience reactions after a story ends. A supported response must **align with the writer's final understanding and the survey's stated purpose**, rather than an impulse or accusation the text never backs.

> Supported reader responses stay anchored to the writer's settled conclusion and the survey's explicit goal of fair comparison across years.

## Responses supported by the text

- **The writer's view of the third hour changes once she grasps comparability** is supported. Paragraph six reports she <Highlight>sulked a little over the third hour</Highlight> before realizing <Highlight>a smaller total we can compare is worth more than a bigger one we cannot</Highlight>.
- **A total following the same rules every year is more useful than a larger irregular one** is supported. Paragraph one states the numbers feed <Highlight>a regional survey that compares sites and years</Highlight>, and paragraph two explains <Highlight>the survey compares only full hours</Highlight>.

## Why the other three options fail

- **Nadia was too strict** clashes with the ending, where the writer ends up agreeing with Nadia's rule.
- **Ilham's rapid clicking made his figures untrustworthy** misstates why the hour was dropped (the <InlineMath math="25" />-minute squall stop, not counting error).
- **The club should have stayed out in the squall** ignores that the lighthouse vanished within a minute and the text never questions taking shelter.

# Answer

The two supported responses are that **the writer's view of the third hour changes once she understands comparability, and that a total following the same rules every year is more useful to the survey than a larger one that does not**.
