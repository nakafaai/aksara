In March, our class organized a science fair for younger students. I was responsible for signs and room directions. At first, I printed small arrows and placed them at adult eye level. During a test walk, Hana, who uses a wheelchair, pointed out that one arrow was hidden behind a table and another led to a narrow doorway.

We paused the setup and followed every route from the entrance. We lowered the signs, moved two displays, and marked a wider path to the experiment room. Then Amir tried the route while wearing lightly blurred safety glasses. He could see the arrows' shapes but not their pale gray words. We replaced the gray text with dark lettering and added simple symbols.

On the morning of the fair, heavy rain forced us to close the nearest entrance. The alternative door had one step. A portable ramp was available, but it was stored in a locked room. We called the caretaker, opened the room, and assigned one volunteer to keep the ramp area clear.

The fair started fifteen minutes late. I expected complaints, yet most visitors followed the revised route without asking for help. One child used the symbols to find the robotics display before his parent had finished reading the sign.

After the event, we did not write that the fair was fully accessible. We listed what had improved and what still failed, especially the locked ramp and the need for a better rain plan. The delay was inconvenient, but it changed how I understood preparation: a route is not ready just because its designer can use it.

Which option best summarizes the recount?
