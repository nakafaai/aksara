Schools should teach source checking as a normal part of every subject, not as a single warning given once a year. Students encounter claims in science, history, health, finance, and daily conversation. The useful question is not only whether a website looks professional, but whether a specific claim can be traced and tested.

A practical routine can begin with three steps. First, identify exactly what is being claimed. Second, find the original source rather than relying on a screenshot or a repost. Third, compare the claim with another relevant source that has independent evidence. These steps do not guarantee certainty, but they reveal missing context and weak support.

The routine should also include a stopping rule. Students do not have unlimited time, and not every claim carries the same risk. A harmless entertainment detail may need little checking. Medical advice, payment instructions, or a claim that could harm someone's reputation deserves more careful verification and help from a trusted adult or professional.

Some people argue that source checking belongs only in language or computer classes. That separation is unrealistic. A graph in geography, a quotation in history, and a product claim in economics all require students to ask where information came from and what it actually supports.

Teaching the routine across subjects does not mean treating every statement as suspicious. It means matching the depth of checking to the importance of the decision. When teachers model that judgment repeatedly, students can learn both healthy doubt and the ability to stop once reliable evidence is sufficient.
