Maya had practiced her lines for the school play every night for a month. She played the village chief's daughter, and her big moment came in the second act, when she had to warn the villagers about a coming flood.

On the night of the performance, the hall was full. Maya stood behind the curtain, repeating her first line under her breath. When she finally stepped into the light, she opened her mouth and nothing came. The words she had said a hundred times were simply gone. The silence stretched. Somebody in the audience coughed.

Then Maya noticed Tomi, who played the village fisherman, standing beside the painted river. He looked at her, lifted his empty bucket, and said loudly, “Chief's daughter! The river is rising faster than yesterday. What have you seen upstream?”

It was not a line from the script. But it was a question, and Maya knew the answer. “The bridge,” she said. “The water has already covered the bridge.” Her voice shook at first, then grew steady. The rest of her speech came back, one sentence pulling the next behind it.

After the show, Maya found Tomi stacking chairs. “You made that up,” she said.

He shrugged. “You looked like you needed a door. I just opened one.”

Maya laughed, but that night she added something new to her script: Tomi's question, written in pencil in the margin, next to the line she had forgotten.
