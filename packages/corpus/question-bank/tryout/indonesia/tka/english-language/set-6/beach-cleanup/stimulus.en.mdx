On the first Sunday of March, our scout troop joined a beach cleanup at Pantai Kuta Baru. About sixty volunteers met at the parking area at half past six, before the sun became too hot.

The organizer, Kak Dewi, divided us into groups of five. Each group received two sacks, one for plastic and one for everything else, and a sheet for recording what we found. She explained that the data would be sent to a university team that studies where beach waste comes from.

My group worked on a two-hundred-meter stretch near the river mouth. At first I expected to find mostly bottles, but the most common items were much smaller: plastic straws and thin snack wrappers. By nine o'clock we had counted 412 wrappers, 96 straws, and only 23 bottles. We also found a flip-flop, a toothbrush, and a fishing net tangled around a piece of driftwood.

At ten, all the groups gathered to weigh the sacks. Together we had collected 87 kilograms of waste. Kak Dewi pointed out that the stretch near the river mouth had far more rubbish than the stretches farther away. She said this suggested that much of it was carried down the river from the town, not left by visitors to the beach.

On the way home, I kept thinking about the wrappers. I had always thought of beach rubbish as something tourists left behind. Now I wonder how many of those wrappers came from my own neighborhood, upstream.
