Kakek's bicycle was the only thing of his I asked for. It was black and heavy, with a bell that sounded like a cough, and after the funeral I kept it in the yard, chained to the iron gatepost with his old padlock. The chain went through the frame and the back wheel, and I wore the only key on a string around my neck, because Kakek had always said a lock is worth nothing if its key hangs where anyone can reach it.

Strange things happened with that bicycle. Some nights its bell rang, a short cough in the dark, and I lay awake until my father showed me that it only rang when the night lorries went past on the main road and shook the gate. One morning both tyres were hard, though I had left them soft. My sister admitted, after a week of denying it, that she had pumped them so she could borrow the bicycle while I was at school.

On the morning of the hundredth day after Kakek died, I went out to the yard and the bicycle was gone. The chain was still wrapped around the gatepost, the padlock still closed, and the key was still on its string around my neck. My father phoned my uncle, who lived beside the cemetery on the other side of the river. The cemetery is twelve kilometres from our yard. My uncle called back an hour later. The bicycle was leaning against Kakek's grave, its tyres dusty, the bell turned towards the headstone.

Someone must have lifted it over the gate, my uncle said, and ridden it there as a cruel joke. My father nodded. I didn't say anything. That afternoon my uncle brought the bicycle home in his truck, and I chained it to the gatepost again, the chain through the frame and the back wheel, as before.
