# Given

The **narrator's actions** stand out clearly across all four paragraphs: following Kakek's key advice, lying awake at night until the father explains the bell, staying silent when the uncle claims someone lifted the bicycle over the gate, and chaining it the exact same way afterwards.

# Asked

Which numbered statements about the narrator are supported by the story.

# Solution

Test each claim strictly against the narrator's own words and actions. Only **statement II fails**, because while the father nods, **the narrator stays completely silent** despite knowing a locked chain through the frame could never be lifted free.

> Silence is not agreement, especially when the silent character knows the physical facts that contradict the adults' explanation.

## Checking each statement about the narrator

| Statement | Supported? | Direct evidence in the story |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | **Yes** | Kakek warned <Highlight>a lock is worth nothing if its key hangs where anyone can reach it</Highlight>, and the narrator keeps the only key on a string around the neck. |
| II | No | The father nodded, whereas the narrator says <Highlight>I didn't say anything</Highlight>. Silence is not saying yes. |
| III | **Yes** | Stated directly: <Highlight>I lay awake until my father showed me</Highlight> that passing lorries shook the gate. |
| IV | **Yes** | On its return, the narrator locks it <Highlight>the chain through the frame and the back wheel, as before</Highlight>. |
| V | **Yes** | Staying silent despite knowing a closed chain through the frame cannot be lifted over a gate shows clear reason to doubt. |

## Why the other options fail

- **I, II, III, IV, and V** turns the narrator's silence into spoken agreement.
- **Options omitting I or V** leave out either Kakek's key advice or the pointed silence in the final paragraph.

# Answer

The supported statements about the narrator are **I, III, IV, and V only**.
