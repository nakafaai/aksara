The exchange week began on a Monday in March, when <InlineMath math="14" /> of us from SMA Tanjung Laut arrived in Watukabut, a farming town on Mount Lirang. That evening Pak Hendra, our teacher, gathered us in the village hall: take off your shoes at the door, he said, eat what you are offered, and greet the elders with both hands.

I stayed with the Wiratama family. Their daughter Ratri took my bag at the gate, and through the open door I saw her grandmother, Nenek Sumi, by the hearth. I rinsed my hands at the clay jar beside the door, as Ratri did, then stepped inside and held out both hands to her, as we had been told. Nenek Sumi slid her hands into her sleeves, nodded once and turned back to the fire. Clearly, I thought, she did not want a girl from the coast in her kitchen.

On Tuesday and most of Wednesday I kept out of her way and wrote in my journal that Nenek Sumi disliked visitors. Then I watched old Nenek Kasih across the lane take both hands of Bima, a classmate staying with her family. So the elders here did take a visitor's hands, I decided. Nenek Sumi simply would not take mine.

When Ratri asked that evening why I had gone so quiet, I told her. Here, she explained, an elder who takes a guest's hands is promising the town that their household stands behind that guest. A household cannot stand behind someone it has not claimed out loud, so the elder waits until a member of the household has spoken the guest's name at the hearth, and only then offers their hands first. The jar, Ratri added, is only a courtesy. On my first evening she had thought her mother would name me, and her mother had thought Ratri already had. Nenek Kasih's family had named Bima before supper. Before bed, Ratri stood me by the hearth and spoke my full name and my town's name.

The next morning Nenek Sumi held out both hands before I could move. On Friday she walked with me to the last house in town, which an elder does, Ratri said, only for a guest whose hands they have already held. On the bus I crossed out the line in my journal. I had spent Tuesday and Wednesday reading a refusal into a promise not yet made.
