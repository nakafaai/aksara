# Given

Paragraph four notes that a stick pushed into low-marsh soil **smells of rotten eggs**, explains that this smell forms only in airless mud, and immediately describes the low-marsh grass as having **hollow channels** down its stems to its roots.

# Asked

Which numbered conclusions can a reader work out from the rotten-egg smell together with the rest of paragraph four.

# Solution

Picture a diver breathing through a snorkel tube from the air above. When the mud below **contains no air at all** and the plant growing there has **hollow pipes running down to its roots**, you can immediately see why those pipes exist: to deliver air to the roots.

> When a paragraph places an environmental problem right beside a plant's physical feature, the feature explains how the plant solves that problem.

## Checking each conclusion

| Statement | Verdict | Reasoning and paragraph-four evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Supported | Follows straight from the text's definition: <Highlight>a smell that forms only in mud with no air in it</Highlight>. |
| II | Supported | The next sentence states the grass <Highlight>has hollow channels running down its stems to its roots</Highlight>, bridging the air above to roots stuck in airless mud. |
| III | Not supported | The smell signals lack of air rather than extreme salt, and paragraph two points to high-marsh salt pans as saltier than the sea. |
| IV | Not supported | The text explains the smell as airless mud and never links waste or the car park to pollution. |

## Why the other options fail

- **I only** misses the logical bridge to the very next sentence explaining the hollow channels.
- **Options adding III or IV** invent salt or car-park pollution claims that the rotten-egg clue never supports.

# Answer

A reader can work out **I and II only**.
