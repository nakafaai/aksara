Every Friday at SMA Tunas Bangsa, students leave with graded assignments due on Monday morning. The school's planning committee is now asking whether weekends should be kept free of graded homework. They should be, but not because practice is unimportant.

Practice does matter, and nobody on the committee disputes it. Ms Hartono, who teaches mathematics, puts it plainly: "A skill you use once a week fades between lessons. Ten minutes a day keeps it." A grade, however, turns practice into a test, and a test taken at home measures the home as much as the student. A student who shares one phone with three siblings, or who works in the family shop on Saturdays, is graded on time and quiet that she does not have.

The school's own records point the same way. Last semester, <InlineMath math="31\%" /> of weekend assignments arrived late or incomplete, compared with <InlineMath math="12\%" /> of assignments due on weekdays. Yet students who missed a weekend deadline had, on average, the same end-of-unit test scores as students who met it.

Pak Darmawan, who chairs the parents' association, worries that a free weekend will become an empty one. "My son crams for three hours on Sunday night and has forgotten half of it by Wednesday," he says. "I would rather he did a little each evening, but if nothing is due, he will not open a book until Monday." His concern is whether students will practise at all, not whether their weekend practice should be graded.

The answer is to keep weekends free of grades, not free of practice. Teachers can set a short, optional weekend task that is checked in class on Monday without a mark, and move every graded deadline to a weekday. That keeps skills fresh, answers Pak Darmawan's worry, and stops a grade from rewarding a quiet house. It is the only fair way to treat weekend learning.
