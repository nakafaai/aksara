A rain gauge made from a clear plastic bottle can give useful daily readings, but only if its scale is made by testing, not by holding a ruler against the bottle. Rain is reported as a depth: <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" /> of rain means a layer of water <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" /> deep over flat ground. The gauge catches rain in a funnel and stores it in the bottle's straight section, which is narrower than the funnel opening. Water therefore rises faster inside the bottle than rain builds up on the ground.

Cut the top off a <InlineMath math="2" />-litre bottle and fit it upside down as the funnel. The funnel used here has an opening of <InlineMath math="80\text{ cm}^2" />, so every <InlineMath math="8\text{ mL}" /> it collects stands for <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" /> of rain. Before marking anything, pour water into the bottle until it covers the domed base and reaches the straight section. Draw the zero line at that level. This base water stays in the gauge for good, because the dome would otherwise swallow the first few millilitres of every rainfall.

Next, add water in measured pours from a measuring cylinder and mark the level after each pour. Read the cylinder at eye level, at the bottom of the curved water surface. The table shows the second trial, which the group used for its final scale.

The first trial had failed. After five pours meant to total <InlineMath math="40\text{ mL}" />, the water stood only <InlineMath math="8\text{ mm}" /> above the zero line instead of <InlineMath math="10\text{ mm}" />. A leak seemed likely at first, but the outside of the bottle stayed dry and the level did not drop overnight. Evaporation was ruled out as well, because the whole trial took less than five minutes. For the second trial, the group changed only one thing: Dimas, who did the pouring, knelt beside the table instead of standing over the cylinder.

Outdoors, set the gauge on open ground, at least twice as far from any wall or tree as that obstacle is tall, because nearby objects block or redirect rain. Read it at the same time every morning, then pour the water out down to the zero line, never below it. Each reading is the rain that fell since the last emptying. It says nothing about how hard the rain fell at any moment. On hot days, read promptly, because water left in the sun slowly evaporates. Remember that a mistake made while marking the scale spoils every reading the gauge will ever give, while a mistake made on one morning spoils only that morning's reading.
