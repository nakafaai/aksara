To compare the two marshes you need the second and fourth paragraphs together, and **a link the text leaves you to make**.

- High marsh: the sea arrives *only during the highest tides of the year or in storms*. The water that does come *evaporates, leaving its salt behind*, so in dry months the salt pans *can be saltier than the sea itself*.
- Low marsh: *the tide covers twice every day*, and its soil *stays soaked between tides*.

Rare flooding sounds like it should mean little salt. But rare flooding is exactly why the salt piles up: each visit of the sea leaves salt behind, and there's no daily tide to wash it away. So the high marsh floods far less often and can still be saltier.

Why the other options are wrong:

- The high marsh floods less often, not more.
- "Floods less, so always less salt" is the easy guess that the salt pans disprove.
- It swaps the two marshes. The soaked, airless soil belongs to the low marsh, and the white crust to the high marsh's salt pans.
- Spring tides *about twice a month* flood the middle marsh, and the low marsh floods twice a day, not once.

So the high marsh is flooded far less often than the low marsh, yet in dry months parts of it can be saltier than the sea.
