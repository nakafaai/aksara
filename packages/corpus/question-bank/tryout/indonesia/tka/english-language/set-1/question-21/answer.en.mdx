# Given

Paragraph two argues that **grading home tasks reflects home conditions**, and paragraph three backs that up with **school records**: <InlineMath math="31\%" /> of weekend tasks were late or incomplete versus <InlineMath math="12\%" /> on weekdays, yet end-of-unit test scores averaged the same.

# Asked

Which numbered statements belong in a combined summary of paragraphs two and three.

# Solution

Think of paragraph two as the **claim** (homes are unequal) and paragraph three as its **evidence** (more late weekend tasks did not lower test scores). A good combined summary captures both points while rejecting distortions.

> A two-paragraph summary keeps the central point of each paragraph and excludes any statement that reverses the data or invents a new demand.

## Checking each summary statement

| Statement | Verdict | Evidence from paragraphs two and three |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Belongs | Captures paragraph two's core point after *however*: <Highlight>a test taken at home measures the home as much as the student</Highlight>. |
| II | Belongs | Captures paragraph three's data: despite <InlineMath math="31\%" /> versus <InlineMath math="12\%" /> late tasks, students missing weekend deadlines earned <Highlight>the same end-of-unit test scores</Highlight>. |
| III | Excluded | Reverses the school records, which prove late students did not score lower. |
| IV | Excluded | Contradicts the writer's explicit stance in paragraph five to keep weekends <Highlight>free of grades, not free of practice</Highlight>. |

## Why the other options fail

- **I only** omits the entire statistical contribution of paragraph three.
- **Any option including III or IV** adds a claim flatly contradicted by the school records or the writer's thesis.

# Answer

The statements belonging in the combined summary are **I and II only**.
