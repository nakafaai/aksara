A secondary school should test a later start time for one term, but it should treat the change as a measured trial rather than a guaranteed solution. Starting later may give some students more time in the morning, yet the effect depends on transport, family routines, after-school activities, and the time students actually go to sleep.

The strongest reason for a trial is that the current schedule affects many connected routines. A change on paper can move bus times, staff duties, meal preparation, sports practice, and care for younger children. Discussing these effects in advance is useful, but a real trial can reveal problems that a timetable meeting may miss.

The school should define success before the trial begins. It could compare attendance, late arrivals, first-period participation, transport delays, and student reports with the previous term. The comparison should not rely on one popular survey question. Different measures may move in different directions, and the school should report that honestly.

Families and staff also need a way to describe unequal effects. A later bus may help one student but prevent another from collecting a younger sibling. If the school only reports an average, it may hide a serious problem for a smaller group.

Therefore, the proposal is not “later is always better.” It is “test a specific schedule, measure several outcomes, and keep a clear route back if the costs outweigh the benefits.” A reversible trial gives the community evidence for a local decision without pretending that one timetable fits every school.
