Last July, I spent one week helping at a community bicycle workshop. I expected to learn how to replace parts quickly, but the first morning began with a rule: do not touch a bicycle until its owner has described the problem. My mentor, Sari, said that a careful question could save more time than a fast tool.

On the second day, a child brought in a bicycle with a chain that kept falling off. I wanted to replace the chain immediately. Sari asked me to spin the pedals slowly and watch the rear wheel. The wheel moved slightly from side to side because it had not been fitted straight. We adjusted it, cleaned the chain, and tested the bicycle outside. No new chain was needed.

Midweek, I made a mistake. I placed several bolts from two repairs in one tray. Although the bolts looked similar, one set was slightly longer. Sari did not solve the problem for me. She asked me to compare the service notes and measure each bolt. It took nearly an hour to sort them correctly. After that, I used labeled cups for every repair.

On the final day, I helped a new volunteer inspect a noisy brake. Instead of giving an answer, I asked what the rider had noticed and suggested one safe test. I realized that I was repeating Sari's method. I left the workshop with fewer dramatic repair stories than I had imagined, but with a better habit: listen, observe, record, and only then decide what to change.

Which explanation best connects the bolt mistake with the writer's behavior on the final day?
