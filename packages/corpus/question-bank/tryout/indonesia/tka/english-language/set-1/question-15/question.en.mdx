Every morning before class, Mina recorded the weather in a blue notebook. She measured rainfall, drew the clouds, and wrote one sentence about the wind. The notebook belonged to the school garden club, but Mina treated it like a promise. The gardeners used her notes to decide when to cover young plants.

One Monday, the notebook disappeared. Mina searched the science room, the garden shed, and her backpack twice. By lunchtime, dark clouds were gathering. Without the previous week's records, the club could not compare the approaching storm with earlier weather. Mina blamed Arif, who had borrowed the notebook on Friday to copy a diagram.

Arif denied taking it home. Instead of arguing, he helped Mina retrace Friday's route. They remembered carrying seed trays from the classroom to the roof. Near the stairs, they found a wet blue corner beneath a loose drainage pipe. The notebook had fallen behind a storage box and rainwater had blurred several pages.

Mina felt embarrassed about her accusation. Arif suggested drying each page between clean sheets and photographing the readable records. They also created a shared digital copy, but kept the paper notebook for drawings and quick field notes. Before the storm arrived, Mina used the readable pages and current measurements to recommend covering the smallest seedlings.

The next morning, the plants were safe. Mina wrote the day's weather on a fresh page. Under it, she added a second observation: when evidence is missing, the first explanation that comes to mind may be the easiest one, not the fairest one.
