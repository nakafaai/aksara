The boardwalk at Harrow Creek runs <InlineMath math="600\text{ m}" /> from the car park to the edge of the mudflat, and in that short distance it crosses three different marshes. The ground drops by less than <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> along the whole route, yet that small drop decides how often the sea arrives, and how often the sea arrives decides what can grow.

The first stretch crosses the high marsh, which the sea reaches only during the highest tides of the year or in storms. You might expect the rarely flooded ground to be the least salty, but in the dry months the opposite is true. Seawater that does arrive sits in shallow hollows and evaporates, leaving its salt behind, so the bare, white-crusted patches called salt pans can be saltier than the sea itself. Sea lavender grows around their edges. It takes salt in through its roots and pushes it out again through tiny glands on its leaves.

Halfway along, the boards cross the middle marsh, which is flooded only by the spring tides that come about twice a month. Here the ground is covered with glasswort, a low plant whose jointed green stems look like strings of beads. Glasswort does not push salt out. It stores salt in its fleshy stems and holds extra water there to keep the salt dilute, which is why the stems swell after each flooding and shrivel in long dry spells.

The last stretch crosses the low marsh, which the tide covers twice every day. Its soil is so waterlogged that air cannot get in, and a stick pushed into it comes out smelling of rotten eggs. The tall, stiff grass that grows here survives by carrying air down to its roots through hollow channels in its stems. Like sea lavender, it gets rid of salt through glands on its leaves, and on dry afternoons the leaves glitter with tiny crystals.

At the far end, the boardwalk stops above the bare mudflat. The tide covers it twice a day, as it does the low marsh, but for longer, and no plant can root in its shifting mud. Information boards along the route show the plants of each zone, but the best guide is the line of dried seaweed left by the last high tide. Where that line lies on a given day tells you which zones the sea has just visited.
