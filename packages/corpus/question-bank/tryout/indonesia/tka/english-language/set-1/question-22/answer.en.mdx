# Given

Four direct quotations from the weekend-homework essay are tested against **two criteria**: the statement must be an unverified judgement rather than a checkable fact, and it must be **the writer's own voice** stated without hedging.

# Asked

Which numbered sentences state the writer's own judgement as if it were an established fact.

# Solution

Run every quotation through two simple filters: first, is it a **value judgement or untested prediction** rather than checkable data, and second, is it **the writer speaking directly** without hedges like "might" or "should".

> An untested prediction written in flat present-tense verbs presents the writer's judgement as if it were already proven fact.

## Checking each quotation

| Sentence | Verdict | Check against both criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Fits both | Paragraph five states <Highlight>It is the only fair way to treat weekend learning</Highlight>: calling one plan "the only fair way" is an uncheckable value judgement stated flatly by the writer. |
| II | Fits both | Paragraph five claims <Highlight>That keeps skills fresh, answers Pak Darmawan's worry, and stops a grade from rewarding a quiet house</Highlight>: the plan is untried, so presenting these future results as certainties states the writer's prediction as fact. |
| III | Fails criterion one | Paragraph three's <InlineMath math="31\%" /> versus <InlineMath math="12\%" /> comes from verifiable school records. |
| IV | Fails criterion two | Paragraph four quotes Pak Darmawan's personal preference marked by <Highlight>I would rather</Highlight>, not the writer's voice. |

## Why the other options fail

- **I only** overlooks sentence II, where an unverified prediction is written with zero hedging.
- **Any option including III or IV** confuses a checkable school statistic or a quoted parent's preference with the writer's own judgement.

# Answer

The sentences stating the writer's own judgement as established fact are **I and II only**.
