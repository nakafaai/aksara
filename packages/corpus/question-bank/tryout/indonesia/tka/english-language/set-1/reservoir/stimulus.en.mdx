When Laras said the history club wanted a map of Karangsari, the village now under the Sembada Reservoir, Grandmother Warsih turned up the radio. "There is nothing to map," she said. "It is all water." Her family had been moved out in 1987, when she was <InlineMath math="12" />. The dam gates were closed <InlineMath math="2" /> years later, and the valley filled in a single rainy season.

So Laras asked about one thing at a time: the well, then the path to school. Grandmother answered in steps, never in metres. It was <InlineMath math="40" /> steps from the well to the school gate and <InlineMath math="90" /> from the well to the mosque. When Laras drew the mosque west of the school, Grandmother tapped the paper and pointed east, and Laras rubbed it out and moved it.

The next morning, the river road, which Laras had ruled straight, curved around an empty space. Nobody had erased it. The line itself had moved, smudge and all, like a sleeper turning over. "The tamarind," Grandmother said. "The road went around the tamarind." Then she asked for the pencil and drew the tree herself.

In August a drought lowered the reservoir until the top of the mosque's minaret rose out of the brown water. Laras and Grandmother hired a boat. A heron stood on the minaret, and as they drifted closer it called, "Sih, you're late for school," a name only Grandmother's mother had ever used. Grandmother laughed, then cried, then had the boatman row in slow circles while she named the houses that had stood below.

On the water, Laras found her own mistake. The boatman, who had grown up in the next village, knew where the well lay, though not who had lived around it, and the minaret stood far closer to the well than her map showed. Laras had drawn the steps at her own stride, but they had been counted by a girl of <InlineMath math="7" />, in the year she started school.

That night Grandmother planted a tamarind seed she had carried since 1987, and by morning its shoot reached Laras's knee. There were still <InlineMath math="11" /> houses without names, and Grandmother could name a house only from the spot where it had stood, never from the kitchen table. The rains were due in the second week of October. Once the minaret went under, the boatman said, nobody would find those spots again. The club wanted the map by the end of October.
