Above a neighborhood learning center, a small rooftop garden turns an unused concrete space into four distinct zones. Near the stairs, shallow boxes hold mint, basil, and spring onions. Their labels include the planting date and a note asking visitors to rub, not pick, the leaves. A narrow path leads to deeper beds where tomatoes climb bamboo frames. The frames are tied to heavy planters rather than the roof railing, so strong wind is less likely to pull them loose.

The western corner is the hottest part of the roof. It contains hardy flowering plants and two pale water containers. Volunteers painted the containers a light color to reduce heating and covered them with mesh to keep insects and leaves out. On the eastern side, a shade cloth protects young seedlings during the brightest hours. A simple rain gauge stands beside them.

The final zone has no plants. It is a work area with a low table, gloves, clean tools, and a notebook sealed in a plastic box. Gardeners record rainfall, watering, pests, and harvest amounts. They also note failed experiments. One page explains that lettuce wilted because a dark container made the water unusually warm.

From the street, the garden is almost invisible. On the roof, however, every zone shows a different response to sunlight, wind, water, or work. Its value does not come from looking perfectly green. It comes from making small choices visible, so the next volunteer can understand why each part of the garden is arranged as it is.

Classify each feature by its main function.
