On the Thursday the dog did not come, Bima waited under the tamarind by the school gate until Pak Darto, the guard, locked it at twenty to two. "Go home before the rain does," the old man said, and then, more quietly: "The vaccination team was at the market this morning. They catch the strays, inject them, paint a red stripe down their backs and let each one go on the street where they found it, before dark." Bima did not ask which street.

The dog had first appeared in January, on the day of the first heavy rain: a thin, coffee-coloured animal whose left ear folded at the tip. Bima named it Kopi. From then on it waited under the tamarind every afternoon and walked him home, and every afternoon he saved a piece of his fried tempe for it.

In the first week they kept to the main road, past the petrol station. At the crossing there, Kopi once pressed against Bima's knees and would not move, and a second later a motorbike shot through the red light. When Bima told this at dinner, his little sister Laras decided that Kopi was an angel in a dog's body. Their mother only told him to keep away from it.

In the second week Kopi chose another way: a path behind the brick kiln, between the drying racks, a good <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes shorter, that came out at the canal bridge. The kiln owner's geese ran hissing at any child who came near, but when Kopi trotted ahead, low and silent, they scattered. At the canal the dog always stopped. It took the tempe from Bima's hand at the near end of the bridge and sat there, ears up, until he had turned into Gang Melati, his lane.

Pak Darto laughed at the angel. On ordinary days, he said, school ended at one, at one Bu Sarni scraped her rice pot into the drain beside the gate, and at one the dog was there. On Fridays, when the canteen was shut and school ended at eleven, Bima walked out to an empty tamarind. And one Saturday, when the school stood empty as it did every weekend, Pak Darto had come in to water the plants and found Kopi by the dry drain at ten past one.

That Thursday Bima stopped at the mouth of the kiln path. The geese were out among the racks. He went the long way, by the main road, and at the bridge he set the tempe on the post where Kopi used to sit. He did not look back until he reached the corner.
