A fair classroom discussion needs more than a list of speakers. It needs a process that helps participants understand the question, use evidence, and notice who has not yet had a chance to contribute.

Begin by writing one focused question where everyone can see it. Define any term that could be understood in more than one way. Give participants two quiet minutes to write an initial response and one reason. This pause allows people to prepare without competing for the first turn.

Next, invite one person to share. The following speaker should briefly restate the previous idea before adding a different point, question, or piece of evidence. The first speaker may correct the restatement if it changes the meaning. Keep each turn short enough for several voices to enter.

Use a visible participation list, but do not treat it as a score. The facilitator can invite someone who has not spoken, while allowing that person to pass. If two people repeatedly respond only to each other, return attention to the original question and ask for another perspective.

Before ending, pause for a written revision. Participants should note whether their view changed, remained the same for a stronger reason, or still depends on missing information. Collect one unanswered question for future study.

Finally, review the process rather than declaring the most confident speaker the winner. Ask whether ideas were restated accurately, evidence was connected to claims, and invitations respected the right to pass. The goal is not equal numbers of words. It is a discussion in which access to the conversation is actively protected.
