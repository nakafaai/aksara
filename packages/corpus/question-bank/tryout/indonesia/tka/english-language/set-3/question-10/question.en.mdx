During my first week as a volunteer at the town archive, I expected to spend most of my time reading old letters. Instead, the archivist, Mr. Vale, gave me a pencil, a clean folder, and one box of unlabeled photographs. My task was not to guess who appeared in them. It was to record what could actually be observed.

On Monday, I wrote descriptions such as “three people outside a brick building” and noted marks on the back of each photograph. I wanted to call one building the old station, but Mr. Vale asked for evidence. We found a matching roof in a dated newspaper image, yet the angle was not enough to be certain. I changed the note to “possibly the old station” and linked the comparison.

On Wednesday, a visitor recognized her grandfather in one photograph. She gave us his name and an approximate year. Mr. Vale recorded her statement as a contributor note rather than replacing the visual description. He explained that future researchers should be able to see which information came from the object and which came from a person's memory.

By Friday, I had described forty-two photographs. Only nine had confirmed names, but the box was more useful than before. Each image had a stable number, a physical description, and a place for later evidence.

I did read one old letter at the end of the week. What stayed with me, however, was the discipline of leaving room for correction. The archive did not become trustworthy by filling every blank. It became trustworthy by showing which blanks were still blanks.
