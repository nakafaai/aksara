Every Saturday I worked in Uncle Bonar's repair shop on Jalan Sutomo, between a tailor's and a shop that sold birdcages. Uncle had three habits I took for granted. He set the wall clock ten minutes fast, so that he would never keep Aunt Mira waiting at dinner. He kept Grandmother's old valve radio on the top shelf, working but silent. It ran only on mains power, and that morning he had pulled its plug from the wall to free the socket for his soldering iron. And he never charged Bu Lastri, the widow from the next street, for anything at all.

Just before ten, a student brought in a small radio that had gone silent. The moment I lifted it from the counter, it burst into a news broadcast, and the student laughed and said I had magic hands. Uncle opened the back and showed me a wire whose end had worked loose. It touched its terminal, he explained, only when the set was tipped forward. "Magic is just a fault you haven't found yet," he said, and he told the student to come back in a week.

At noon the tailor pulled down his shutter and left for his niece's wedding in Binjai, calling out that he would not be back until Monday. The birdcage seller on the other side had been away all week. In the afternoon Bu Lastri brought in a fan. Uncle fixed it in twenty minutes and waved away her money, saying she could pay him in kue lapis next time, which is what he says every time.

Our cat, Belang, always seemed to know when Uncle was coming back from an errand. She would wake, stretch and sit by the door a full minute before his motorbike turned into the street. Uncle said she simply heard the engine at the corner, long before we could.

When the wall clock said five and I was sweeping, a song began to play: the Batak lullaby Grandmother used to sing to me. It was coming from the top shelf. I reached up and laid my hand on the radio, and its speaker cloth trembled under my fingers. The song must be coming through the wall from next door, I told myself, and I went on sweeping. Uncle, who had come in from the back, stood very still. Then he smiled, lifted the radio down, dusted it and set it on the counter, where every customer would see it.
