Every January, the Larangan City Library blocks the card of any borrower whose unpaid late fees have passed <InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> rupiah. Until the debt is paid, that borrower cannot take out another book. The rule was meant to protect the collection. I believe it now does more harm than good, and the library should stop charging people for books that are simply returned late. Charges for books that are lost or damaged are a separate question, and I will come back to them.

Supporters of late fees usually point to the money. Last year the library collected <InlineMath math="41" /> million rupiah in late fees. That sounds useful until it is set beside what the fees cost to collect. By the library's own estimate, staff spent about <InlineMath math="1{,}900" /> hours recording, chasing and processing these small payments. That time was worth roughly <InlineMath math="48" /> million rupiah in wages. In other words, the fines did not pay for their own collection.

The second problem is who the rule shuts out. Of the <InlineMath math="3{,}200" /> cards blocked last January, <InlineMath math="2{,}050" /> belonged to children and teenagers, and most of them owed less than <InlineMath math="25{,}000" /> rupiah. Some people argue that fines teach responsibility. Perhaps they do, for adults who can pay without noticing. For a child, though, even <InlineMath math="15{,}000" /> rupiah is too high a price for one forgotten book. A child who cannot pay simply stops borrowing.

The library has already tested the alternative. For <InlineMath math="6" /> months in 2025, its Cempaka branch dropped late fees and sent automatic text reminders <InlineMath math="3" /> days before each due date instead. The first month alarmed some staff, because only <InlineMath math="70\%" /> of books came back on time. By the sixth month, however, the share had reached <InlineMath math="79\%" />, against <InlineMath math="81\%" /> in the same month a year earlier. Meanwhile, <InlineMath math="410" /> borrowers whose cards had been blocked started borrowing again. The pilot kept one charge. A book still missing after <InlineMath math="60" /> days was billed at its replacement price, and almost all such books were eventually returned.

None of this means a library should give up every charge. A book that never comes back is a real loss, and asking the borrower to pay for it is fair. What the city should end is the fee for lateness alone. On the evidence of its own records, that fee costs more to collect than it raises, falls hardest on young borrowers, and does little to bring books back on time.
