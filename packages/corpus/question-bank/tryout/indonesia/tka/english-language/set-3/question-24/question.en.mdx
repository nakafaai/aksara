Public organizations should check whether equipment can be repaired before replacing it, but repair should not become an automatic rule. A sensible policy asks three questions: Is the equipment safe? Can parts and skills be obtained? Will the repair provide enough useful life for its cost and environmental impact?

Checking repairability has clear benefits. A damaged cable, worn wheel, or failed switch may be cheaper to replace than an entire device. Maintenance records can also reveal repeated failures that a purchasing team would otherwise miss. When several identical items break in the same way, the organization gains evidence for choosing a better design next time.

However, keeping unsafe or unsupported equipment is not responsible. A repair that removes a safety guard, uses an unapproved battery, or depends on unavailable expertise may create greater risk. Very old equipment can also consume more energy or fail to meet current accessibility needs. In those cases, replacement may be the better decision.

The policy should therefore require a short decision record. It should name the fault, safety assessment, estimated repair cost, expected additional life, replacement option, and reason for the final choice. This record prevents “repair” from meaning endless spending and prevents “replace” from becoming the easiest answer without examination.

The goal is not to keep every object forever. It is to make the trade-off visible. A transparent repair-first check can reduce waste and improve purchasing decisions while leaving a clear path to replacement when evidence shows that continued use is unsafe, inaccessible, or poor value.
