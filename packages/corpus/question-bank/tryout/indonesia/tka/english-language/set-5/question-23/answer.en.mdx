# Given

Some stallholders worried that during the trial **shoppers would switch to stalls still handing out free bags**. The text notes charging-stall sales fell <InlineMath math="3\%" /> while whole-market sales fell <InlineMath math="2\%" /> due to rain.

# Asked

Which numbered findings, if true, would **strengthen the stallholders' worry** that shoppers moved to free-bag stalls.

# Solution

Ask one sharp question for every finding: **does this point to lost customers moving over to the free-bag stalls**? Findings explaining the drop via rain or showing regulars stayed loyal work against the worry rather than strengthening it.

> Keep the direction straight: facts the writer uses to dismiss the worry (rain, regulars bringing reusable bags) weaken the worry instead of strengthening it.

## Testing each finding against the worry

The worry under test is that <Highlight>shoppers would walk to stalls that still gave bags away free</Highlight>.

| Finding | Strengthens worry? | Effect on the customer-switching worry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | **Yes** | If sales rose at the <InlineMath math="40" /> free-bag stalls while charging-stall sales fell <InlineMath math="3\%" />, business likely shifted there. |
| II | No | Market-wide rain explains the dip without customer switching, which **weakens** the worry. |
| III | **Yes** | Observing former charging-stall customers shopping at free-bag stalls is direct proof of switching. |
| IV | No | Regulars bringing their own bags stayed at the charging stalls, cutting against the worry. |

## Why the other options fail

- **Options including II or IV** select points that actually undercut the stallholders' worry.
- **I only** overlooks the direct observational evidence in III.

# Answer

The findings that would strengthen the stallholders' worry are **I and III only**.
