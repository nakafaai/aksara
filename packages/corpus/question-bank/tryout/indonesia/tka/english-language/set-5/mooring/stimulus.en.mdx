Hendra waited until Tuesday evening to say it out loud. "Salim from Karangsari will give <InlineMath math="30" /> million rupiah for the *Kenanga*, as she is," he told his younger brother on the jetty. "He wants an answer by Friday noon. She's wood and a motor, Yus. Wood rots." Yusuf did not look away from the boat. "Father never let anyone else take her out. And you want to hand her to a stranger?"

Neither son knew that in his last year, when his back grew too stiff to haul the anchor, their father, Pak Darmo to the whole harbour, had let Danu, the boy next door, take the *Kenanga* out on calm mornings. Danu never went beyond the reef, and Pak Darmo watched from the jetty. He also taught the boy the short rising tune he always whistled before a trip.

Unable to sleep that night, Yusuf walked to the harbour near midnight. From the dark beside the *Kenanga* came the tune, whistled slowly, the way Father whistled when his hands were busy. Yusuf went cold. His first thought was the wheelhouse radio, but he had taken out its batteries a month before. He climbed aboard. In the stern, by lamplight, Danu was bailing rainwater from the bilge. "Pak Darmo said a boat left full of rain dies from the bottom," the boy said. "And every Sunday I turn the engine over by hand, like he showed me."

Hendra heard it all at breakfast and shrugged. "So the boy is sentimental. Sentiment won't pay my daughter's university fees." Still, he went down to the jetty. The engine had not been started since the funeral, and it caught on the third pull. Hendra stood a long time with his hand on the warm cover, saying nothing. On the way back he admitted what Salim's message had really said: <InlineMath math="30" /> million if the *Kenanga* reached the fuel jetty under her own engine, <InlineMath math="18" /> million if she had to be towed.

On Thursday night Hendra sat in the bow with a reply to Salim typed on his phone, unsent. "How did Father go past the reef?" he asked. "Never alone," Yusuf said. "That was his one rule." "And the boy?" "Away with his class at the district exams until Saturday noon. And I'm at the workshop until Friday evening." Hendra's train to the city left at seven on Saturday morning. He looked at the screen a while, then laid the phone face down. "So she waits for Saturday afternoon," he said.
