Last month, our geography class visited Gua Lawa, a limestone cave about three hours from our school. We left at six in the morning and reached the entrance just before nine.

Our guide, Pak Darto, first gave each of us a helmet with a headlamp. He told us not to touch the rock formations, because the oil on our hands can stop them from growing. Then we walked down a set of concrete steps into the first chamber. It was cooler than I had expected, and the air smelled of wet earth.

In the second chamber, Pak Darto asked everyone to switch off their headlamps. For about a minute we stood in complete darkness and listened to water dripping somewhere ahead. Some of my classmates giggled nervously, but most of us went silent. When the lamps came back on, he pointed to a thin stalactite and explained that it had probably taken hundreds of years to grow just a few centimeters.

Before we left, we visited a small museum near the entrance. It displayed photographs of the cave from fifty years ago, when visitors were allowed to break off pieces of rock to take home. Comparing the old photos with what we had just seen, we noticed that several large formations were now missing.

On the bus home, our teacher asked us to write one sentence about the trip. I wrote that the moment of darkness was the most memorable part of the day. Looking back, I also think the museum was the most important part, because it showed us why the rules exist.
