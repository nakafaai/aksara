Bayu had never used a film camera before his aunt lent him hers for the school photography contest. “Twenty-four pictures,” Aunt Ratih said, holding up the roll. “No deleting. Think before you press.”

At first, Bayu thought the rule was silly. On his phone, he took dozens of photos of the same thing and kept the best one. With the old camera, he walked around the harbor for an hour and took only three pictures. Each time, he waited for the light to change or for a fishing boat to move into the frame.

On the second day, disaster struck. While he was climbing onto the sea wall, the lens cap slipped out of his pocket and rolled between two rocks into the water. Bayu stared at the waves, imagining his aunt's face. He spent the rest of the afternoon too worried to take a single photo.

That evening, he told Aunt Ratih the truth before she could ask. She was quiet for a moment, then laughed. “The first cap fell off in a crowded market years ago,” she said, “and I bought a new one from a street seller the next day. Caps can be replaced. What did you shoot?”

Bayu finished the roll the following weekend. When the prints came back, most were blurry or too dark. But one showed an old fisherman mending a net, his hands lit by the low sun and a gull frozen in the air above him. Bayu entered that picture in the contest. It did not win first prize, but it won second, and the judge wrote on the back: “You waited for this moment.”

Bayu gave the prize money to his aunt for a new lens cap. She bought one and taped the receipt inside the camera bag, next to a note in her handwriting: “Still think before you press.”
