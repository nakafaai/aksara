The village of Tanah Merah sits on a bend of a slow brown river, and almost every house in it has a workshop at the back. The village takes its name from the red clay that lies under its rice fields, and that clay is the reason the village exists.

Walking down the main street, you first pass the drying yards. Hundreds of unfired pots stand in rows on bamboo racks, pale orange and still soft enough to dent with a fingernail. They must dry slowly in the shade for about a week. If they dry too quickly in direct sun, they crack.

Farther along are the workshops. In most of them, a potter sits at a low wheel that is turned by foot, shaping a lump of clay into a water jar or a flower pot in a few minutes. A few younger potters use electric wheels, but they still finish the edges by hand.

At the end of the street stands the shared kiln, a long brick oven built into a small slope. Families take turns firing their pots there, usually at night, when the air is cooler and the fire is easier to watch. A firing lasts about eight hours, and the finished pots come out a deep reddish brown.

The village shop sells everything from tiny cups to jars taller than a child. Visitors can also pay to sit at a wheel for an hour, although most of their first pots collapse before they are finished.
