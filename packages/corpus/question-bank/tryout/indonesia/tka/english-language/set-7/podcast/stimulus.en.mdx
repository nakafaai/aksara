A home is full of sounds you stop noticing, and a microphone notices them all. This guide covers a two-person interview in one room, with a microphone for each speaker, a recorder that takes both, and closed headphones. The aim is a voice that stays clearly above whatever noise is left.

Start with the room. Hard walls throw the voice back at the microphone a moment late, and this echo makes a speaker sound far away. A bedroom with curtains and a full wardrobe soaks up much of it. Then seat each speaker a hand's width, <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="15\,\text{cm}" />, from their own microphone. If someone leans back to twice that distance, much less of the voice reaches the microphone while the room stays exactly as loud, and turning up the gain will not help: gain lifts the hum and the echo along with the voice.

Next, decide which noises must stop. My editing program can lower a noise afterwards, but only if it is steady, with the same pitch and loudness throughout, and was already running when the room tone was recorded. A desk fan on one speed all evening passes both tests. A barking dog, a door or a phone passes neither, so silence what you can. Watch the fridge: its low hum is steady for a while, then it clicks off and on again. Echo is another matter: the program cannot remove it, because echo is the voice itself arriving late.

Just before the first question, record <InlineMath math="30" /> seconds of room tone: both people seated and silent, with everything that will run during the interview already running. Then make a short test. Ask the guest a real question, such as how they spent last weekend: people count numbers in a flat voice, then laugh at full volume once the talk warms up. Set the level so the loudest words reach about halfway up the meter, and listen back on the headphones, because laptop speakers drop the low sounds that headphones carry.

Even in a well-prepared room, sudden noises arrive. When a motorbike or a cough covers a word, stop and wait for quiet. Clap once near the microphones, then ask the guest to repeat the whole sentence from its first word. A single word recorded later never matches the voice around it, but a whole sentence can replace the spoiled one cleanly. The clap leaves a sharp spike on the recording, so in editing you can find the cut in seconds.
