Compute both determinants first, because two statements use them: <InlineMath math="\det C=2\cdot(-1)-0\cdot1=-2" /> and <InlineMath math="\det D=1\cdot2-3\cdot0=2" />.

1. The statement <InlineMath math="(CD)^T=C^TD^T" /> is false. The correct rule is <InlineMath math="(CD)^T=D^TC^T" />, so **the order of the product reverses**. The computation shows the difference.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
CD&=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 3-2 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\qquad (CD)^T=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\
C^TD^T&=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ -3 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}" />

2. The statement that <InlineMath math="C+D" /> has no inverse is true. <InlineMath math="C+D=\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" /> and its determinant is <InlineMath math="3-3=0" />. A matrix with determinant zero has no inverse.
3. The statement <InlineMath math="\det\left(C^3\right)=8" /> is false. The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants, so <InlineMath math="\det\left(C^3\right)=(\det C)^3=(-2)^3=-8" />.
4. The statement about <InlineMath math="D^{-1}" /> is true. Swap the main diagonal entries, change the signs of the other two, and divide by <InlineMath math="\det D=2" />. Check: <InlineMath math="D\cdot\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}=I" />.

So the statements about <InlineMath math="C+D" /> and <InlineMath math="D^{-1}" /> are true, and the statements about <InlineMath math="(CD)^T" /> and <InlineMath math="\det\left(C^3\right)" /> are false.
