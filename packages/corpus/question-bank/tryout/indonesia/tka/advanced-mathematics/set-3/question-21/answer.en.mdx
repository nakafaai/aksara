# Given

We are given a **rational algebraic function** <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> whose denominator vanishes at <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

# Asked

The **limit value** of the function as <InlineMath math="x" /> approaches <InlineMath math="3" />.

# Solution

> When direct substitution gives zero over zero, factor the numerator to cancel the shared zero factor before plugging in.

The function <InlineMath math="f" /> is continuous at <InlineMath math="x=3" /> exactly when the expression below, so you need the limit. Direct substitution of <InlineMath math="x=3" /> gives the equation below. This indeterminate form means <InlineMath math="x=3" /> is a root of the numerator, so **the numerator contains the factor** <InlineMath math="x-3" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
f(3)=\lim_{x\to3}f(x) \\
\frac{9-6-3}{0}=\frac00 \\
x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1)
\end{gathered}" />

For <InlineMath math="x\ne3" />, you may cancel <InlineMath math="x-3" />, and then direct substitution is safe.

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to3}\frac{(x-3)(x+1)}{x-3}=\lim_{x\to3}(x+1)=4" />

Draw <InlineMath math="f" /> after cancelling the factor <InlineMath math="x-3" />. **The graph is the line <InlineMath math="y=x+1" /> with a hole only at <InlineMath math="x=3" />**, and the hole closes if <InlineMath math="f(3)=4" />.

So <InlineMath math="f(3)" /> must be defined as <InlineMath math="4" />. With that value, <InlineMath math="f(x)=x+1" /> for every real number, which is clearly continuous.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="0" /> assumes a zero numerator makes the limit zero. The denominator is zero too.
- <InlineMath math="2" /> factors the numerator as <InlineMath math="(x-3)(x-1)" />, but the equation below.

  <BlockMath math="(x-3)(x-1)=x^2-4x+3" />

- <InlineMath math="\infty" /> assumes a zero denominator always makes the limit infinite.
- <InlineMath math="6" /> differentiates only the <InlineMath math="x^2" /> term to <InlineMath math="2x" /> and forgets the <InlineMath math="-2x" /> term.

# Answer

Therefore, the **correct answer** is **<InlineMath math="4" />**.
