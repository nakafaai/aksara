An inverse function **swaps the roles of input and output**. Write <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" />, solve for <InlineMath math="x" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="y" />, then rename the variable.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y(x+2)&=3x-1 \\
xy+2y&=3x-1 \\
xy-3x&=-1-2y \\
x(y-3)&=-(2y+1) \\
x&=\frac{2y+1}{3-y}
\end{aligned}" />

The last step divides by <InlineMath math="y-3" />, so it needs <InlineMath math="y\ne3" />. Renaming <InlineMath math="y" /> as <InlineMath math="x" /> gives <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(x)=\frac{2x+1}{3-x}" /> for <InlineMath math="x\ne3" />.

Check: <InlineMath math="f(1)=\frac23" />, and <InlineMath math="f^{-1}\left(\frac23\right)=\frac{\frac43+1}{3-\frac23}=\frac{7/3}{7/3}=1" />.

So <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(x)=\frac{2x+1}{3-x}" /> with <InlineMath math="x\ne3" />.

- <InlineMath math="\frac{x+2}{3x-1}" /> is the reciprocal <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{f(x)}" />, not the inverse function.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{2x-1}{x-3}" /> slips a sign when moving the <InlineMath math="-1" /> to the right side.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{2x+1}{x+3}" /> has the wrong sign in the denominator, as if the factor were <InlineMath math="y+3" />.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{3-x}{2x+1}" /> turns the correct inverse upside down.
