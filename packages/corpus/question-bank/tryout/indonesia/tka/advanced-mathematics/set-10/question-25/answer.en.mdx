The two-sided limit exists exactly when the one-sided limits agree: the equation below, that is <InlineMath math="a=1" />.

<BlockMath math="2a+1=4-a" />

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- **True.** For <InlineMath math="a=1" />, both one-sided limits equal <InlineMath math="3" />.
- **True.** For <InlineMath math="a=1" />, the limit is <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="f(2)=3" />. They agree, so <InlineMath math="f" /> is continuous at <InlineMath math="x=2" />.

  <BlockMath math="f(2)=4-1=3" />

- **False.** For <InlineMath math="a=0" />, the left-hand limit uses the rule <InlineMath math="ax+1=1" />, so it is <InlineMath math="1" />. The value <InlineMath math="4" /> is the right-hand limit.
- **True.** For <InlineMath math="a=0" />, the left-hand limit <InlineMath math="1" /> and the right-hand limit <InlineMath math="4" /> differ, so the limit does not exist.
- **False.** the equation below is linear and has only one solution, <InlineMath math="a=1" />.

  <BlockMath math="2a+1=4-a" />

# Answer

Therefore, the correct choices are **statements <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="2" />, and <InlineMath math="4" />**.
