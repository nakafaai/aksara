# Given

We are given a **rational limit at infinity** whose numerator is the cube of a linear binomial.

# Asked

The **limit value** as <InlineMath math="x" /> grows without bound.

# Solution

> Just compare the leading coefficients of the top and bottom cubic terms because lower powers fade away at infinity.

The numerator and denominator both have degree three. With equal degrees, the limit is **the ratio of the leading coefficients**. The <InlineMath math="x^3" /> coefficient of <InlineMath math="(2x-1)^3" /> is <InlineMath math="2^3=8" />.

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(2x-1)^3}{x^3+4x}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\left(2-\frac1x\right)^3}{1+\frac{4}{x^2}}=\frac{2^3}{1}=8" />

Draw the graph of the fraction as <InlineMath math="x" /> grows. **The curve rises and hugs the line <InlineMath math="y=8" />**, not <InlineMath math="y=2" />, because the cube also cubes the coefficient <InlineMath math="2" />.

That line divides top and bottom by <InlineMath math="x^3" />, and the equation below. For example, at <InlineMath math="x=100" /> the value is about <InlineMath math="7.88" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{(2x-1)^3}{x^3}=\left(2-\frac1x\right)^3" />

So the limit is <InlineMath math="8" />.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="2" /> takes the coefficient of <InlineMath math="2x" /> without cubing it.
- <InlineMath math="6" /> multiplies <InlineMath math="2" /> by the exponent <InlineMath math="3" /> instead of raising it to that power.
- <InlineMath math="0" /> assumes the denominator grows faster, but the degrees are equal.
- <InlineMath math="\infty" /> assumes the numerator grows faster, but the degrees are equal.
