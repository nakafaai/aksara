Two vectors are perpendicular exactly when **their dot product is zero**. Let the vector be <InlineMath math="(x,y,z)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x+y-z&=0 \\
x+3y+2z&=0
\end{aligned}" />

Multiply the first equation by <InlineMath math="2" /> and add the second: <InlineMath math="5x+5y=0" />, so <InlineMath math="y=-x" />. Substituting into the first gives <InlineMath math="z=2x+y=x" />. Every vector perpendicular to both has the form <InlineMath math="x(1,-1,1)" />, and <InlineMath math="x=1" /> gives <InlineMath math="\vec{i}-\vec{j}+\vec{k}" />.

Check: <InlineMath math="(1,-1,1)\cdot\vec{a}=2-1-1=0" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,-1,1)\cdot\vec{b}=1-3+2=0" />.

So the vector is <InlineMath math="\vec{i}-\vec{j}+\vec{k}" />.

- <InlineMath math="\vec{i}-2\vec{j}" /> is perpendicular to <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />, but its dot product with <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> is <InlineMath math="1-6=-5" />.
- <InlineMath math="3\vec{i}-\vec{j}" /> is perpendicular to <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" />, but its dot product with <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> is <InlineMath math="6-1=5" />.
- <InlineMath math="2\vec{i}+\vec{j}-\vec{k}" /> is <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> itself, which is not perpendicular to itself.
- <InlineMath math="3\vec{i}+4\vec{j}+\vec{k}" /> is <InlineMath math="\vec{a}+\vec{b}" />, which lies in the plane spanned by the two vectors.
