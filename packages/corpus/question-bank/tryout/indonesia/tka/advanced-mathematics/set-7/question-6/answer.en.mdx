# Given

A **quadratic polynomial** <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> has known output values at three distinct inputs.

# Asked

The **formula or requested value** of quadratic <InlineMath math="P(x)" />.

# Solution

> Three points lock down one unique parabola, and knowing a root lets you write its factored form right away.

A quadratic has three coefficients, and the problem gives exactly three values. Write <InlineMath math="P(x)" />, then **set up three equations and solve by elimination**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
P(x)=ax^2+bx+c \\
\begin{aligned}
a+b+c&=0 \\
4a+2b+c&=3 \\
9a+3b+c&=10
\end{aligned}
\end{gathered}" />

Subtract consecutive equations to remove <InlineMath math="c" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
3a+b&=3 \\
5a+b&=7 \\
2a&=4\ \Rightarrow\ a=2,\quad b=-3,\quad c=1
\end{aligned}" />

Draw <InlineMath math="P" /> through the three known points. **The rises between points are <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="7" />, then <InlineMath math="11" />**, growing by <InlineMath math="4" /> each step, so the next point is at height <InlineMath math="10+11=21" />.

So <InlineMath math="P(x)" />, and <InlineMath math="P(4)=21" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
P(x)=2x^2-3x+1 \\
P(4)=32-12+1=21
\end{gathered}" />

Another route checks this. The differences of the values <InlineMath math="0,3,10" /> are <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="7" />, and for a quadratic the second difference is always constant, here <InlineMath math="4" />. The next difference is <InlineMath math="7+4=11" />, so <InlineMath math="P(4)=21" />.

<BlockMath math="P(4)=10+11=21" />

So <InlineMath math="P(4)=21" />.
