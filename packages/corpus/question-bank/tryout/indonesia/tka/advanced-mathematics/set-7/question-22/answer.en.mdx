# Given

We are given a **fraction limit** combining a square root and sine in radians near <InlineMath math="x=0" />.

# Asked

The **limit value** after simplifying both the square root and sine.

# Solution

> Multiply by the conjugate to expose a linear factor, then pair it directly with the small-angle sine.

Direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac00" />. The numerator is a difference involving a root, so **multiply by its conjugate**, <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x+4}+2" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{\sin\frac{x}{4}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}=\frac{x}{\sin\frac{x}{4}\,\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)}" />

Split it into a standard sine limit and a part you can substitute into directly. Since the value <InlineMath math="4" />, you get the following.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{\sin\frac{x}{4}}=4 \\
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{\sin\frac{x}{4}}\cdot\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}=4\cdot\frac14=1
\end{gathered}" />

Draw the function on both sides of zero. **The curve falls gently and passes height <InlineMath math="1" /> right at <InlineMath math="x=0" />**, even though the function itself isn't defined there.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="0" /> only looks at the numerator tending to <InlineMath math="0" />, but the denominator tends to <InlineMath math="0" /> as well.
- <InlineMath math="2" /> takes <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x+4}+2" /> to be <InlineMath math="2" /> at <InlineMath math="x=0" />, but it is <InlineMath math="4" />.
- <InlineMath math="\frac14" /> treats <InlineMath math="\sin\frac{x}{4}" /> like <InlineMath math="\sin x" />, losing the factor <InlineMath math="4" /> from the sine limit.
- <InlineMath math="\infty" /> only looks at the denominator tending to <InlineMath math="0" />.
