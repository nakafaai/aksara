As <InlineMath math="x\to-2" />, both fractions grow without bound with opposite signs, so the form is <InlineMath math="\infty-\infty" /> and the limit is not visible yet. **Use a common denominator** first, since <InlineMath math="x^2-4=(x+2)(x-2)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{x+2}+\frac{4}{(x+2)(x-2)}&=\frac{(x-2)+4}{(x+2)(x-2)} \\
&=\frac{x+2}{(x+2)(x-2)}=\frac{1}{x-2},\qquad x\neq-2
\end{aligned}" />

Once the factor <InlineMath math="x+2" /> is cancelled, direct substitution is safe.

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to-2}\frac{1}{x-2}=\frac{1}{-4}=-\frac14" />

Numerical check: for <InlineMath math="x=-1.99" />, <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{0.01}+\frac{4}{-0.0399}\approx100-100.25=-0.25" />.

So the limit is <InlineMath math="-\frac14" />.

- <InlineMath math="0" /> assumes the two unbounded parts cancel completely.
- <InlineMath math="\infty" /> only looks at the first fraction growing.
- <InlineMath math="\frac14" /> factors <InlineMath math="x^2-4" /> wrongly as <InlineMath math="(x+2)(2-x)" />, leaving <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2-x}" />.
- <InlineMath math="-4" /> substitutes into <InlineMath math="x-2" /> and forgets that what remains is the fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{x-2}" />.
