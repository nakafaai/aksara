# Given

We are given a **rational function** <InlineMath math="p(x)" /> for all real <InlineMath math="x\ne\pm1" />.

# Asked

The **limit value** of <InlineMath math="p(x)" /> at the zero-over-zero point.

# Solution

> Factor both numerator and denominator to cancel the common zero factor before substituting.

Substituting <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> gives the equation below. So <InlineMath math="x+1" /> is **a common factor of the numerator and the denominator**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
\frac{1-3+2}{1-1}=\frac00 \\
p(x)=\frac{(x+1)(x+2)}{(x+1)(x-1)}=\frac{x+2}{x-1},\qquad x\ne\pm1
\end{gathered}" />

For <InlineMath math="x\ne-1" /> you may cancel that factor, and the simplified form is continuous at <InlineMath math="x=-1" />.

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{x+2}{x-1}=\frac{-1+2}{-1-1}=-\frac12" />

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="\frac12" /> has a sign slip in the denominator, <InlineMath math="-1-1" />.
- <InlineMath math="0" /> only looks at the numerator tending to zero.
- <InlineMath math="-1" /> is the value that <InlineMath math="x" /> approaches, not the value of the limit.
- <InlineMath math="\infty" /> assumes a denominator tending to zero must make the limit infinite.
