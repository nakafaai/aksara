# Given

A determinant equation sets a <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> **determinant** on the left equal to a <InlineMath math="3\times3" /> **determinant** on the right containing <InlineMath math="x" />.

# Asked

The **sum of all real values of** <InlineMath math="x" /> satisfying the equation.

# Solution

> Turn both determinants into plain algebra first so the matrix problem becomes an ordinary quadratic whose root sum you can read straight off.

The equation compares two determinants, so the first step is to **turn each determinant into an algebraic expression in** <InlineMath math="x" />. After that, the determinant equation becomes an ordinary quadratic equation.

The determinant of a <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> matrix is the product of the main diagonal minus the product of the other diagonal.

<BlockMath math="\begin{vmatrix} x & 2 \\ 3 & x-1 \end{vmatrix}=x(x-1)-2\cdot3=x^2-x-6" />

Expand the <InlineMath math="3\times3" /> determinant along its first row. The middle entry of that row is <InlineMath math="0" />, so only two cofactors need to be calculated. The cofactor signs along the first row are positive, negative, positive.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & x & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}&=1\begin{vmatrix} x & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}-0\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}+2\begin{vmatrix} 0 & x \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \\
&=1(3x-1)-0+2(0\cdot1-x\cdot1) \\
&=3x-1-2x=x-1
\end{aligned}" />

Set the two results equal and move every term to the left side.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x^2-x-6&=x-1 \\
x^2-2x-5&=0
\end{aligned}" />

Draw both sides as graphs. **The two crossing points sit symmetrically about the line <InlineMath math="x=1" />**, so the two values of <InlineMath math="x" /> add up to <InlineMath math="2\cdot1=2" />.

The discriminant of this quadratic is <InlineMath math="D=24" />. Since <InlineMath math="D>0" />, there are two different real values of <InlineMath math="x" />, namely <InlineMath math="x=1+\sqrt6" /> and <InlineMath math="x=1-\sqrt6" />. The sum-of-roots formula gives <InlineMath math="2" />, and adding the roots directly gives the same value, <InlineMath math="2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
D=(-2)^2-4(1)(-5)=24 \\
-\frac{b}{a}=-\frac{-2}{1}=2 \\
(1+\sqrt6)+(1-\sqrt6)=2
\end{gathered}" />

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="-5" /> is the **product** of the two roots, <InlineMath math="\frac{c}{a}" />, not their sum.
- <InlineMath math="-2" /> comes from dropping the minus sign in the formula <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{a}" />.
- <InlineMath math="1" /> comes from solving only the equation below, as if the right side were zero. The <InlineMath math="3\times3" /> determinant equals <InlineMath math="x-1" />, not zero.

  <BlockMath math="x^2-x-6=0" />

- <InlineMath math="6" /> appears if the minor of the third entry is computed as <InlineMath math="x\cdot1-0\cdot1=x" /> instead of <InlineMath math="0\cdot1-x\cdot1=-x" />. The right-hand determinant then becomes <InlineMath math="5x-1" />, and the equation below has root sum <InlineMath math="6" />.

  <BlockMath math="x^2-6x-5=0" />

# Answer

Therefore, the **correct answer** is **<InlineMath math="2" />**.
