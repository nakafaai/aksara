# Given

**The polynomial** **<InlineMath math="P(x)" />** is divisible by **<InlineMath math="(x+1)(x-2)" />**.

<BlockMath math="P(x) = x^4 + ax^3 - 3x^2 + bx + 4" />

# Asked

The **remainder when** <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divided by <InlineMath math="x - 3" />.

# Solution

> Divisibility means plugging in each root gives zero, so solve for a and b from those two roots and evaluate the polynomial at three.

The remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> is <InlineMath math="P(3)" />, but that value can be computed only after <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are known. Both coefficients follow from the fact that <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is **divisible by** <InlineMath math="(x+1)(x-2)" />.

By the factor theorem, <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divisible by <InlineMath math="x-c" /> exactly when <InlineMath math="P(c)=0" />. Since <InlineMath math="x+1" /> and <InlineMath math="x-2" /> are both factors, <InlineMath math="P(-1)=0" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2)=0" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(-1)&=1-a-3-b+4=2-a-b=0 &&\Rightarrow\ a+b=2 \\
P(2)&=16+8a-12+2b+4=8+8a+2b=0 &&\Rightarrow\ 4a+b=-4
\end{aligned}" />

Subtract the first equation from the second to remove <InlineMath math="b" />, then substitute the value of <InlineMath math="a" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(4a+b)-(a+b)&=-4-2 \\
3a&=-6 \\
a&=-2 \\
b&=2-a=4
\end{aligned}" />

The polynomial is <InlineMath math="P(x)" />. By the remainder theorem, the remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> equals <InlineMath math="P(3)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
P(x)=x^4-2x^3-3x^2+4x+4 \\
P(3)=81-2\cdot27-3\cdot9+4\cdot3+4=81-54-27+12+4=16
\end{gathered}" />

Draw <InlineMath math="P(x)" />. **The graph touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at both factors <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" />**, and the remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> is the graph's height at <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

<BlockMath math="P(x)=(x^2-x-2)^2" />

As a check, divide <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> by the equation below. The quotient is also <InlineMath math="x^2-x-2" />, so <InlineMath math="P(x)" />. In this form, <InlineMath math="P(3)=16" />, the same result.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
(x+1)(x-2)=x^2-x-2 \\
P(x)=(x^2-x-2)^2 \\
P(3)=(9-3-2)^2=4^2=16
\end{gathered}" />

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="4" /> is the constant term of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />, that is, <InlineMath math="P(0)" />. The remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> is the value of the polynomial at <InlineMath math="x=3" />, not at <InlineMath math="x=0" />.
- <InlineMath math="12" /> comes from forgetting the constant term <InlineMath math="4" /> while computing <InlineMath math="P(3)" />.
- <InlineMath math="100" /> comes from taking the zeros of the factors as <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-2" />, which gives <InlineMath math="a=2" /> and <InlineMath math="b=-4" />. The value <InlineMath math="100" /> is also obtained when the correct <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is evaluated at <InlineMath math="x=-3" />, since <InlineMath math="P(-3)=100" />.
- <InlineMath math="160" /> comes from swapping the values of <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" />, so that the expression below is computed.

  <BlockMath math="3^4+4\cdot3^3-3\cdot3^2-2\cdot3+4" />

# Answer

So the remainder when <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divided by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> is <InlineMath math="16" />.
