Every linear transformation of the plane has a matrix whose **columns are the images of the unit vectors**. The first column is the image of <InlineMath math="(1,0)" />, and the second column is the image of <InlineMath math="(0,1)" />. So the two facts in the question build the matrix of <InlineMath math="T" /> directly.

<BlockMath math="M_T=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=2\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" /> is the rotation by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about <InlineMath math="O" />, because the rotation matrix for an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}" />, with <InlineMath math="\cos90^\circ=0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin90^\circ=1" />. The factor <InlineMath math="2" /> in front is the dilation about <InlineMath math="O" /> with scale factor <InlineMath math="2" />, whose matrix is <InlineMath math="2I" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=M_T" />

Check with the points in the question. The <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> rotation moves <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0,1)" />, and the dilation doubles it to <InlineMath math="(0,2)" />. The point <InlineMath math="(0,1)" /> rotates to <InlineMath math="(-1,0)" /> and then becomes <InlineMath math="(-2,0)" />. Both match.

So <InlineMath math="T" /> is the rotation by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about <InlineMath math="O" />, followed by the dilation about <InlineMath math="O" /> with scale factor <InlineMath math="2" />.

- A clockwise rotation by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> followed by a dilation of factor <InlineMath math="2" /> has matrix <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" />. It maps <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0,-2)" />, not to <InlineMath math="(0,2)" />.
- A reflection in <InlineMath math="y=x" /> followed by a dilation of factor <InlineMath math="2" /> has matrix <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" />. It maps <InlineMath math="(0,1)" /> to <InlineMath math="(2,0)" />, not to <InlineMath math="(-2,0)" />.
- A rotation by <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> followed by a dilation of factor <InlineMath math="2" /> has matrix <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}" /> and maps <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-2,0)" />, not to <InlineMath math="(0,2)" />.
- A dilation with scale factor <InlineMath math="\frac12" /> shrinks distances. A rotation keeps distances to <InlineMath math="O" />, and the image of <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> is at distance <InlineMath math="2" /> from <InlineMath math="O" />, so the scale factor is <InlineMath math="2" />.
