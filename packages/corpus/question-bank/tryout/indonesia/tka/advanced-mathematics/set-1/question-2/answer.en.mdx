# Given

Two <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> matrices, **matrix** <InlineMath math="A" /> and matrix <InlineMath math="B" />, satisfy the equation **<InlineMath math="XA = B" />**.

# Asked

The <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> **matrix** <InlineMath math="X" /> solving the equation.

# Solution

> Because matrix multiplication cannot swap sides, clear A from the right side of X by multiplying both sides by A inverse on the right too.

The matrix <InlineMath math="X" /> stands **to the left of** <InlineMath math="A" />. Matrix multiplication is not commutative, so <InlineMath math="A" /> is removed by multiplying both sides by <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> on the **right**. This step is valid only if <InlineMath math="A" /> has an inverse, so check its determinant first.

<BlockMath math="\det A=3\cdot2-1\cdot5=1" />

The determinant is not zero, so <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> exists. For a <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> matrix, swap the entries of the main diagonal, change the signs of the other two entries, and divide by the determinant.

<BlockMath math="A^{-1}=\frac{1}{1}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" />

The diagram below compares the two sides on which <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> can be multiplied. **Only multiplying on the right puts <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> right next to <InlineMath math="A" />**, so <InlineMath math="AA^{-1}=I" /> disappears.

Multiply both sides of <InlineMath math="XA=B" /> by <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> on the right. Since <InlineMath math="AA^{-1}=I" />, only <InlineMath math="X" /> remains on the left.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
XAA^{-1}&=BA^{-1} \\
X&=\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \\
&=\begin{pmatrix} 4\cdot2+1\cdot(-5) & 4\cdot(-1)+1\cdot3 \\ 1\cdot2+0\cdot(-5) & 1\cdot(-1)+0\cdot3 \end{pmatrix} \\
&=\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}" />

Check the result by computing <InlineMath math="XA" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 9-5 & 3-2 \\ 6-5 & 2-2 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}=B" />

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- the matrix below is <InlineMath math="A^{-1}B" />. It solves <InlineMath math="AX=B" />, not <InlineMath math="XA=B" />, because the order of multiplication is reversed.

  <BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -17 & -5 \end{pmatrix}" />

- the matrix below is only <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" />. The multiplication by <InlineMath math="B" /> is missing.

  <BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" />

- the matrix below comes from keeping the signs of the off-diagonal entries, so the matrix used is the matrix below instead of <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" />.

  <BlockMath math="\begin{gathered} \begin{pmatrix} 13 & 7 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \end{gathered}" />

- the matrix below is <InlineMath math="BA" />. It appears when <InlineMath math="B" /> is multiplied by <InlineMath math="A" />, although only the inverse removes <InlineMath math="A" />.

  <BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 17 & 6 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

# Answer

So the matrix <InlineMath math="X" /> is the matrix below.

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" />
