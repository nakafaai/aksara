# Given

Function <InlineMath math="f" /> has an **inverse** <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" /> and point <InlineMath math="(2,7)" /> lies on its graph.

# Asked

The **corresponding point or value** on the graph of <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" />.

# Solution

> An inverse simply swaps input and output, so every point on the original graph just trades its two coordinates.

The point <InlineMath math="(2,7)" /> on the graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> means <InlineMath math="f(2)=7" />. An inverse function **reverses the direction of the mapping**, so <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(7)=2" />. That means the point <InlineMath math="(7,2)" /> lies on the graph of <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" />.

Geometrically, the graph of <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" /> is the reflection of the graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> in the line <InlineMath math="y=x" />, and that reflection swaps the two coordinates of every point.

Draw the point and its mirror image. **Reflection in <InlineMath math="y=x" /> swaps the two coordinates**, so the point <InlineMath math="(2,7)" /> on <InlineMath math="f" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(7,2)" /> on <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" />.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="(-2,7)" /> is the reflection in the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, not in <InlineMath math="y=x" />.
- <InlineMath math="(2,-7)" /> is the reflection in the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis.
- <InlineMath math="(-7,-2)" /> swaps the coordinates and changes both signs, the reflection in <InlineMath math="y=-x" />.
- <InlineMath math="\left(\frac12,\frac17\right)" /> reads <InlineMath math="f^{-1}" /> as the reciprocal of each coordinate.
