# Given

We are given a **quadratic function** <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> and a **linear function** <InlineMath math="g(x)" /> on all real numbers.

# Asked

The **composite function formula** combining <InlineMath math="f" /> and <InlineMath math="g" />.

# Solution

> Drop the entire inner function formula right into the outer variable slot, then simplify cleanly.

The notation <InlineMath math="(g\circ f)(x)" /> means <InlineMath math="g(f(x))" />: **<InlineMath math="f" /> acts first**, and its output becomes the input of <InlineMath math="g" />. So replace every <InlineMath math="x" /> in the rule for <InlineMath math="g" /> with <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2+1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(g\circ f)(x)&=2\left(x^2+1\right)-3 \\
&=2x^2+2-3 \\
&=2x^2-1
\end{aligned}" />

The diagram below shows the order in <InlineMath math="g\circ f" />. **<InlineMath math="f" /> acts first, then its output goes into <InlineMath math="g" />**, and the example <InlineMath math="x=2" /> follows the same path.

Check at <InlineMath math="x=2" />: <InlineMath math="f(2)=5" /> and <InlineMath math="g(5)=7" />, while <InlineMath math="2\cdot4-1=7" />.

So <InlineMath math="(g\circ f)(x)=2x^2-1" />.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="4x^2-12x+10" /> is the equation below, the composition in the wrong order.

  <BlockMath math="(f\circ g)(x)=(2x-3)^2+1" />

- The expression below is the product <InlineMath math="f(x)\cdot g(x)" />, not the composition.

  <BlockMath math="2x^3-3x^2+2x-3" />

- <InlineMath math="2x^2-2" /> multiplies only <InlineMath math="x^2" /> by <InlineMath math="2" /> and leaves the <InlineMath math="1" /> alone.
- <InlineMath math="x^2-2" /> is <InlineMath math="f(x)-3" />, forgetting the factor <InlineMath math="2" /> in <InlineMath math="g" />.

# Answer

Therefore, the **correct answer** is **<InlineMath math="2x^2-1" />**.
