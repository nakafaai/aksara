# Given

Polynomial <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> leaves the **same remainder** <InlineMath math="5" /> when divided by <InlineMath math="x - 1" />, <InlineMath math="x - 2" />, or <InlineMath math="x - 4" />.

# Asked

The **remainder when** <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divided by <InlineMath math="x - 3" />.

# Solution

> Subtract five first so those three numbers turn into exact roots of a factored polynomial, then add five back at the end.

By the remainder theorem, the remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-c" /> equals <InlineMath math="P(c)" />. So the problem says <InlineMath math="P(1)=5" />, and you want <InlineMath math="P(3)" />.

<BlockMath math="P(1)=P(2)=P(4)=5" />

The key is the helper polynomial <InlineMath math="Q(x)=P(x)-5" />. It **is zero at** <InlineMath math="x=1" />, <InlineMath math="x=2" /> **and** <InlineMath math="x=4" />, so by the factor theorem <InlineMath math="Q(x)" /> is divisible by the expression below. <InlineMath math="Q" /> has degree three and its <InlineMath math="x^3" />-coefficient is still <InlineMath math="2" />, so the only other factor is the constant <InlineMath math="2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
(x-1)(x-2)(x-4) \\
\begin{aligned}
P(x)-5&=2(x-1)(x-2)(x-4) \\
P(x)&=2(x-1)(x-2)(x-4)+5
\end{aligned}
\end{gathered}" />

Now substitute <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

<BlockMath math="P(3)=2(2)(1)(-1)+5=-4+5=1" />

Draw <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> with the line <InlineMath math="y=5" />. **The three equal remainders mean the graph crosses <InlineMath math="y=5" /> at <InlineMath math="x=1" />, <InlineMath math="2" />, and <InlineMath math="4" />**, and the remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-3" /> is the graph's height at <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="-4" /> forgets to add the <InlineMath math="5" /> back, so it is <InlineMath math="Q(3)" />, not <InlineMath math="P(3)" />.
- <InlineMath math="5" /> assumes the remainder is the same for every linear divisor. That only holds at the three given points.
- <InlineMath math="9" /> comes from writing the factor <InlineMath math="3-4" /> as <InlineMath math="1" />, which makes the product positive.
- <InlineMath math="3" /> forgets the leading coefficient <InlineMath math="2" /> and uses <InlineMath math="P(x)" />.

  <BlockMath math="P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-4)+5" />

# Answer

Therefore, the **correct answer** is **<InlineMath math="1" />**.
