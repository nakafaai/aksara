First compute <InlineMath math="A^2" />, then write <InlineMath math="pA+qI" /> as one matrix. Two matrices are equal when **every pair of matching entries is equal**, so you get four equations for the two unknown numbers.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A^2&=\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 9-2 & 3+1 \\ -6-2 & -2+1 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 4 \\ -8 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\
pA+qI&=\begin{pmatrix} 3p+q & p \\ -2p & p+q \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}" />

The entry in row one, column two gives <InlineMath math="p=4" /> at once. The entry in row two, column one checks it, because <InlineMath math="-2\cdot4=-8" />. The top left entry gives <InlineMath math="q" />.

<BlockMath math="3(4)+q=7\ \Rightarrow\ q=-5" />

The bottom right entry matches as well, since <InlineMath math="p+q=4-5=-1" />. All four equations hold, so <InlineMath math="p=4" /> and <InlineMath math="q=-5" /> is the right pair.

So <InlineMath math="pq=4\cdot(-5)=-20" />.

- <InlineMath math="9" /> is <InlineMath math="p-q" />, not the product.
- <InlineMath math="-1" /> is <InlineMath math="p+q" />.
- <InlineMath math="20" /> comes from taking <InlineMath math="q=5" />, for example by writing <InlineMath math="q=12-7" />.
- <InlineMath math="-5" /> is only the value of <InlineMath math="q" />.
