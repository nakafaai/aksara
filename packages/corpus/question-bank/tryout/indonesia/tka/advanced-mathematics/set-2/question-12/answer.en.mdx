Two nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when **their dot product is zero**. So the condition is <InlineMath math="(\vec{a}+k\vec{b})\cdot\vec{a}=0" />. The dot product distributes, so this splits into two terms you can compute from the given data.

<BlockMath math="(\vec{a}+k\vec{b})\cdot\vec{a}=\vec{a}\cdot\vec{a}+k\,\vec{b}\cdot\vec{a}=|\vec{a}|^2+k\,\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}" />

Compute both terms. Here <InlineMath math="\cos120^\circ=-\frac12" />, because an obtuse angle has a negative cosine.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
|\vec{a}|^2&=3^2=9 \\
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}&=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos120^\circ=3\cdot4\cdot\left(-\frac12\right)=-6
\end{aligned}" />

Set the sum equal to zero.

<BlockMath math="9+k(-6)=0\ \Rightarrow\ k=\frac96=\frac32" />

So <InlineMath math="k=\frac32" />.

- <InlineMath math="-\frac32" /> uses <InlineMath math="\cos120^\circ=\frac12" />, which gives the dot product the wrong sign.
- <InlineMath math="\frac38" /> solves the condition that <InlineMath math="\vec{a}+k\vec{b}" /> is perpendicular to <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" />, namely <InlineMath math="-6+16k=0" />.
- <InlineMath math="\frac23" /> flips the fraction when solving <InlineMath math="6k=9" />.
- <InlineMath math="-\frac38" /> combines the wrong vector with the wrong cosine sign.
