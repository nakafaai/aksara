# Given

A circle centered in the **first quadrant** touches both coordinate axes and passes through a given point.

# Asked

The **radii or requested property** of the circles satisfying all three conditions.

# Solution

> Touching both axes in the first quadrant forces both center coordinates to equal the radius, leaving just one unknown.

A circle tangent to the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis is <InlineMath math="r" /> away from it, and a circle tangent to the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis is <InlineMath math="r" /> away from that axis. In the first quadrant **the centre must be** <InlineMath math="(r,r)" />, so the equation is the equation below.

<BlockMath math="(x-r)^2+(y-r)^2=r^2" />

The point <InlineMath math="(2,1)" /> lies on the circle, so substitute it.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(2-r)^2+(1-r)^2&=r^2 \\
4-4r+r^2+1-2r+r^2&=r^2 \\
r^2-6r+5&=0 \\
(r-1)(r-5)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

Draw both circles that work. **The centre of a circle touching both axes is always on the line <InlineMath math="y=x" />**, and two circles centred on that line pass through <InlineMath math="(2,1)" />.

There are two circles, with <InlineMath math="r=1" /> and <InlineMath math="r=5" />. Both are valid: the equation below contains <InlineMath math="(2,1)" /> because <InlineMath math="1+0=1" />, and the equation below contains it because <InlineMath math="9+16=25" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2=1 \\
(x-5)^2+(y-5)^2=25
\end{gathered}" />

So the largest radius is <InlineMath math="5" />.

## Checking common traps

Let us check why these **common trap choices** miss the mark.

- <InlineMath math="1" /> is the radius of the smaller circle.
- <InlineMath math="3" /> is the average of the two radii, not the radius of either circle.
- <InlineMath math="6" /> is the sum of the two radii, the coefficient of <InlineMath math="r" /> in the quadratic.
- <InlineMath math="\sqrt5" /> is the distance from <InlineMath math="(2,1)" /> to the origin.
