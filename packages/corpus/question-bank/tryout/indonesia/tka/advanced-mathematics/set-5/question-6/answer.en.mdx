Write both divisions as equations, then **substitute the second division into the first**. That makes the factor <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x+2)" /> appear on its own.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(x)&=(x-1)Q(x)+3 \\
Q(x)&=(x+2)H(x)+4
\end{aligned}" />

Replace <InlineMath math="Q(x)" /> in the first equation.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(x)&=(x-1)\big[(x+2)H(x)+4\big]+3 \\
&=(x-1)(x+2)H(x)+4(x-1)+3 \\
&=(x-1)(x+2)H(x)+4x-1
\end{aligned}" />

The part <InlineMath math="4x-1" /> has degree one, less than the divisor's degree, so it is the remainder.

Check with the remainder theorem: <InlineMath math="P(1)=4-1=3" />, matching the remainder on division by <InlineMath math="x-1" />. The first equation also gives <InlineMath math="P(-2)=(-3)Q(-2)+3=(-3)(4)+3=-9" />, and <InlineMath math="4(-2)-1=-9" />.

So the remainder when <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divided by <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x+2)" /> is <InlineMath math="4x-1" />.
