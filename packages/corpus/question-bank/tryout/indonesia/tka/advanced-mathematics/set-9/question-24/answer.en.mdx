The denominator tends to <InlineMath math="0" />. For the limit to exist and be finite, **the numerator must also be <InlineMath math="0" /> at <InlineMath math="x=2" />**. Otherwise the fraction grows without bound.

<BlockMath math="4a+2b-6=0\ \Rightarrow\ b=3-2a" />

With that condition, <InlineMath math="x-2" /> is a factor of the numerator.

<BlockMath math="ax^2+(3-2a)x-6=(x-2)(ax+3)" />

Check the factorisation: <InlineMath math="(x-2)(ax+3)=ax^2+3x-2ax-6" />, which matches. Once the factor <InlineMath math="x-2" /> is cancelled, the limit is just substitution.

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to2}(ax+3)=2a+3=7\ \Rightarrow\ a=2,\quad b=3-4=-1" />

Draw <InlineMath math="y=\tfrac{2x^2-x-6}{x-2}" />. **Its graph is the line <InlineMath math="y=2x+3" /> with one hole at <InlineMath math="x=2" />**, and both sides of the line head to height <InlineMath math="7" /> at that hole.

Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{2x^2-x-6}{x-2}=\frac{(x-2)(2x+3)}{x-2}=2x+3" />, which tends to <InlineMath math="7" /> at <InlineMath math="x=2" />.

So <InlineMath math="a+b=2+(-1)=1" />.

- <InlineMath math="3" /> is <InlineMath math="a-b" />, not <InlineMath math="a+b" />.
- <InlineMath math="-1" /> is just the value of <InlineMath math="b" />.
- <InlineMath math="7" /> is the value of the limit, not <InlineMath math="a+b" />.
- <InlineMath math="-2" /> is the product <InlineMath math="ab" />.
