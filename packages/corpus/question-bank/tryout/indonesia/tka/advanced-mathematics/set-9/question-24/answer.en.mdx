# Given

We are given a **parametric algebraic limit** with unknowns <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> equal to <InlineMath math="7" />.

# Asked

The **values of** <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> making the limit equal <InlineMath math="7" />.

# Solution

> For a finite limit when the denominator vanishes, the numerator must vanish too, locking down the first parameter immediately.

The denominator tends to <InlineMath math="0" />. For the limit to exist and be finite, **the numerator must also be <InlineMath math="0" /> at <InlineMath math="x=2" />**. Otherwise the fraction grows without bound.

<BlockMath math="4a+2b-6=0\ \Rightarrow\ b=3-2a" />

With that condition, <InlineMath math="x-2" /> is a factor of the numerator.

<BlockMath math="ax^2+(3-2a)x-6=(x-2)(ax+3)" />

Check the factorisation: the equation below, which matches. Once the factor <InlineMath math="x-2" /> is cancelled, the limit is just substitution.

<BlockMath math="\begin{gathered}
(x-2)(ax+3)=ax^2+3x-2ax-6 \\
\lim_{x\to2}(ax+3)=2a+3=7\ \Rightarrow\ a=2,\quad b=3-4=-1
\end{gathered}" />

Draw <InlineMath math="y" />. **Its graph is the line <InlineMath math="y=2x+3" /> with one hole at <InlineMath math="x=2" />**, and both sides of the line head to height <InlineMath math="7" /> at that hole.

<BlockMath math="y=\tfrac{2x^2-x-6}{x-2}" />
