The limit laws for sums, differences, products and powers apply whenever the limit of each part exists. For a quotient, **the limit of the denominator must not be zero**.

- **True.** <InlineMath math="\lim\bigl(f+g\bigr)=4+0=4" />.
- **True.** <InlineMath math="\lim f\,g=4\cdot0=0" />.
- **True.** <InlineMath math="\lim\bigl(f^2-g\bigr)=4^2-0=16" />.
- **False.** The denominator tends to <InlineMath math="0" /> while the numerator tends to <InlineMath math="4" />, so the quotient law does not apply and the size of <InlineMath math="\frac fg" /> grows without bound. For example, <InlineMath math="f(x)=4" /> and <InlineMath math="g(x)=x-2" /> give <InlineMath math="\frac{4}{x-2}" />, which does not tend to <InlineMath math="0" />.
- **False.** A limit only describes the values of <InlineMath math="f" /> near <InlineMath math="2" />, not at <InlineMath math="2" /> itself. For example, <InlineMath math="f(x)=4" /> for <InlineMath math="x\neq2" /> with <InlineMath math="f(2)=0" /> still has limit <InlineMath math="4" />.

So the true statements are the first, second and third.
