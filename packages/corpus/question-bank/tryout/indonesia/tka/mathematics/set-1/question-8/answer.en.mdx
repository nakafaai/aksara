Divide the numerator by the denominator to separate the constant part.

<BlockMath math="f(x)=2+\frac5{x-1}" />

The denominator excludes <InlineMath math="x=1" />. The fraction is never zero, so <InlineMath math="y=2" /> is outside the range. The asymptotes <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="y=2" /> intersect at <InlineMath math="(1,2)" />. When <InlineMath math="x>1" />, the fraction is positive, giving <InlineMath math="f(x)>2" />.

On each interval <InlineMath math="(-\infty,1)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,\infty)" />, the reciprocal <InlineMath math="\frac1{x-1}" /> decreases as <InlineMath math="x" /> increases. Multiplying by positive <InlineMath math="5" /> and adding <InlineMath math="2" /> preserves this behavior. For the inverse, solve <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" /> for <InlineMath math="x" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y(x-1)&=2x+3 \\
x(y-2)&=y+3 \\
x&=\frac{y+3}{y-2}
\end{aligned}" />

Thus <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x+3}{x-2}" /> for <InlineMath math="x\ne2" />. The claims about the asymptote intersection, decreasing behavior, values to the right of the asymptote, and the inverse are all true. The value <InlineMath math="f(1)" /> is undefined.
