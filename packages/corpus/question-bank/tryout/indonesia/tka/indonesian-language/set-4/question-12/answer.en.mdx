# Given

In **paragraph three of Classroom Noise**, the physics teacher measures clap decay falling from about <InlineMath math="1.4" /> seconds to <InlineMath math="0.8" /> seconds on a phone app. **Paragraph four** immediately cautions that phone apps are less accurate than acoustic meters and the room was empty.

# Asked

How the content of paragraph four relates to paragraph three.

# Solution

Imagine measuring a table with **a rough school ruler** and noting a promising difference, then adding a reminder that a ruler is less exact than a laser meter. You are not rejecting your result, you are setting careful limits on how strongly to read it.

> In scientific reporting, a caution paragraph right after numbers qualifies how readers should interpret those measurements.

## Reading the qualification in paragraph four

Paragraph four opens directly with <Highlight>Hasil itu menjanjikan, tetapi perlu dibaca dengan hati-hati</Highlight> (that result is promising, yet needs careful reading) because <Highlight>Aplikasi ponsel tidak seteliti alat ukur akustik, dan pengukuran dilakukan di kelas kosong</Highlight> (a phone app is less accurate than an acoustic meter, and testing happened in an empty room).

Therefore, **paragraph four limits how to interpret the measurement results presented in paragraph three**.

## Why the other four options fail

- **Denying that corkboards cut echo** clashes with calling the result *promising*.
- **Explaining how to hang curtains** appears in paragraph three, not four.
- **Citing failing schools** never occurs in the text.
- **Concluding crowded rooms echo more** reverses paragraph four, which states <InlineMath math="30" /> students actually absorb more sound.

# Answer

Paragraph four qualifies and limits how to interpret the measurement results reported in paragraph three.
