# Given

The community leader claims the helmet-loan program **"succeeded in saving children's lives"** after helmet use rose from <InlineMath math="300" /> to <InlineMath math="700" /> children, even though the count measured only helmets worn and noted loose chin straps.

# Asked

The fairest evaluation of the community leader's life-saving claim.

# Solution

Imagine counting **life jackets near a pool** and declaring, **"More jackets mean lives were saved!"** without checking a single swimming report while several buckles stay unclipped. Any claim that leaps past what was actually measured **goes too far**.

> Claims about real-world outcomes like saving lives require direct accident or injury records rather than counts of safety gear alone.

## Testing the claim against the actual evidence

The health worker pinpoints the exact gap: <Highlight>pengamatan itu hanya menghitung helm yang dipakai, bukan jumlah kecelakaan atau cedera</Highlight> (the observation counted only helmets worn, not accidents or injuries). Worse, <Highlight>sebagian helm yang dipakai anak tidak dikencangkan talinya</Highlight> (some chin straps were unfastened), prompting the plan to seek police accident records.

Therefore, **the claim goes too far because the data only counts helmets worn, not accidents or injuries**.

## Why the other four evaluations miss

- **Proven by <InlineMath math="400" /> extra wearers** (<InlineMath math="700 - 300 = 400" />) confuses gear adoption with measured lives saved.
- **Wrong because helmet use fell** reverses the recorded rise from <InlineMath math="300" /> to <InlineMath math="700" />.
- **Right because teachers taught helmet use** mistakes a program activity for outcome proof.
- **Irrelevant because total children differed** blames a minor headcount shift instead of the missing accident metric.

# Answer

The best evaluation is that **the claim goes too far because the data only counted helmets worn rather than accidents or injuries**.
