# Given

During **Mangrove Nursery** monitoring, a missing seedling is never automatically logged as dead because currents may have carried away its marker. Staff log "not found" to keep uncertain observations honest rather than inflating failure counts.

# Asked

Which monitoring principle transfers most cleanly to other field-monitoring projects.

# Solution

Imagine not spotting **a library book on its usual shelf**. That does not prove the book was destroyed, it might be checked out or sitting one shelf over. Separating "not seen" from "confirmed gone" keeps any record trustworthy.

> A universal monitoring principle is separating missing or unverified observations from proven failure.

## Extracting the core principle from paragraph five

Paragraph five states clearly: <Highlight>Bibit yang hilang tidak otomatis dianggap mati karena penanda mungkin terbawa arus</Highlight> (a missing seedling is not automatically counted dead because the marker may have drifted away), so the team records <Highlight>tidak ditemukan</Highlight> (not found) when proof is lacking.

The transferable rule is to **distinguish between not observed and proven to have failed**.

## Why the other four options fail

- **Filling missing records with guesses** creates false certainty that the team intentionally rejects.
- **Ignoring site differences** or **skipping current and flooding logs** violates paragraph three's habitat-matching approach.
- **Stopping after week one** drops the one-month and three-month follow-ups.

# Answer

The most relevant principle for other monitoring projects is **to distinguish between not seen and proven to have failed**.
