# Given

SMA Nusa's **five-day temperature survey** found the back garden (<InlineMath math="30.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}" />) far more comfortable than the open field (<InlineMath math="34.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}" />), leading the school to move two benches there while leaving the separate roles of shade, ground surface, wind, and fans to future tests.

# Asked

Which main conclusion best matches the entire temperature-mapping report.

# Solution

Imagine picking **the coolest bench in a park** for lunch. You already know the spot feels nicer to sit in today, yet you still cannot prove whether the coolness came mostly from leaves, bare soil, or a breeze.

> A sound main conclusion honors both what the early numbers can guide right now and what they still cannot prove.

## Reading the explicit boundary at the end

The closing paragraph states directly: <Highlight>pemindahan bangku merupakan keputusan praktis untuk kenyamanan siswa, sedangkan penyebab perbedaan suhu masih perlu diuji</Highlight> (moving benches is a practical decision for student comfort, while the causes of temperature differences still need testing).

The initial data therefore guide a **practical decision**, yet are not enough to **explain the exact cause**.

## Why the other conclusions fail

- **Claiming trees are the main cause because their spot was coolest** is doubly wrong: the reading room (<InlineMath math="29.9\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}" />) was even cooler, and causes were not isolated.
- **Saying the data cannot be used at all** ignores the real bench relocation.
- **Closing the reading room** is never suggested anywhere in the text.
- **Moving benches to the single lowest-temperature site** misses that the lowest site was the fan-cooled reading room, not the garden.

# Answer

The main conclusion is that **the initial data are useful for a practical decision, yet not sufficient to explain the causes**.
