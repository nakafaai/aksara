# Given

A school announces **"Our air is terrible this morning"** based solely on a **single low-cost sensor spike during morning fog**, even though the text explains fog droplets trigger false optical counts and researchers advise calibrated daily averages.

# Asked

Which evaluation best judges the school's foggy-morning report.

# Solution

Imagine a kitchen scale jumping up while **your wet umbrella is still leaning on it**. You would not declare the flour grew heavier. You would recheck because extra water interfered with the reading. Foggy air tricks optical dust sensors the exact same way.

> Evaluating a sensor-based claim means checking whether environmental conditions like fog distort how the sensor measures particles.

## Testing the school report against paragraph two

Paragraph two explains this exact scenario: <Highlight>Ketika udara lembap, butir air yang sangat kecil ikut terhitung sebagai partikel. Akibatnya, pada pagi berkabut atau saat hujan, angka yang tampil bisa jauh lebih tinggi daripada keadaan sebenarnya</Highlight> (in humid air tiny water droplets count as particles, so on foggy mornings numbers can look far higher than reality).

Thus **the report needs checking because fog humidity can push sensor numbers higher than actual air quality**.

## Why the other four evaluations fail

- **Definitely right** ignores both fog moisture distortion and single-moment reading bias.
- **Definitely wrong because sensors are never accurate** goes too far because calibrated sensors are valuable complements.
- **Trustworthy near a kitchen** makes local distortion worse, and **fog always containing exhaust** invents an unsupported fact.

# Answer

The best evaluation is that **the report needs checking because fog humidity can make sensor readings appear higher than the actual condition**.
