# Given

In **paragraph four of Study Breaks**, the group explains why they hold back from calling the break the cause of higher scores: everyone did the no-break session first, days and classroom mood differed, test difficulty could differ, and all twelve students came from one class.

# Asked

The main idea of paragraph four.

# Solution

Imagine scoring higher on **Thursday's quiz** than Tuesday's. Before crediting one habit alone, you list every rival explanation: maybe you warmed up on Tuesday, maybe the room was quieter, or maybe Thursday's questions ran easier. Listing those rivals is paragraph four's exact core.

> A paragraph's main idea unites every specific detail under the overarching claim introduced in its opening sentence.

## Uniting the rival explanations in paragraph four

Paragraph four opens with <Highlight>Kelompok tidak langsung menyimpulkan bahwa jeda menyebabkan peningkatan</Highlight> (the group did not immediately conclude that breaks caused the gain). It then lists practice carryover from Tuesday, day and room atmosphere, possible difficulty gaps between sets, and single-class sampling.

Every sentence supports one main idea: **there are several alternative explanations that limit a cause-and-effect conclusion**.

## Why the other four options fail

- **Higher averages being enough to prove benefit** directly contradicts the opening sentence.
- **Assuming both sets are equal because of length** clashes with <Highlight>tingkat kesulitan dua paket juga mungkin berbeda</Highlight> (the difficulty of the two sets may also differ).
- **Phone use improving accuracy** is false, since breaks were explicitly phone-free.
- **Sample size representing other classes** contradicts the warning about drawing from just one class.

# Answer

The main idea of paragraph four is that **several alternative explanations limit a cause-and-effect conclusion**.
