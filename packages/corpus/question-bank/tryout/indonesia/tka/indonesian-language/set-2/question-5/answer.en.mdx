# Given

After six sessions, the Repair Clinic reports **<InlineMath math="28" /> items successfully reused**, <InlineMath math="11" /> referred to technicians, and <InlineMath math="9" /> left unrepaired for lack of spare parts, noting that owners chose which broken items to bring.

# Asked

Which evaluation best judges the meaning of the <InlineMath math="28" /> reused items.

# Solution

Imagine bringing **only toys with loose screws** to a class fix-it table and fixing nearly every single one. That tally honestly describes what happened at your table, yet it cannot predict repair odds for every broken toy across town.

> Counts from self-selected items accurately document the event itself, yet cannot be generalized to all unexamined items outside the group.

## Reading the explicit limit in the final paragraph

The closing sentence warns <Highlight>Benda yang dibawa peserta dipilih sendiri oleh pemiliknya, sehingga peluang perbaikan untuk seluruh kerusakan rumah tangga masih belum diketahui</Highlight> (items brought were chosen by their owners, so repair odds across all household damage remain unknown).

Thus <InlineMath math="28" /> genuinely **describes participant activity, yet cannot be generalized** beyond the clinic's self-selected items.

## Why the other evaluations fail

- **Saying <InlineMath math="28" /> percent** confuses a raw count (<InlineMath math="28" /> out of <InlineMath math="48" /> items) with a percentage.
- **Claiming repair was always cheaper** goes beyond a count of working items.
- **Predicting unbrought items** or **proving identical outcomes at other schools** directly breaks the sample-selection warning.

# Answer

The best evaluation is that the figure of <InlineMath math="28" /> describes the participants' activity, yet cannot be generalized.
