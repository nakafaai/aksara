Compare the two equations as two lines. The second equation is <InlineMath math="3(x+2y)=b" />. If the first equation also has the form <InlineMath math="x+2y=\ldots" />, which happens when <InlineMath math="a=1" />, **the two lines are parallel or the same line**. If <InlineMath math="a\ne1" />, the slopes differ, so the lines cross at exactly one point.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a=1:\quad x+2y=4\ &\iff\ 3x+6y=12
\end{aligned}" />

- For <InlineMath math="a=2" /> the first line has slope <InlineMath math="-1" /> and the second has slope <InlineMath math="-\frac12" />. They always cross, whatever <InlineMath math="b" /> is. This statement is false.
- For <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="b=12" /> the second equation is three times the first. The lines coincide, so there are infinitely many solutions. This statement is true.
- For <InlineMath math="a=3" /> and <InlineMath math="b=6" />, subtract the first equation from the second: <InlineMath math="4y=2" />, so <InlineMath math="y=\frac12" /> and <InlineMath math="3x=4-1" />, giving <InlineMath math="x=1" />. Then <InlineMath math="x+y=\frac32" />, not <InlineMath math="1" />. This statement is false.
- For <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="b=10" /> the second equation becomes <InlineMath math="x+2y=\frac{10}{3}" />, while the first is <InlineMath math="x+2y=4" />. The lines are parallel and distinct, so there is no solution. This statement is true.
