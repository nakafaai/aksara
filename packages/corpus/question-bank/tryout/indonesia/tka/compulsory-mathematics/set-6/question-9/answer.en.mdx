# A handier form

The five statements cover the range, a composition, the inverse and the fixed points of <InlineMath math="f" />. One rewrite of <InlineMath math="f" /> helps a lot. **Split the fraction into a constant plus a remainder**:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=\frac{2x+6}{x-3}=\frac{2(x-3)+12}{x-3}=2+\frac{12}{x-3}" />

The fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{12}{x-3}" /> is never zero, and it can take any value other than zero.

# Statement one

For <InlineMath math="f(x)=2" />, the fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{12}{x-3}" /> would have to be zero, which can't happen. Another way: <InlineMath math="2x+6=2(x-3)" /> gives <InlineMath math="6=-6" />, which is false. So **<InlineMath math="2" /> is not in the range of <InlineMath math="f" />**, and this statement is **True**.

# Statement two

The value <InlineMath math="f(f(x))" /> needs two conditions: <InlineMath math="x\neq3" /> so that <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> exists, and **<InlineMath math="f(x)\neq3" /> so that <InlineMath math="f" /> can be applied again**. Find when <InlineMath math="f(x)=3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{2x+6}{x-3}&=3 \\
2x+6&=3x-9 \\
x&=15
\end{aligned}" />

At <InlineMath math="x=15" />, <InlineMath math="f(15)=\frac{36}{12}=3" />, and then <InlineMath math="f(3)" /> is undefined. So <InlineMath math="f\circ f" /> is also undefined at <InlineMath math="x=15" />, and this statement is **False**.

# Statement three

Write <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" />, solve for <InlineMath math="x" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="y" />, then swap the names:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y(x-3)&=2x+6 \\
xy-2x&=3y+6 \\
x(y-2)&=3y+6 \\
x&=\frac{3y+6}{y-2}
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(x)=\frac{3x+6}{x-2}" />, and **the condition <InlineMath math="x\neq2" /> matches statement one**, because the domain of the inverse is the range of <InlineMath math="f" />. Check one value: <InlineMath math="f(0)=-2" /> and <InlineMath math="f^{-1}(-2)=\frac{0}{-4}=0" />. This statement is **True**.

# Statement four

The intersection points with the line <InlineMath math="y=x" /> satisfy <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x+6&=x(x-3) \\
x^{2}-5x-6&=0 \\
(x-6)(x+1)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

That gives <InlineMath math="x=6" /> or <InlineMath math="x=-1" />. **Neither of them is <InlineMath math="3" />**, so both count: <InlineMath math="f(6)=\frac{18}{3}=6" /> and <InlineMath math="f(-1)=\frac{4}{-4}=-1" />. There are exactly two points and their x-coordinates add up to <InlineMath math="6+(-1)=5" />. This statement is **True**.

# Statement five

This statement says the same thing as <InlineMath math="f" /> being its own inverse. The inverse in statement three has a different formula from <InlineMath math="f" />, so **one counterexample is enough**. Take <InlineMath math="p=0" />: <InlineMath math="q=f(0)=-2" />, but

<BlockMath math="f(-2)=\frac{-4+6}{-5}=-\frac{2}{5}\neq0" />

This statement is **False**.

So the true statements are statements one, three and four.
