You have to check five transformations one by one. Instead of finding the image equation five times, first find **the two features that pin down the target parabola**, then test each transformation against those two features.

# Two features of the target parabola

Rewrite the target in vertex form by completing the square:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y&=-2x^2+12x-15 \\
&=-2\left(x^2-6x\right)-15 \\
&=-2\left(x-3\right)^2+18-15 \\
&=-2\left(x-3\right)^2+3
\end{aligned}" />

So its vertex is <InlineMath math="(3,3)" /> and its leading coefficient is <InlineMath math="-2" />. The parabola <InlineMath math="y=x^2" /> has its vertex at <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" /> and coefficient <InlineMath math="1" />. A correct transformation must **carry the vertex <InlineMath math="O" /> to <InlineMath math="(3,3)" /> and turn the coefficient <InlineMath math="1" /> into <InlineMath math="-2" />**.

# What a dilation does to the coefficient

Take a point <InlineMath math="(t,t^2)" /> on <InlineMath math="y=x^2" /> and dilate it about <InlineMath math="O" /> by the factor <InlineMath math="k" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(t,t^2)&\to(kt,\,kt^2) \\
X=kt,\quad Y&=kt^2=k\left(\frac{X}{k}\right)^2=\frac{X^2}{k}
\end{aligned}" />

The coefficient becomes <InlineMath math="\frac1k" />, **not <InlineMath math="k" />**. A dilation about another centre is a dilation about <InlineMath math="O" /> followed by a translation, so its coefficient is also <InlineMath math="\frac1k" />. A <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation is a dilation with factor <InlineMath math="-1" />, and a translation doesn't change the coefficient. In a composition the factors multiply. To get the coefficient <InlineMath math="-2" />, the product of the factors has to be <InlineMath math="-\frac12" />.

The image of a point <InlineMath math="X" /> under a dilation with centre <InlineMath math="C" /> and factor <InlineMath math="k" /> is <InlineMath math="C+k(X-C)" />. That's the rule you'll use to track the vertex.

# Judging each statement

**Statement 1.** The factor is <InlineMath math="-\frac12" />, so the coefficient is <InlineMath math="-2" />. The vertex <InlineMath math="O" /> moves to

<BlockMath math="(2,2)-\tfrac12\bigl((0,0)-(2,2)\bigr)=(2,2)+(1,1)=(3,3)" />

Check one more point: <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(2,2)-\frac12(-1,-1)=\left(\frac52,\frac52\right)" />, and <InlineMath math="-2\left(\frac52-3\right)^2+3=\frac52" />. **True**.

**Statement 2.** The <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation about <InlineMath math="\left(\frac32,\frac32\right)" /> sends <InlineMath math="O" /> to <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />, because the centre of the rotation is the midpoint of <InlineMath math="O" /> and its image. The dilation about <InlineMath math="(3,3)" /> doesn't move the point <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />, so the vertex stays there. The product of the factors is <InlineMath math="(-1)\cdot\frac12=-\frac12" />. **True**.

**Statement 3.** The vertex does reach <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />, but the factor is <InlineMath math="-2" />, so the coefficient is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{-2}=-\frac12" />. The image is <InlineMath math="y=-\frac12(x-3)^2+3" />, a wider parabola. This is the trap of **taking the coefficient to be the scale factor**. **False**.

**Statement 4.** The factor is exactly <InlineMath math="-\frac12" />, but the vertex moves to

<BlockMath math="(3,3)-\tfrac12\bigl((0,0)-(3,3)\bigr)=(3,3)+\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac32\right)=\left(\tfrac92,\tfrac92\right)" />

The centre of a dilation is the point that stays put, not the place the vertex goes to. **False**.

**Statement 5.** The translation sends <InlineMath math="O" /> to <InlineMath math="(-6,-6)" />, and then the dilation about <InlineMath math="O" /> with factor <InlineMath math="-\frac12" /> sends it to <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />. The factor is <InlineMath math="-\frac12" />, so the coefficient is <InlineMath math="-2" />. Order matters here: translating by <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\end{pmatrix}" /> first would put the vertex at <InlineMath math="\left(-\frac32,-\frac32\right)" />. **True**.

So the transformations that map <InlineMath math="y=x^2" /> exactly onto <InlineMath math="y=-2x^2+12x-15" /> are statements 1, 2, and 5.
