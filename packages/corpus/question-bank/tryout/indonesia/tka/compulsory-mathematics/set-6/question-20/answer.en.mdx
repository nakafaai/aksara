# The height of the pole

The sun ray passing the top of the pole makes a <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> angle with the ground. Since <InlineMath math="\tan45^\circ=1" />, the ray drops <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> for every <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> across. By the time it reaches the wall it has gone <InlineMath math="6\text{ m}" /> across and dropped <InlineMath math="6\text{ m}" />, and it hits the wall at a height of <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
h-6\cdot\tan45^\circ&=3 \\
h-6&=3 \\
h&=9\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

So **the pole is <InlineMath math="9\text{ m}" /> tall**, not <InlineMath math="6\text{ m}" />. At <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, the part of the shadow on the ground is only <InlineMath math="6\text{ m}" />, because the wall stops it.

# The shadow at sixty degrees

Without a wall, the shadow's length is the pole's height divided by <InlineMath math="\tan60^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{9}{\tan60^\circ}=\frac{9}{\sqrt3}=3\sqrt3\approx5.2\text{ m}" />

Since <InlineMath math="3\sqrt3\approx5.2" /> is less than <InlineMath math="6" />, **the shadow doesn't reach the wall** and lies entirely on the ground.
