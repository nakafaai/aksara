The question asks for the largest number of whole cones. No metal is lost, so the metal in the ball is exactly the metal available for the cones. There are two traps before you divide: **the ball is hollow**, and the cone's height isn't given.

# The volume of the metal

The metal is **the big ball minus the cavity inside it**. A ball has volume <InlineMath math="\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3" />, so:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{metal}}&=\frac{4}{3}\pi\left(12^3-3^3\right) \\
&=\frac{4}{3}\pi\,(1{,}728-27) \\
&=\frac{4}{3}\pi\cdot1{,}701=2{,}268\pi\text{ cm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

# The height and volume of one cone

The slant height is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose other sides are the base radius and the height of the cone. **Use Pythagoras for the height**, then work out the volume:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
h&=\sqrt{10^2-6^2}=\sqrt{64}=8\text{ cm} \\
V_{\text{cone}}&=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h=\frac{1}{3}\pi\cdot36\cdot8=96\pi\text{ cm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

# The number of cones

Divide the metal's volume by the volume of one cone:

<BlockMath math="\frac{2{,}268\pi}{96\pi}=23.625" />

The cones have to be whole, so **round down** to <InlineMath math="23" />. Check both sides. Twenty-three cones use <InlineMath math="23\cdot96\pi=2{,}208\pi" />, which fits, and the leftover <InlineMath math="60\pi" /> is less than one cone. Twenty-four cones would need <InlineMath math="2{,}304\pi" />, more than the metal you have.

So the largest number of cones that can be cast is <InlineMath math="23" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="24" /> comes from rounding <InlineMath math="23.625" /> up or to the nearest whole number. You also get it if you forget the cavity, because <InlineMath math="\frac{4}{3}\pi\cdot12^3=2{,}304\pi" /> is exactly enough for <InlineMath math="24" /> cones. **There isn't enough metal for a 24th cone**.
- <InlineMath math="18" /> uses the slant height <InlineMath math="10" /> as the height, so one cone becomes <InlineMath math="120\pi" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{2{,}268}{120}=18.9" />.
- <InlineMath math="10" /> uses <InlineMath math="(12-3)^3=729" /> instead of <InlineMath math="12^3-3^3" />, so the metal becomes <InlineMath math="972\pi" />. The difference of two cubes isn't the cube of the difference.
- <InlineMath math="7" /> uses the cylinder formula <InlineMath math="\pi r^2h=288\pi" /> for a cone and forgets the factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}" />.
