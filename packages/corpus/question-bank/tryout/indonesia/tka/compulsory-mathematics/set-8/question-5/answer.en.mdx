Write the information as one equation. **One equation in two unknowns doesn't fix <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />**, but it can fix how they are related.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
3x+2y&=2x+3y+500 \\
x-y&=500
\end{aligned}" />

Only the difference is certain: an exercise book always costs <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}500" /> more than a pencil. For example, <InlineMath math="x=3{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="y=2{,}500" /> satisfy the equation, and so do <InlineMath math="x=5{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="y=4{,}500" />.

So the statement that must be true is that an exercise book costs <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}500" /> more than a pencil.

- The price of the exercise book can't be pinned down, since <InlineMath math="x=3{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="x=5{,}000" /> are both possible.
- The book costs twice the pencil only when <InlineMath math="x=1{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="y=500" />, not in every case.
- The difference is the wrong way round. From <InlineMath math="x-y=500" />, the exercise book is the dearer one.
- The sum <InlineMath math="x+y" /> can't be pinned down. In the two examples above it is <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}5{,}500" /> and <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}9{,}500" />.
