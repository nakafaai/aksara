<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}

# Judging each statement

**Statement 1**. A fixed point would need <InlineMath math="(x+6,-y)=(x,y)" />. The equation <InlineMath math="x+6=x" /> means <InlineMath math="6=0" />, which is impossible. Not a single point stays put. **True**.

**Statement 2**. Take any point on that line, <InlineMath math="(3,t)" />:

<BlockMath math="T(3,t)=(3+6,\,-t)=(9,\,-t)" />

All the images lie on the line <InlineMath math="x=9" />, not on <InlineMath math="x=3" />. A mirror line doesn't move under the reflection alone, but here the rotation first carries <InlineMath math="(3,t)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-3,-t)" />, far from the mirror. **False**.

**Statement 3**. A point on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis looks like <InlineMath math="(t,0)" />, and its image <InlineMath math="(t+6,0)" /> is still on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Going the other way, every point <InlineMath math="(s,0)" /> is the image of <InlineMath math="(s-6,0)" />, so the image covers the whole <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. The points move, but the line stays. **True**.

**Statement 4**. A point on the line <InlineMath math="y=x" /> looks like <InlineMath math="(t,t)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
T(t,t)&=(t+6,\,-t) \\
X=t+6,\quad Y&=-t \\
Y&=-(X-6)=-X+6
\end{aligned}" />

Check with two points: <InlineMath math="(0,0)" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(6,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(7,-1)" />. Both satisfy <InlineMath math="y=-x+6" />. **True**.

**Statement 5**. Apply the formula for <InlineMath math="T" /> twice:

<BlockMath math="T\bigl(T(x,y)\bigr)=T(x+6,\,-y)=\bigl((x+6)+6,\,-(-y)\bigr)=(x+12,\,y)" />

The flip over the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis happens twice and cancels out, while the slides add up. Every point moves <InlineMath math="12" /> units to the right. **True**.

So the true statements are statements 1, 3, 4, and 5.
