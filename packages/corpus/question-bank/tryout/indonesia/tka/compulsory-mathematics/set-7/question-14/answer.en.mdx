The question asks for the centre <InlineMath math="P" /> of the dilation. The catch is that the image vertices **come without names**. So first you match each vertex with its image, and then you find the scale factor <InlineMath math="k" /> together with its sign.

# Match the vertices

A dilation with factor <InlineMath math="k" /> multiplies every side vector by <InlineMath math="k" />, that is <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{X'Y'}=k\,\overrightarrow{XY}" />. That's why angles stay the same and every length is multiplied by <InlineMath math="|k|" />.

Triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" /> has its right angle at <InlineMath math="A" />, with <InlineMath math="AB=3" /> horizontal and <InlineMath math="AC=4" /> vertical. In the image, the point <InlineMath math="(-3,0)" /> is <InlineMath math="6" /> across from <InlineMath math="(-9,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="8" /> straight above <InlineMath math="(-3,-8)" />, so the right angle sits at <InlineMath math="(-3,0)" />. **A right angle has to land on a right angle**, and the short leg on the short leg:

<BlockMath math="A'=(-3,0),\qquad B'=(-9,0),\qquad C'=(-3,-8)" />

# Find the scale factor

Compare each side vector before and after the dilation.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\overrightarrow{AB}&=(3,0), & \overrightarrow{A'B'}&=(-6,0)=-2\,(3,0) \\
\overrightarrow{AC}&=(0,4), & \overrightarrow{A'C'}&=(0,-8)=-2\,(0,4)
\end{aligned}" />

Both sides give the same factor, so <InlineMath math="k=-2" />. The lengths alone only tell you <InlineMath math="|k|=2" />. **The minus sign comes from the sides pointing the other way**: <InlineMath math="B" /> is to the right of <InlineMath math="A" />, but <InlineMath math="B'" /> is to the left of <InlineMath math="A'" />.

# Find the centre

A dilation with centre <InlineMath math="P" /> means <InlineMath math="A'-P=k\,(A-P)" />. Collect every <InlineMath math="P" /> on one side, then put in <InlineMath math="k=-2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A'-P&=kA-kP \\
A'-kA&=(1-k)\,P \\
P&=\frac{A'-kA}{1-k}=\frac{(-3,0)+2\,(3,3)}{1+2}=\frac{(3,6)}{3}=(1,2)
\end{aligned}" />

Now check the other two vertices. **The right centre has to work for all three points**, not just for <InlineMath math="A" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P+k\,(B-P)&=(1,2)-2\,(5,1)=(-9,0)=B' \\
P+k\,(C-P)&=(1,2)-2\,(2,5)=(-3,-8)=C'
\end{aligned}" />

In the figure, the three lines joining each vertex to its image all meet at one point, and that point is <InlineMath math="P" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="(9,6)" /> comes from using <InlineMath math="k=2" /> just because the sides are twice as long. With that centre, <InlineMath math="B" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(3,0)" />, which isn't a vertex of the image. **Sides that flip direction need a negative <InlineMath math="k" />**.
- <InlineMath math="\left(0,\tfrac{3}{2}\right)" /> is the midpoint of <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="A'" />. The centre sits exactly halfway only when <InlineMath math="k=-1" />. Here <InlineMath math="PA'=2\,PA" />.
- <InlineMath math="(-1,1)" /> comes from <InlineMath math="k=-\tfrac{1}{2}" />, the ratio turned upside down. That factor takes the image back to the original triangle, not the other way round.
- <InlineMath math="(-1,-2)" /> is a sign slip when moving terms across, that is computing <InlineMath math="\frac{kA-A'}{1-k}" />. It gives the true centre reflected through the origin.

So the centre of the dilation is <InlineMath math="P(1,2)" />, with scale factor <InlineMath math="k=-2" />.
