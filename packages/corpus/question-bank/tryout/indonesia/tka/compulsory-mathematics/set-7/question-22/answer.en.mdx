All three statements are about changes in a mean salary. The safest way is to **always work with salary totals, not with means**, and divide by the number of people only at the end. Every amount below is in millions of rupiah.

# The March situation

From the table, the sewing total is <InlineMath math="15\cdot4.8=72" /> and the design total is <InlineMath math="5\cdot7.2=36" />, so the overall total is <InlineMath math="108" /> for <InlineMath math="20" /> people and **the mean is <InlineMath math="5.4" />**.

# Judging each statement

1. **False**. The design raise adds <InlineMath math="0.25\cdot36=9" />, but the sewing cut removes <InlineMath math="0.15\cdot72=10.8" />. The total falls by <InlineMath math="1.8" />, so the mean drops to <InlineMath math="5.31" />. **The percentages have to be weighted by each department's salary total**. A plain average of <InlineMath math="+25\%" /> and <InlineMath math="-15\%" />, which is <InlineMath math="+5\%" />, makes it look like a rise.
2. **True**. With <InlineMath math="k" /> new employees, the mean is <InlineMath math="\frac{108+7.2k}{20+k}" />. The denominator is positive, so a mean of at least <InlineMath math="6" /> is the same as <InlineMath math="108+7.2k\ge120+6k" />, that is <InlineMath math="1.2k\ge12" /> or <InlineMath math="k\ge10" />. Both directions hold, so the words **exactly when** are right.
3. **True**. The total becomes <InlineMath math="108-9=99" /> for <InlineMath math="17" /> people, so the mean is <InlineMath math="\frac{99}{17}\approx5.82" />. The rise is about <InlineMath math="0.42" />, more than <InlineMath math="0.4" />. If you keep counting <InlineMath math="20" /> people, the mean even seems to fall.
