# Eliminate without dropping a factor

The question has two conditions: the system must have **exactly one** solution, and that solution must satisfy <InlineMath math="x>0" /> and <InlineMath math="y>0" />. The safest route is to eliminate one variable while keeping every factor that contains <InlineMath math="k" /> in plain view.

From the second equation, <InlineMath math="2x=(k+1)-(k+1)y=(k+1)(1-y)" />. Multiply the first equation by <InlineMath math="2" /> and replace <InlineMath math="2x" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(k+3)\cdot2x+8y&=4(k+1) \\
(k+3)(k+1)(1-y)+8y&=4(k+1) \\
\left[8-(k+3)(k+1)\right]y&=4(k+1)-(k+3)(k+1) \\
-(k+5)(k-1)\,y&=-(k+1)(k-1) \\
(k+5)(k-1)\,y&=(k+1)(k-1)
\end{aligned}" />

In the third line, <InlineMath math="8-(k^2+4k+3)=-(k^2+4k-5)=-(k+5)(k-1)" /> and <InlineMath math="(k+1)(4-k-3)=-(k+1)(k-1)" />.

# Three possibilities

The last equation has the form <InlineMath math="ay=b" />, so there are three cases.

- If <InlineMath math="k=1" />, it becomes <InlineMath math="0\cdot y=0" />. Every <InlineMath math="y" /> works, so the system has **infinitely many** solutions. Indeed, for <InlineMath math="k=1" /> both equations turn into <InlineMath math="x+y=1" />.
- If <InlineMath math="k=-5" />, it becomes <InlineMath math="0\cdot y=24" />, so there's no solution.
- For every other <InlineMath math="k" />, the solution is unique:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y&=\frac{k+1}{k+5} \\
x&=\frac{(k+1)(1-y)}{2}=\frac{(k+1)}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{k+5}=\frac{2(k+1)}{k+5}
\end{aligned}" />

# The positive condition

Since <InlineMath math="x=2y" />, you only need <InlineMath math="y>0" />. The numerator <InlineMath math="k+1" /> and the denominator <InlineMath math="k+5" /> must have the same sign:

| Interval | <InlineMath math="k+1" /> | <InlineMath math="k+5" /> | <InlineMath math="y" /> |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="k<-5" /> | negative | negative | positive |
| <InlineMath math="-5<k<-1" /> | negative | positive | negative |
| <InlineMath math="k>-1" /> | positive | positive | positive |

So <InlineMath math="y>0" /> when <InlineMath math="k<-5" /> or <InlineMath math="k>-1" />. Now remember the case <InlineMath math="k=1" />. It sits inside <InlineMath math="k>-1" />, but there the system has infinitely many solutions, not exactly one. So **<InlineMath math="k=1" /> has to be removed**.

Check <InlineMath math="k=-6" />: <InlineMath math="y=\frac{-5}{-1}=5" /> and <InlineMath math="x=10" />. Substituting: <InlineMath math="-3\cdot10+4\cdot5=-10=2(-6)+2" /> and <InlineMath math="2\cdot10-5\cdot5=-5=-6+1" />.

So the set is <InlineMath math="k<-5" /> or <InlineMath math="-1<k<1" /> or <InlineMath math="k>1" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="-1<k<1" /> or <InlineMath math="k>1" /> drops the branch <InlineMath math="k<-5" />. A fraction is also positive when **the numerator and the denominator are both negative**.
- <InlineMath math="k<-5" /> or <InlineMath math="k>-1" /> forgets to remove <InlineMath math="k=1" />. That happens when you cancel the factor <InlineMath math="k-1" /> on both sides, which hides the case with infinitely many solutions.
- <InlineMath math="-5<k<-1" /> flips the sign table. On that interval <InlineMath math="y" /> is actually negative.
- <InlineMath math="k\neq-5" /> and <InlineMath math="k\neq1" /> only makes the solution unique. It never uses the conditions <InlineMath math="x>0" /> and <InlineMath math="y>0" />.
