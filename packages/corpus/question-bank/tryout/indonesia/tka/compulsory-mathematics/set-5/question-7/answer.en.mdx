Using two price cuts one after another means **composing two functions**. Discount first, then voucher is <InlineMath math="(g\circ f)(x)" />. Voucher first, then discount is <InlineMath math="(f\circ g)(x)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(g\circ f)(400{,}000)&=g(320{,}000)=320{,}000-50{,}000=270{,}000 \\
(f\circ g)(400{,}000)&=f(350{,}000)=0.8\cdot350{,}000=280{,}000
\end{aligned}" />

The difference is <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}10{,}000" />. The general forms show why: <InlineMath math="(g\circ f)(x)=0.8x-50{,}000" /> and <InlineMath math="(f\circ g)(x)=0.8x-40{,}000" />. If the voucher comes first, the discount also shrinks the voucher, so it is only worth <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}40{,}000" />.

So using the discount first is cheaper by <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}10{,}000" />.

- Voucher first is actually <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}10{,}000" /> more expensive.
- <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}40{,}000" /> is the voucher's value after the discount, not the difference between the prices.
- <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}50{,}000" /> is the voucher's face value, not the difference between the two orders.
- The order matters because the percentage discount acts on different prices.
