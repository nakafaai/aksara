# When a line lands back on itself

You need to check five transformations one at a time. The question is always the same: does **every point of <InlineMath math="g" /> still land on <InlineMath math="g" />**? First note two facts about <InlineMath math="g: 2x-y=4" />, which is <InlineMath math="y=2x-4" />.

- Its gradient is <InlineMath math="2" />, so the line runs in the direction of the vector <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}" />.
- Some points on <InlineMath math="g" />: <InlineMath math="(2,0)" />, <InlineMath math="(3,2)" />, <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" />, and <InlineMath math="(4,4)" />. A point lies on <InlineMath math="g" /> when <InlineMath math="2x-y" /> equals exactly <InlineMath math="4" />.

That gives you four rules. A translation keeps <InlineMath math="g" /> when its vector runs along <InlineMath math="g" />. A <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation and a dilation keep <InlineMath math="g" /> when the centre lies on <InlineMath math="g" />. A reflection keeps <InlineMath math="g" /> when the mirror is <InlineMath math="g" /> itself or perpendicular to <InlineMath math="g" />. For a composition, don't judge the last step alone. Combine the steps into one transformation first.

# Judging each statement

Now go through the five statements in order. Each verdict uses one of the rules above and is then checked with one sample point. **A sample point is only a check**, not a proof, because the proof comes from the general rule.

## A reflection in a perpendicular line

Statement 1. **True**. The line <InlineMath math="x+2y=12" /> has gradient <InlineMath math="-\tfrac12" />, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot\left(-\tfrac12\right)=-1" />, so this mirror is perpendicular to <InlineMath math="g" />. In a reflection, every point moves perpendicular to the mirror, which here means it moves along <InlineMath math="g" /> itself. So its image stays on <InlineMath math="g" />. For example, the point <InlineMath math="(2,0)" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(6,8)" />, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot6-8=4" />.

## A rotation and then a translation along the line

Statement 2. **False**. Follow a point <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> through both steps, then find the equation of the image:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(x,y)\ \xrightarrow{\ 180^\circ,\ O\ }\ (-x,-y)\ &\xrightarrow{\ \text{translation}\ }\ (2-x,\ 4-y) \\
x=2-x',\quad y=4-y'\quad&\Rightarrow\quad 2(2-x')-(4-y')=4 \\
&\Rightarrow\quad 2x-y=-4
\end{aligned}" />

The image is parallel to <InlineMath math="g" /> but it isn't <InlineMath math="g" />. The trap is the vector <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}2\\4\end{pmatrix}" />, which really does run along <InlineMath math="g" />. That translation only slides a line along itself, but the rotation has already moved the line to <InlineMath math="2x-y=-4" />. Together the two steps form a <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation about <InlineMath math="(1,2)" />, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot1-2=0\ne4" />, so the centre isn't on <InlineMath math="g" />.

## Two perpendicular reflections

Statement 3. **True**. Reflecting in <InlineMath math="x=3" /> sends <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(6-x,y)" />, and then reflecting in <InlineMath math="y=2" /> sends it to <InlineMath math="(6-x,4-y)" />. That's a <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation about <InlineMath math="(3,2)" />, and this centre lies on <InlineMath math="g" /> because <InlineMath math="2\cdot3-2=4" />. Notice that the first step alone moves <InlineMath math="g" /> to <InlineMath math="2x+y=8" />. It's the second step that brings it back.

## A dilation with a negative factor

Statement 4. **True**. The centre <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="g" /> because <InlineMath math="2\cdot1-(-2)=4" />. A dilation moves each point <InlineMath math="P" /> along the line through the centre and <InlineMath math="P" />, and for <InlineMath math="P" /> on <InlineMath math="g" /> that line is <InlineMath math="g" /> itself. A negative factor just throws the point to the other side of the centre, still on the same line. For example, <InlineMath math="(2,0)" /> goes to <InlineMath math="(1,-2)-3\cdot(1,2)=(-2,-8)" />, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot(-2)-(-8)=4" />.

## A translation given by two points

Statement 5. **True**. The translation vector is <InlineMath math="(4-1,\ 9-3)=(3,6)=3\cdot(1,2)" />, which runs along <InlineMath math="g" />. The point <InlineMath math="(1,3)" /> isn't on <InlineMath math="g" />, but that doesn't matter. A translation moves every point by the same vector, so only the vector counts. For example, <InlineMath math="(2,0)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(5,6)" />, and <InlineMath math="2\cdot5-6=4" />.

So the true statements are statements 1, 3, 4, and 5.
