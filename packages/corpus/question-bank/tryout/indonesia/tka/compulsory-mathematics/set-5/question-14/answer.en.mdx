# The equation of the image line

The question asks for the area of a triangle bounded by three lines: the line <InlineMath math="g" />, its image <InlineMath math="g'" />, and the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. So you need the equation of <InlineMath math="g'" /> first, then the three corners of the triangle.

A <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> rotation **clockwise** about <InlineMath math="O" /> sends the point <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(y,-x)" />. For example, the point <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> on the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis moves down to <InlineMath math="(0,-1)" />. To get the image equation, write the old coordinates in terms of the new ones, then substitute them into the equation of <InlineMath math="g" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x'=y,\quad y'=-x\quad&\Rightarrow\quad x=-y',\quad y=x' \\
x+2y=10\quad&\Rightarrow\quad -y'+2x'=10 \\
g'&: 2x-y=10
\end{aligned}" />

Check it with one point. The point <InlineMath math="(10,0)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="g" />, and its image <InlineMath math="(0,-10)" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="2\cdot0-(-10)=10" />. The equation fits.

# The three corners

Two corners sit on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, where <InlineMath math="g" /> and <InlineMath math="g'" /> cross it. The third corner is **the point where <InlineMath math="g" /> and <InlineMath math="g'" /> meet**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
g\text{ with }x=0&: 2y=10\Rightarrow P(0,5) \\
g'\text{ with }x=0&: -y=10\Rightarrow Q(0,-10) \\
y=2x-10\text{ into }g&: x+2(2x-10)=10\Rightarrow x=6,\ y=2\Rightarrow R(6,2)
\end{aligned}" />

# The area

The base of the triangle is <InlineMath math="PQ" />, which lies along the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Because the base is vertical, **the height is the horizontal distance from <InlineMath math="R" /> to the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis**, which is the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate of <InlineMath math="R" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
PQ&=5-(-10)=15 \\
\text{height}&=6 \\
\text{area}&=\tfrac12\cdot15\cdot6=45
\end{aligned}" />

Here's a neat check. A <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> rotation makes <InlineMath math="g'" /> perpendicular to <InlineMath math="g" /> (their gradients are <InlineMath math="-\tfrac12" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />), so the angle at <InlineMath math="R" /> is a right angle. With <InlineMath math="RP=\sqrt{45}" /> and <InlineMath math="RQ=\sqrt{180}" />, the area is <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\sqrt{45\cdot180}=\tfrac12\cdot90=45" />. Same result.

So the area of the triangle bounded by <InlineMath math="g" />, <InlineMath math="g'" />, and the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis is <InlineMath math="45" /> square units.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="5" /> comes from the **counterclockwise** rule <InlineMath math="(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)" />, or from plugging <InlineMath math="y" /> for <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="-x" /> for <InlineMath math="y" /> straight into the equation of <InlineMath math="g" />. The image becomes <InlineMath math="-2x+y=10" />, the lines meet at <InlineMath math="(-2,6)" />, the base is <InlineMath math="10-5=5" />, and the area is <InlineMath math="5" />.
- <InlineMath math="15" /> uses the <InlineMath math="y" />-coordinate of <InlineMath math="R" />, which is <InlineMath math="2" />, as the height. The base is vertical, so the height has to be horizontal.
- <InlineMath math="25" /> is the area of the triangle that <InlineMath math="g'" /> makes with both axes, <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\cdot5\cdot10" />. That's not the triangle the question asks about.
- <InlineMath math="90" /> forgets the <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" /> in the triangle area formula.
