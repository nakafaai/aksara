# The first statement

For <InlineMath math="x=1" /> to <InlineMath math="6" />, <InlineMath math="f" /> always stays below <InlineMath math="g" />. The closest call is <InlineMath math="x=6" />: <InlineMath math="64<65" />, **a gap of only <InlineMath math="1" />**, so <InlineMath math="6" /> doesn't work yet. At <InlineMath math="x=7" />, <InlineMath math="128>75" />. The statement is **True**.

# The second statement

Look at what happens when <InlineMath math="x" /> goes up by <InlineMath math="1" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
f(x+1)-f(x)&=2^{x+1}-2^x=2^x \\
g(x+1)-g(x)&=10
\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="x\ge7" />, <InlineMath math="f" /> grows by at least <InlineMath math="2^7=128" /> per step, far more than the <InlineMath math="10" /> that <InlineMath math="g" /> always adds. At <InlineMath math="x=7" />, <InlineMath math="f" /> is already ahead by <InlineMath math="128-75=53" />, and **every further step makes that lead bigger**. The statement is **True**.

# The third statement

Check every integer, including the negative ones. For <InlineMath math="x\le-1" />, <InlineMath math="2^x" /> is always positive, while <InlineMath math="10x+5\le-5" />, so <InlineMath math="f(x)>g(x)" />. For <InlineMath math="x\ge7" />, the second statement already showed <InlineMath math="f(x)>g(x)" />. That leaves <InlineMath math="x=0,1,2,3,4,5,6" />, and the table shows <InlineMath math="f(x)<g(x)" /> for all seven, including <InlineMath math="x=0" />, because <InlineMath math="1<5" />.

There are **seven, not six**. The statement is **False**.

# The fourth statement

One counterexample is enough. At <InlineMath math="x=3" />, <InlineMath math="f(3)=8" /> and <InlineMath math="h(3)=9" />, so <InlineMath math="f(3)<h(3)" />. At <InlineMath math="x=4" /> they're equal, <InlineMath math="16=16" />, and only from <InlineMath math="x=5" /> on is <InlineMath math="2^x" /> bigger. **The statement fails at <InlineMath math="x=3" />**, so it's **False**.

# The fifth statement

The hard part is <InlineMath math="g(x)<h(x)" />, that is <InlineMath math="x^2>10x+5" />. The quadratic only passes the linear function after <InlineMath math="x=10" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x=10&:\ 100<105 \\
x=11&:\ 121>115
\end{aligned}" />

At <InlineMath math="x=11" /> the three values line up as <InlineMath math="115<121<2{,}048" />, so <InlineMath math="g(11)<h(11)<f(11)" />. One example is enough for the word there is. The statement is **True**.

So the true statements are the first, the second, and the fifth.
