# Turn the percentages into numbers of students

The question asks for the percentage of Jurnalistik members in the combined group of grades X and XII. The catch is that **the <InlineMath math="10\%" /> and <InlineMath math="25\%" /> in the table come from different numbers of students**, <InlineMath math="160" /> and <InlineMath math="80" />. You can't average or add percentages with different bases directly. Turn them into numbers of students first.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{grade X}&: 10\%\cdot160=16\text{ students} \\
\text{grade XII}&: 25\%\cdot80=20\text{ students}
\end{aligned}" />

# The percentage in the combined group

The combined group has <InlineMath math="160+80=240" /> students, and <InlineMath math="16+20=36" /> of them chose Jurnalistik.

<BlockMath math="\frac{36}{240}\cdot100\%=15\%" />

Check it another way. Grade X is twice as big as grade XII, so **the average has to be weighted <InlineMath math="2:1" />**: <InlineMath math="\frac{2\cdot10\%+1\cdot25\%}{3}=15\%" />. The result sits closer to <InlineMath math="10\%" /> because grade X is bigger.

So the percentage of students in the combined group who chose Jurnalistik is <InlineMath math="15\%" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="17.5\%" /> is the plain average of <InlineMath math="10\%" /> and <InlineMath math="25\%" />. **A plain average treats both grades as the same size**, but grade X is twice as big as grade XII.
- <InlineMath math="20\%" /> pairs each percentage with the wrong number of students, <InlineMath math="10\%\cdot80+25\%\cdot160=48" />, and then divides by <InlineMath math="240" />.
- <InlineMath math="35\%" /> adds the two percentages. That's even higher than the highest percentage in either grade, so it can't be right.
- <InlineMath math="60\%" /> answers a different question: of the <InlineMath math="16+24+20=60" /> Jurnalistik members, <InlineMath math="36" /> come from grade X or XII. That's a percentage among Jurnalistik members, not among the students of the combined group.
