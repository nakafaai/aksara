# Judging each statement

**True**. Statement 1 is about area. Triangle <InlineMath math="DEF" /> has area <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\cdot4\cdot3=6" />. Triangle <InlineMath math="D'E'F'" /> has its right angle at <InlineMath math="D'" />, with <InlineMath math="D'E'=6-(-2)=8" /> and <InlineMath math="D'F'=3-(-3)=6" />:

<BlockMath math="L_{D'E'F'}=\tfrac12\cdot8\cdot6=24=4\cdot6" />

That matches the general rule: a dilation with factor <InlineMath math="k" /> multiplies areas by <InlineMath math="k^2=(-2)^2=4" />, and a reflection doesn't change area. The minus sign in the scale factor **doesn't make the area negative**.

**True**. Statement 2 is about the direction of a line. Write an image point as <InlineMath math="(X,Y)=(6-2x,\;2y-3)" />, so <InlineMath math="x=\tfrac{6-X}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="y=\tfrac{Y+3}{2}" />. Substitute these into the original line:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
3\cdot\frac{6-X}{2}-4\cdot\frac{Y+3}{2}&=1 \\
18-3X-4Y-12&=2 \\
3X+4Y&=4
\end{aligned}" />

The line <InlineMath math="3x+4y=4" /> is parallel to <InlineMath math="3x+4y=0" /> because both have gradient <InlineMath math="-\tfrac34" />. The dilation keeps the direction of a line, but the reflection in the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis flips the gradient <InlineMath math="\tfrac34" /> to <InlineMath math="-\tfrac34" />. So the image is **not parallel to the original line**.

**False**. Statement 3 is about the order of the vertices. <InlineMath math="D\to E\to F" /> really is counterclockwise: from <InlineMath math="D" /> you go right to <InlineMath math="E" />, then up and left to <InlineMath math="F" />. On the image you go from <InlineMath math="D'(6,-3)" /> left to <InlineMath math="E'(-2,-3)" />, then up and right to <InlineMath math="F'(6,3)" />. That's clockwise. A dilation with a negative factor is a <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> rotation combined with an enlargement, so **that dilation doesn't reverse the order**. Only the reflection reverses it, and one reversal leaves the image clockwise. The trap is thinking the negative factor and the reflection cancel out.

**False**. Statement 4 is about the centre of the dilation. The dilation leaves its own centre in place, but the reflection afterwards moves it:

<BlockMath math="T(2,1)=(6-4,\;2-3)=(2,-1)\neq(2,1)" />

The point that really stays put satisfies <InlineMath math="6-2x=x" /> and <InlineMath math="2y-3=y" />, which is <InlineMath math="(2,3)" />. So the centre of the dilation **isn't a fixed point of this composition**.

**True**. Statement 5 is about the line <InlineMath math="x=2" />. Every point <InlineMath math="(2,y)" /> on that line goes to

<BlockMath math="T(2,y)=(6-4,\;2y-3)=(2,\;2y-3)" />

The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays <InlineMath math="2" />, so every image stays on <InlineMath math="x=2" />. In the other direction, any point <InlineMath math="(2,t)" /> is the image of <InlineMath math="\bigl(2,\tfrac{t+3}{2}\bigr)" />. So the image of the line is **exactly the line <InlineMath math="x=2" /> again**, even though its points slide along it, except the fixed point <InlineMath math="(2,3)" />.

So the true statements are statements 1, 2, and 5.
