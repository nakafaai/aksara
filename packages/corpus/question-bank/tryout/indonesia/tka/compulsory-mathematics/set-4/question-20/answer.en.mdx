When the paper is folded along <InlineMath math="AC" />, the points <InlineMath math="B" /> and <InlineMath math="D" /> swing around the line <InlineMath math="AC" />, so their distance changes. The key is **the point <InlineMath math="O" /> where the diagonals cross**: you need the distances from <InlineMath math="B" /> and <InlineMath math="D" /> to <InlineMath math="O" />, and then the angle between them after the fold.

# Half diagonals with trigonometric ratios

The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular, cut each other in half, and cut the rhombus's angles in half. So triangle <InlineMath math="AOB" /> has a right angle at <InlineMath math="O" />, with <InlineMath math="\angle OAB=\tfrac12\cdot60^\circ=30^\circ" /> and hypotenuse <InlineMath math="AB=8" />. Side <InlineMath math="OB" /> is opposite the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> angle, so you use the **sine**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
OB&=AB\sin30^\circ=8\cdot\tfrac12=4\text{ cm} \\
OA&=AB\cos30^\circ=8\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=4\sqrt3\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

In the same way, <InlineMath math="OD=4\text{ cm}" />. Before folding, the short diagonal <InlineMath math="BD" /> is <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> long and the long diagonal <InlineMath math="AC" /> is <InlineMath math="8\sqrt3\text{ cm}" /> long.

# The angle between the planes after folding

After the fold, <InlineMath math="OB" /> still lies in plane <InlineMath math="ABC" /> and is still perpendicular to <InlineMath math="AC" />. The same goes for <InlineMath math="OD" /> in plane <InlineMath math="ADC" />. Two segments that are both perpendicular to the fold line at the same point form the angle between the two planes. The planes are perpendicular, so **<InlineMath math="\angle BOD=90^\circ" />**.

# The distance from B to D

Triangle <InlineMath math="BOD" /> now has a right angle at <InlineMath math="O" /> and legs <InlineMath math="OB=OD=4" />:

<BlockMath math="BD=\sqrt{OB^2+OD^2}=\sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2\text{ cm}" />

Sense check: <InlineMath math="4\sqrt2\approx5.66" />, shorter than the <InlineMath math="8" /> before folding. That makes sense, because **folding brings <InlineMath math="B" /> and <InlineMath math="D" /> closer together**.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="8\text{ cm}" /> is <InlineMath math="BD" /> before folding. The fold **changes the distance between points on opposite sides of the fold line**.
- <InlineMath math="4\sqrt5\text{ cm}" /> comes from picturing <InlineMath math="B" /> rising straight up by <InlineMath math="4" /> from where it started, so <InlineMath math="BD=\sqrt{8^2+4^2}" />. In fact <InlineMath math="B" /> swings around <InlineMath math="AC" /> and ends up directly above <InlineMath math="O" />.
- <InlineMath math="4\sqrt6\text{ cm}" /> swaps sine and cosine, so <InlineMath math="OB" /> is taken as <InlineMath math="8\cos30^\circ=4\sqrt3" />. That's the length of <InlineMath math="OA" />, not <InlineMath math="OB" />.
- <InlineMath math="8\sqrt2\text{ cm}" /> puts the right angle at <InlineMath math="A" />, with legs <InlineMath math="AB=AD=8" />. The <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> angle between the planes sits at <InlineMath math="O" />, not at <InlineMath math="A" />.

So after folding, the distance from <InlineMath math="B" /> to <InlineMath math="D" /> is <InlineMath math="4\sqrt2\text{ cm}" />.
