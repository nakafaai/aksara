# Finding the quadratic

This question has two parts. First, find <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> from the three points. Second, find **every real <InlineMath math="x" />** that makes one of the factors zero, then multiply them.

Substitute the three points into <InlineMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
f(-1)=19:&\quad a-b+c=19 \\
f(1)=-5:&\quad a+b+c=-5 \\
f(4)=-11:&\quad 16a+4b+c=-11
\end{aligned}" />

Subtract the first equation from the second to get <InlineMath math="2b=-24" />, so <InlineMath math="b=-12" /> and <InlineMath math="a+c=7" />. Put <InlineMath math="b=-12" /> into the third equation.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
16a-48+c&=-11 \\
16a+c&=37 \\
(16a+c)-(a+c)&=37-7 \\
15a=30,\quad a=2,\quad c&=5
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="f(x)=2x^2-12x+5=2(x-3)^2-13" />. The smallest value of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="-13" />, at <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

# Two cases from a zero product

A product of two factors is zero when one of the factors is zero. So there are **two cases**: <InlineMath math="f(x)=-7" /> or <InlineMath math="f(x)=-15" />.

The case <InlineMath math="f(x)=-15" /> has no real solution, because the smallest value of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="-13" />. Check it with the discriminant. For <InlineMath math="px^2+qx+r=0" /> the discriminant is <InlineMath math="D=q^2-4pr" />, and there are real roots only when <InlineMath math="D\ge0" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x^2-12x+20=0 &\iff x^2-6x+10=0 \\
D&=(-6)^2-4\cdot1\cdot10=-4<0
\end{aligned}" />

The case <InlineMath math="f(x)=-7" /> gives a new equation whose **constant term has changed**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x^2-12x+5&=-7 \\
2x^2-12x+12&=0 \\
x^2-6x+6&=0,\qquad D=(-6)^2-4\cdot1\cdot6=12>0
\end{aligned}" />

There are two real roots, <InlineMath math="x=3\pm\sqrt3" />. By Vieta, the product of the roots of <InlineMath math="px^2+qx+r=0" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{r}{p}" />, here <InlineMath math="\frac{6}{1}=6" />. Direct check: <InlineMath math="(3+\sqrt3)(3-\sqrt3)=9-3=6" />.

# The tempting options

Each of the other options **slips on one Vieta step or one case**.

- <InlineMath math="-6" /> comes from carrying the minus sign of the sum formula, <InlineMath math="-\frac{q}{p}" />, over to the product. The product of the roots is <InlineMath math="\frac{r}{p}" />, with no minus sign.
- <InlineMath math="\frac52" /> uses the original constant <InlineMath math="c=5" /> from <InlineMath math="f(x)=0" />. The equation is <InlineMath math="f(x)=-7" />, so the constant becomes <InlineMath math="5+7=12" />.
- <InlineMath math="10" /> is the product from the case <InlineMath math="f(x)=-15" />, <InlineMath math="\frac{20}{2}" />. That case has no real roots at all.
- <InlineMath math="60" /> multiplies the products of both cases, <InlineMath math="6\cdot10" />. The roots of the second case aren't real, so they can't be included.

So the product of all real <InlineMath math="x" /> that satisfy the equation is <InlineMath math="6" />.
