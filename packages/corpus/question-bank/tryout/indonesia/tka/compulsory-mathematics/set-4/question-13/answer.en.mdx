Both transformations use the same centre, <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" />. You can therefore work directly with coordinates, applying the **rotation first and the dilation second**.

A rotation of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> clockwise about <InlineMath math="O" /> maps <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(y,-x)" />. Picture the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis turning clockwise: it moves onto the negative <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, which matches this rule.

<BlockMath math="P(2,-3)\longrightarrow(-3,-2)" />

A dilation with centre <InlineMath math="O" /> and scale factor <InlineMath math="-2" /> maps <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-2x,-2y)" />. The negative sign places the image on the opposite side of <InlineMath math="O" />.

<BlockMath math="(-3,-2)\longrightarrow(6,4)" />

Check the **distance from <InlineMath math="O" />**. The distance <InlineMath math="OP=\sqrt{2^2+3^2}=\sqrt{13}" /> does not change under the rotation, and the dilation doubles it. The final distance must be <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{13}" />, and indeed <InlineMath math="\sqrt{6^2+4^2}=\sqrt{52}=2\sqrt{13}" />.

- <InlineMath math="(-4,6)" /> applies only the dilation and skips the rotation.
- <InlineMath math="(-6,-4)" /> results from reversing the turn direction. A counterclockwise rotation maps <InlineMath math="P" /> to <InlineMath math="(3,2)" />, and the dilation then gives <InlineMath math="(-6,-4)" />.
- <InlineMath math="(-4,-6)" /> results from reflecting the point in the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis instead of rotating it. The reflection gives <InlineMath math="(2,3)" />, and the dilation then gives <InlineMath math="(-4,-6)" />.
- <InlineMath math="(-3,-2)" /> is the result of the rotation alone, so the dilation was skipped.

So the final image of <InlineMath math="P" /> has coordinates <InlineMath math="(6,4)" />.
