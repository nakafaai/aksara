# What kind of quadrilateral

Diagonals that bisect each other make <InlineMath math="ABCD" /> a parallelogram. If they're also perpendicular, all four sides are equal, so **<InlineMath math="ABCD" /> is a rhombus** with side <InlineMath math="10" />. The diagonals aren't fixed: a rhombus with side <InlineMath math="10" /> can be flat, or it can be a square.

Let <InlineMath math="AC=p" /> and <InlineMath math="BD=q" />. The diagonals cross at right angles in the middle, so half of each diagonal and a side form a right triangle.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\left(\tfrac p2\right)^2+\left(\tfrac q2\right)^2&=10^2 \\
p^2+q^2&=400 \\
\text{area}&=\tfrac12pq
\end{aligned}" />

# Judging each statement

First statement: in a rhombus, triangles <InlineMath math="ABC" /> and <InlineMath math="ADC" /> are congruent because all three sides match, so **the diagonal splits the angle into two equal parts**. This statement is **True**.

Second statement: a rhombus with side <InlineMath math="10" /> can be a square, with area <InlineMath math="10\cdot10=100" />. The area is never more than <InlineMath math="100" />, but it can be exactly <InlineMath math="100" />, so **it isn't certainly less than <InlineMath math="100" />**. This statement is **False**.

Third statement: an area of <InlineMath math="96" /> means <InlineMath math="pq=192" />. Combine it with <InlineMath math="p^2+q^2=400" />.

<BlockMath math="(p+q)^2=p^2+q^2+2pq=400+384=784" />

Since <InlineMath math="p+q>0" />, you get <InlineMath math="p+q=28" />, for example diagonals of <InlineMath math="12" /> and <InlineMath math="16" />. This statement is **True**.

So the answers are True, False, True.
