In each wave, every person who has just learned the message tells **three new people**. So the number of new people in wave <InlineMath math="k" /> is <InlineMath math="3^k" />, and the first person is not counted as new. The number of people who know the message after <InlineMath math="k" /> waves is therefore the first person plus all the new people:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1+3+3^2+\cdots+3^k&=\frac{3^{k+1}-1}{2}
\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="k=3" /> this gives <InlineMath math="\frac{3^4-1}{2}=40" />, and for <InlineMath math="k=4" /> it gives <InlineMath math="\frac{3^5-1}{2}=121" />. Use these values to check each statement.

The statement about wave 2 with 6 new people is false. In wave 2 there are <InlineMath math="3^2=9" /> new people, because each of the three people from wave 1 tells three more. The number 6 appears if you double the count instead of tripling it.

The statement about three waves is true: <InlineMath math="1+3+9+27=40" />, including the first person.

The statement about four waves is also true: <InlineMath math="1+3+9+27+81=121" />, and <InlineMath math="121>120" />.

The statement about wave 4 with 64 new people is false. The number of new people in wave 4 is <InlineMath math="3^4=81" />. The number 64 is <InlineMath math="4^3" />, which comes from swapping the base and the exponent.

The statement about wave 3 is true. Wave 3 has <InlineMath math="27=3\cdot9" /> new people, three times the number in wave 2.

So the true statements concern 40 people after three waves, more than 120 people after four waves, and 27 new people in wave 3.
