The question asks how many students have more siblings than the mean of **all** the students. The catch: the mean <InlineMath math="3.2" /> in the reading isn't the mean of all the students. It's **the mean of the students who aren't only children**. So you need three steps: find <InlineMath math="x" />, work out the mean of all the students, then count who's above it.

# The missing frequency

The students who aren't only children are every row except the <InlineMath math="0" />-sibling row. There are <InlineMath math="6+4+11+x+9=30+x" /> of them. Their total number of siblings is **the sum of each sibling count times its number of students**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1\cdot6+2\cdot4+3\cdot11+4x+5\cdot9&=92+4x \\
\frac{92+4x}{30+x}&=3.2 \\
92+4x&=96+3.2x \\
0.8x&=4 \\
x&=5
\end{aligned}" />

# The mean of all the students

Now the only children join in. They add <InlineMath math="5" /> students but no siblings, because each of them has <InlineMath math="0" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n&=5+6+4+11+5+9=40 \\
\text{total siblings}&=92+4\cdot5=112 \\
\bar x&=\frac{112}{40}=2.8
\end{aligned}" />

The mean of all the students is <InlineMath math="2.8" />, **smaller than <InlineMath math="3.2" />**, because the only children pull the mean down.
