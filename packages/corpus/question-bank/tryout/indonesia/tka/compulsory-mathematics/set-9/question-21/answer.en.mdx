The question asks how many students have more siblings than the mean of **all** the students. The catch: the mean <InlineMath math="3.2" /> in the reading isn't the mean of all the students. It's **the mean of the students who aren't only children**. So you need three steps: find <InlineMath math="x" />, work out the mean of all the students, then count who's above it.

# The missing frequency

The students who aren't only children are every row except the <InlineMath math="0" />-sibling row. There are <InlineMath math="6+4+11+x+9=30+x" /> of them. Their total number of siblings is **the sum of each sibling count times its number of students**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1\cdot6+2\cdot4+3\cdot11+4x+5\cdot9&=92+4x \\
\frac{92+4x}{30+x}&=3.2 \\
92+4x&=96+3.2x \\
0.8x&=4 \\
x&=5
\end{aligned}" />

# The mean of all the students

Now the only children join in. They add <InlineMath math="5" /> students but no siblings, because each of them has <InlineMath math="0" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n&=5+6+4+11+5+9=40 \\
\text{total siblings}&=92+4\cdot5=112 \\
\bar x&=\frac{112}{40}=2.8
\end{aligned}" />

Draw the frequencies with the mean line. **The line at <InlineMath math="2.8" /> falls between the bars for <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />**, so every student with <InlineMath math="3" /> or more siblings is above the mean.

The mean of all the students is <InlineMath math="2.8" />, **smaller than <InlineMath math="3.2" />**, because the only children pull the mean down.

# Students above the mean

A number of siblings is always a whole number, so "more than <InlineMath math="2.8" />" means **at least <InlineMath math="3" /> siblings**:

<BlockMath math="11+5+9=25\text{ students}" />

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="14" /> students uses <InlineMath math="3.2" /> as the cut-off, so it only counts students with <InlineMath math="4" /> or <InlineMath math="5" /> siblings. That's **the mean of the wrong group**.
- <InlineMath math="20" /> students forgets to include the <InlineMath math="x=5" /> students with <InlineMath math="4" /> siblings.
- <InlineMath math="29" /> students uses the plain average <InlineMath math="\frac{0+3.2}{2}=1.6" />, as if both groups were the same size. The cut-off then drops to <InlineMath math="2" /> siblings.
- <InlineMath math="34" /> students treats <InlineMath math="3.2" /> as the mean of all the students. The equation becomes <InlineMath math="\frac{92+4x}{35+x}=3.2" />, so <InlineMath math="x=25" />, and then counting students with more than <InlineMath math="3.2" /> siblings gives <InlineMath math="25+9=34" />.

So <InlineMath math="25" /> students have more siblings than the mean of all the students.
