# The big triangle

The star is **the union of two overlapping triangles**, so its area isn't the sum of the two triangle areas. Start with triangle <InlineMath math="ACE" />.

Every side of triangle <InlineMath math="ACE" /> is a short diagonal of the hexagon, so the triangle is equilateral with side <InlineMath math="12" />. The same goes for triangle <InlineMath math="BDF" />.

<BlockMath math="L_{ACE}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot12^2=36\sqrt3" />

# Cutting into small triangles

The two triangles cut each other and split every side into three equal parts of <InlineMath math="4" />. So the star is made of **<InlineMath math="12" /> small equilateral triangles** with side <InlineMath math="4" />: six form the hexagon in the middle and six more are the points of the star. One big triangle holds <InlineMath math="9" /> small ones.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{small}}&=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot4^2=4\sqrt3 \\
L_{\text{star}}&=12\cdot4\sqrt3=48\sqrt3
\end{aligned}" />

As a check, <InlineMath math="L_{ACE}=9\cdot4\sqrt3=36\sqrt3" />, which matches the first calculation.

So the star's area is <InlineMath math="48\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2" />.

- <InlineMath math="36\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2" /> is the area of one triangle only.
- <InlineMath math="72\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2" /> adds the two triangles, so the middle hexagon is counted twice. It also happens to equal the area of hexagon <InlineMath math="ABCDEF" />.
- <InlineMath math="144\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2" /> treats <InlineMath math="12" /> as the hexagon's side, but it's the short diagonal.
- <InlineMath math="216\sqrt3\text{ cm}^2" /> is the area of a hexagon with side <InlineMath math="12" />, not the star.
