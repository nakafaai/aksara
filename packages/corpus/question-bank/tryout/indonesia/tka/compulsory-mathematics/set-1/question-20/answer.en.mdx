Two theorems carry the whole problem. An **inscribed angle on a diameter** is a right angle (Thales' theorem). And all inscribed angles on the same arc are equal, while the central angle is twice as large.

Because <InlineMath math="AC" /> is a diameter, <InlineMath math="\angle ABC=\angle ADC=90^\circ" />. The angle sum in a triangle gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\angle ACB&=180^\circ-90^\circ-28^\circ=62^\circ \\
\angle ACD&=180^\circ-90^\circ-46^\circ=44^\circ \\
\angle BCD&=62^\circ+44^\circ=106^\circ
\end{aligned}" />

The inscribed angle <InlineMath math="\angle BAC" /> and the central angle <InlineMath math="\angle BOC" /> stand on the arc <InlineMath math="BC" />. The angles <InlineMath math="\angle ACB" /> and <InlineMath math="\angle ADB" /> stand on the arc <InlineMath math="AB" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\angle BOC&=2\cdot28^\circ=56^\circ \\
\angle BDC&=\angle BAC=28^\circ \\
\angle ADB&=\angle ACB=62^\circ
\end{aligned}" />

As a check, opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, and indeed <InlineMath math="\angle BAD+\angle BCD=74^\circ+106^\circ=180^\circ" />.

- <InlineMath math="\angle BCD=106^\circ" /> is true.
- As a central angle, <InlineMath math="\angle BOC" /> is twice <InlineMath math="\angle BAC" />, so it is <InlineMath math="56^\circ" />. The statement is false.
- <InlineMath math="\angle BDC" /> stands on the same arc as <InlineMath math="\angle BAC" /> and measures <InlineMath math="28^\circ" />. The value <InlineMath math="46^\circ" /> belongs to <InlineMath math="\angle CAD" />. The statement is false.
- <InlineMath math="\angle ADB=62^\circ" /> is true.
