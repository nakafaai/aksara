The dilation multiplies both coordinates by <InlineMath math="2" />. The following rotation maps <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-y,x)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
T(x,y)&=(-2y,2x) \\
T(1,-2)&=(-2(-2),2(1))=(4,2)
\end{aligned}" />

The length scale factor is <InlineMath math="2" /> and the area factor is <InlineMath math="2^2=4" />. A positive dilation and a rotation both **preserve orientation**. Apply the coordinate rule again to find <InlineMath math="T^2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
T^2(x,y)&=T(-2y,2x) \\
&=(-2(2x),2(-2y)) \\
&=(-4x,-4y)
\end{aligned}" />

The statements about the point image, lengths, areas, and <InlineMath math="T^2" /> are true. The claim that orientation is reversed is false.

Here, orientation means the **order around the vertices**, rather than the direction the figure faces. In the triangle example, both <InlineMath math="A\to B\to C" /> and <InlineMath math="A^{\prime}\to B^{\prime}\to C^{\prime}" /> run counterclockwise. The base and height each double, so the area becomes four times as large.
