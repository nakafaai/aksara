# Picture a thousand products

**Percentages are easier to follow as numbers of items**. Suppose the factory makes <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> products.

| Machine | Products | Defective |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| A | <InlineMath math="600" /> | <InlineMath math="3\%\cdot600=18" /> |
| B | <InlineMath math="400" /> | <InlineMath math="5\%\cdot400=20" /> |
| Total | <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> | <InlineMath math="38" /> |

# Probability given a defect

You're told the product is defective. So **only the <InlineMath math="38" /> defective products count**, not all <InlineMath math="1{,}000" />. Of those <InlineMath math="38" />, <InlineMath math="20" /> come from machine B:

<BlockMath math="P(\text{B}\mid\text{defective})=\frac{20}{38}=\frac{10}{19}\approx0.53" />

Check: the probability for machine A is <InlineMath math="\frac{18}{38}=\frac9{19}" />, and <InlineMath math="\frac9{19}+\frac{10}{19}=1" />. It also makes sense that machine B is a little more likely even though it makes fewer products, because its defect rate is higher.

So the probability that the product came from machine B is <InlineMath math="\frac{10}{19}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\frac25" /> is the probability that a product comes from machine B **before** you know it's defective. The defect information isn't used.
- <InlineMath math="\frac1{20}" /> is the probability that a product from machine B is defective, which is the condition the wrong way round.
- <InlineMath math="\frac1{50}" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{20}{1{,}000}" />, the probability that a product comes from B **and** is defective, counted out of all products.
- <InlineMath math="\frac9{19}" /> is the probability that the defective product came from machine A.
