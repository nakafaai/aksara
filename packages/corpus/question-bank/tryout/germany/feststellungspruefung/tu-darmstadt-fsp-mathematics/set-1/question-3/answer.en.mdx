# Part a: Building the square and base plane

Picture three corners of a tilted table in space: the fourth corner closes the frame into a parallelogram, yet only **equal side lengths and a right angle** make it a square.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \overrightarrow{BC}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-4\\-4\end{pmatrix}\\
\overrightarrow{OD}=\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\7\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}2\\-4\\-4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-3\\3\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}" />

Both adjacent edges have length <InlineMath math="\sqrt{36}=6" />, and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC}=8-16+8=0" /> proves the right angle at <InlineMath math="B" />. So **<InlineMath math="ABCD" /> is a square with <InlineMath math="D(0\mid-3\mid3)" />**. The cross product supplies the normal of plane <InlineMath math="E" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{BC}=\begin{pmatrix}-24\\12\\-24\end{pmatrix}=-12\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\\
2(-2)-1+2\cdot7=9\implies E:\ 2x-y+2z=9
\end{aligned}" />

# Part b: Circumscribed sphere and selecting the apex

By symmetry, both the apex <InlineMath math="S_h" /> and the sphere centre <InlineMath math="K" /> sit on the line perpendicular to <InlineMath math="E" /> through the square centre <InlineMath math="M(1\mid1\mid4)" />. The unit normal into <InlineMath math="2x-y+2z>9" /> is <InlineMath math="\vec n_0=\tfrac13(2,-1,2)^T" />, and half the diagonal measures <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{MA}|=\sqrt{9+0+9}=3\sqrt2" />.

If <InlineMath math="K" /> sits at signed height <InlineMath math="z_K" /> above <InlineMath math="M" />, **both its distance to <InlineMath math="A" /> and its distance to <InlineMath math="S_h" /> must equal <InlineMath math="R=4.5" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
z_K^2+18=\left(\tfrac92\right)^2=\tfrac{81}{4}\implies z_K^2=\tfrac94\implies z_K=\pm\tfrac32\\
|h-z_K|=\tfrac92\ \text{with }h>0\implies h=z_K+\tfrac92\in\{3,\,6\}
\end{aligned}" />

Here sits the trap: both <InlineMath math="h=3" /> and <InlineMath math="h=6" /> give radius <InlineMath math="4.5" />. For <InlineMath math="h=3" />, however, the sphere centre sits at <InlineMath math="z_K=-1.5<0" /> below the base plane <InlineMath math="E" />, outside the pyramid. Only for <InlineMath math="h=6" /> do you get <InlineMath math="0<z_K=1.5<6" />, putting <InlineMath math="K" /> strictly inside on segment <InlineMath math="MS" />.

# Part c: Interior angle between adjacent side faces

Flatten a square pyramid and two adjacent roof faces approach a flat <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, stretch it tall and they approach <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. **So the interior angle between adjacent side faces of a right square pyramid is always obtuse**. First find both face normals:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AS}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-18\\-36\end{pmatrix},\qquad \overrightarrow{BC}\times\overrightarrow{BS}=\begin{pmatrix}-36\\-18\\0\end{pmatrix}" />

Simplify and orient both outwards by testing against <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AM}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BM}" />: <InlineMath math="\vec u_1=(0,1,2)^T" /> gives <InlineMath math="\vec u_1\cdot\overrightarrow{AM}=-6<0" />, and <InlineMath math="\vec u_2=(2,1,0)^T" /> gives <InlineMath math="\vec u_2\cdot\overrightarrow{BM}=-6<0" />, so both point out of the pyramid.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\cos\varphi=\frac{\vec u_1\cdot\vec u_2}{|\vec u_1|\,|\vec u_2|}=\frac{1}{5}\implies \varphi\approx78.46^\circ\\
\gamma=180^\circ-\varphi\approx101.54^\circ
\end{aligned}" />

# Part d: Skew lines and the smallest triangle

The direction vectors <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AS}=(7,-2,1)^T" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BD}=(-2,-8,-2)^T" /> aren't multiples of each other. Solving <InlineMath math="A+\lambda\overrightarrow{AS}=B+\mu\overrightarrow{BD}" /> from the <InlineMath math="x" />- and <InlineMath math="z" />-rows gives <InlineMath math="\lambda=1" /> and <InlineMath math="\mu=-\tfrac32" />, which contradicts the <InlineMath math="y" />-row (<InlineMath math="-1\neq17" />). **So the two lines are skew**.

Triangle <InlineMath math="BDT" /> has fixed base <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{BD}|=\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt2" />, so its area is smallest when <InlineMath math="T" /> comes closest to line <InlineMath math="BD" />. Midpoint <InlineMath math="M(1\mid1\mid4)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="BD" />, so find <InlineMath math="T=A+\lambda\overrightarrow{AS}" /> with <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{MT}\perp\overrightarrow{AS}" />:

<BlockMath math="\left(\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\\3\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}7\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix}\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}7\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix}=-18+54\lambda=0\implies \lambda=\tfrac13" />

That gives <InlineMath math="T_0(\tfrac13\mid\tfrac13\mid\tfrac{22}{3})" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{MT_0}=\tfrac13(-2,-2,10)^T" />. Checking <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{MT_0}\cdot\overrightarrow{BD}=\tfrac13(4+16-20)=0" /> confirms <InlineMath math="MT_0" /> is the common perpendicular. **The crucial edge check**: since <InlineMath math="0\le\tfrac13\le1" />, <InlineMath math="T_0" /> truly lies on edge <InlineMath math="AS" />.

# Part e: Light parameter keeping the shadow on the edge

Trace the light ray from <InlineMath math="S" /> down to plane <InlineMath math="E" />. **Because the light arrives from above <InlineMath math="E" />, the ray must hit <InlineMath math="E" /> in the forward direction <InlineMath math="\mu>0" />**:

<BlockMath math="2(5-\mu)-(-1+2\mu)+2(8+\mu t)=9\implies 27+\mu(2t-4)=9\implies \mu=\frac{9}{2-t}\quad(t<2)" />

At <InlineMath math="t=2" /> the ray runs parallel to <InlineMath math="E" />, and for <InlineMath math="t>2" /> light would come from underneath. Compare the shadow point <InlineMath math="S'(5-\mu\mid-1+2\mu\mid8+\mu t)" /> with a point <InlineMath math="B+\sigma\overrightarrow{BC}=(2+2\sigma\mid5-4\sigma\mid5-4\sigma)" /> on line <InlineMath math="BC" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x:&\quad 5-\mu=2+2\sigma\implies \mu=3-2\sigma\\
y:&\quad -1+2(3-2\sigma)=5-4\sigma\quad\text{always true}\\
z:&\quad 8+(3-2\sigma)t=5-4\sigma\implies \sigma=\frac{-3(1+t)}{2(2-t)}
\end{aligned}" />

So the shadow of <InlineMath math="S" /> lies on line <InlineMath math="BC" /> for every <InlineMath math="t<2" />. It stays on the edge exactly when <InlineMath math="0\le\sigma\le1" />, and because <InlineMath math="2-t>0" />:

<BlockMath math="\sigma\ge0\iff 1+t\le0\iff t\le-1,\qquad \sigma\le1\iff -3-3t\le4-2t\iff t\ge-7" />

# Part f: Radius of the inscribed sphere

By symmetry, the inscribed sphere's centre <InlineMath math="I" /> sits on axis <InlineMath math="MS" /> at height <InlineMath math="r" /> above <InlineMath math="M" />: <InlineMath math="I=M+\tfrac{r}{3}(2,-1,2)^T" /> with <InlineMath math="0<r<6" />. It automatically touches the base at distance <InlineMath math="r" />, and **its distance to side face <InlineMath math="BCS" /> must also equal <InlineMath math="r" />**.

From part c you already have the normal <InlineMath math="(2,1,0)^T" /> of <InlineMath math="BCS" />, and substituting <InlineMath math="B(2\mid5\mid5)" /> yields the clean plane equation <InlineMath math="2x+y=9" />. Inside the pyramid (testing <InlineMath math="M(1\mid1\mid4)" /> gives <InlineMath math="3<9" />), you have <InlineMath math="2x+y<9" />, which fixes the sign inside the absolute value:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
d(I,BCS)=\frac{\left|2\left(1+\tfrac{2r}{3}\right)+\left(1-\tfrac{r}{3}\right)-9\right|}{\sqrt5}=\frac{|r-6|}{\sqrt5}=r\\
0<r<6\implies 6-r=r\sqrt5\implies r=\frac{6}{\sqrt5+1}=\frac{3\left(\sqrt5-1\right)}{2}
\end{aligned}" />

Placing the centre below the base (<InlineMath math="z_I=-r" />) would give the exscribed sphere of radius <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3(\sqrt5+1)}{2}\approx4.85" /> outside the pyramid. The 3D view shows the inscribed sphere with its equal radii <InlineMath math="IM" /> and <InlineMath math="IQ" />.
