# Part a: Behaviour at both ends

Think of a tug of war: two factors pull on <InlineMath math="f_a(x)=(x^2-a)\,e^{x}" />, and the product goes wherever the stronger one pulls.

To the right, both pull the same way. As <InlineMath math="x\to+\infty" />, <InlineMath math="x^2-a" /> grows without bound and so does <InlineMath math="e^{x}" />, so **<InlineMath math="f_a(x)\to+\infty" /> for every <InlineMath math="a" />**.

To the left it gets interesting. <InlineMath math="x^2-a" /> keeps growing, but <InlineMath math="e^{x}" /> shrinks towards <InlineMath math="0" />. That's the case “infinity times zero”, and the exponential wins: **<InlineMath math="e^{x}" /> goes to <InlineMath math="0" /> faster than any polynomial grows**. So <InlineMath math="f_a(x)\to0" /> as <InlineMath math="x\to-\infty" />, and the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis is a horizontal asymptote on the left.

From which side does the graph arrive? Check the sign. As soon as <InlineMath math="x<-\sqrt{|a|}" />, you have <InlineMath math="x^2>|a|\ge a" />, so <InlineMath math="x^2-a>0" />. And <InlineMath math="e^{x}" /> is always positive. Far to the left <InlineMath math="f_a(x)" /> is positive, so **<InlineMath math="G_a" /> approaches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis from above for every <InlineMath math="a" />**.

# Part b: How many zeros

A product is zero only when one of its factors is zero. **The exponential is never zero**, because <InlineMath math="e^{x}>0" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" />. So the quadratic factor decides on its own:

<BlockMath math="f_a(x)=0\iff x^2-a=0\iff x^2=a" />

How often a square takes the value <InlineMath math="a" /> depends on the sign of <InlineMath math="a" />:

| Case | Zeros | What the graph does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="a>0" /> | <InlineMath math="x=-\sqrt a" /> and <InlineMath math="x=\sqrt a" /> | crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis twice |
| <InlineMath math="a=0" /> | only <InlineMath math="x=0" /> | touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at the origin |
| <InlineMath math="a<0" /> | none | stays completely above the axis |

Why does <InlineMath math="G_0" /> only touch the axis? For <InlineMath math="a=0" /> you get <InlineMath math="f_0(x)=x^2\,e^{x}\ge0" />, so the graph never dips below the axis. The zero <InlineMath math="x=0" /> is a double zero, and <InlineMath math="f_0'(0)=0" />. **<InlineMath math="G_0" /> touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at the origin**, where it even has a minimum. For <InlineMath math="a<0" />, <InlineMath math="x^2-a>0" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" />, so there's no zero at all. Including the touching case <InlineMath math="a=0" />, <InlineMath math="f_a" /> stays **nonnegative everywhere precisely for <InlineMath math="a\le0" />**.

# Part d: An inflection point on the axis

You're looking for a spot where two things happen at once, like a meeting point where two paths cross: the graph sits on the axis, and it changes its bending right there. For that you need the second derivative:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
f_a''(x)&=(2x+2)e^{x}+\left(x^2+2x-a\right)e^{x}\\
&=\left(x^2+4x+2-a\right)e^{x}
\end{aligned}" />

**Both conditions must hold at the same <InlineMath math="x" />**, <InlineMath math="f_a(x)=0" /> and <InlineMath math="f_a''(x)=0" />. After dividing by <InlineMath math="e^{x}" />, subtract one equation from the other:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x^2-a&=0\\
x^2+4x+2-a&=0\\
\Rightarrow\quad 4x+2&=0\\
x&=-\tfrac12,\quad a=x^2=\tfrac14
\end{aligned}" />

Check that it really is an inflection point: <InlineMath math="f_{1/4}''(x)=\left(x+\tfrac12\right)\left(x+\tfrac72\right)e^{x}" /> has a simple zero at <InlineMath math="-\tfrac12" />, **so the bending really does switch**. The inflection point is <InlineMath math="W(-\tfrac12\mid0)" />. The second inflection point at <InlineMath math="x=-\tfrac72" /> sits off the axis, because <InlineMath math="f_{1/4}(-\tfrac72)=12\,e^{-7/2}>0" />.

The slope there is <InlineMath math="f_{1/4}'(-\tfrac12)=\left(\tfrac14-1-\tfrac14\right)e^{-1/2}=-e^{-1/2}" />. The tangent (_Wendetangente_, the inflection tangent) passes through <InlineMath math="W" />, so **<InlineMath math="t(x)=-e^{-1/2}\left(x+\tfrac12\right)" />**.

# Part e: The curve through all extrema

Imagine every member of the family (_Funktionenschar_) putting a small lamp at each of its maxima and minima. The locus (_Ortskurve_) is the path all those lamps stand on. To find it, **you throw the parameter <InlineMath math="a" /> out**. At an extremum <InlineMath math="x^2+2x-a=0" />, so <InlineMath math="a=x^2+2x" />. Put that into the function:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y&=\left(x^2-a\right)e^{x}\\
&=\left(x^2-x^2-2x\right)e^{x}\\
&=-2x\,e^{x}
\end{aligned}" />

Does it work the other way round? Take any <InlineMath math="x" /> and set <InlineMath math="a=x^2+2x=(x+1)^2-1" />. For <InlineMath math="x\neq-1" /> you get <InlineMath math="a>-1" />, and then <InlineMath math="G_a" /> really has an extremum at <InlineMath math="x" />, because <InlineMath math="x=-1\pm\sqrt{1+a}" /> with <InlineMath math="\sqrt{1+a}=|x+1|" />.

So the locus is <InlineMath math="y=-2x\,e^{x}" /> without the point <InlineMath math="(-1\mid\tfrac{2}{e})" />. Because <InlineMath math="x_1=-1-\sqrt{1+a}<-1" />, **the maxima lie on the part with <InlineMath math="x<-1" />** and the minima on the part with <InlineMath math="x>-1" />.

# Part f: The largest tent triangle

Think of a tent: the top is <InlineMath math="H_a" />, a pole goes straight down to <InlineMath math="L_a" />, and the canvas stretches back to the origin. The top moves with <InlineMath math="a" />, and you want the tent with the biggest area.

This is where part e pays off. Call the position of the maximum <InlineMath math="u=-1-\sqrt{1+a}" />. Then <InlineMath math="u<-1" />, and **you read the height straight off the locus**: <InlineMath math="H_a(u\mid-2u\,e^{u})" />. The triangle has its right angle at <InlineMath math="L_a(u\mid0)" />, and its base is <InlineMath math="|u|=-u" />:

# Part g: Area between the zeros

Integrating means differentiating backwards: you look for a function whose derivative is <InlineMath math="f_a" />. Differentiating <InlineMath math="(\text{polynomial})\cdot e^{x}" /> gives <InlineMath math="(\text{polynomial})\cdot e^{x}" /> again, so try <InlineMath math="F_a(x)=\left(x^2+bx+c\right)e^{x}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
F_a'(x)&=\left(x^2+(b+2)x+b+c\right)e^{x}\overset{!}{=}\left(x^2-a\right)e^{x}\\
b&=-2,\quad c=2-a
\end{aligned}" />

So **<InlineMath math="F_a(x)=\left(x^2-2x+2-a\right)e^{x}" /> is an antiderivative**. Integrating by parts twice gives the same.

For <InlineMath math="a>0" /> the zeros are at <InlineMath math="\pm\sqrt a" />. In between, <InlineMath math="x^2<a" />, so <InlineMath math="f_a<0" />. **The region lies below the axis**, the integral is negative, and the area is its opposite. With <InlineMath math="s=\sqrt a" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\int_{-s}^{s}f_a(x)\,dx&=F_a(s)-F_a(-s)\\
&=(2-2s)\,e^{s}-(2+2s)\,e^{-s}
\end{aligned}" />

<BlockMath math="A(a)=2\left(\sqrt a-1\right)e^{\sqrt a}+2\left(\sqrt a+1\right)e^{-\sqrt a}" />

Quick test with <InlineMath math="a=1" />: <InlineMath math="A(1)=0+4e^{-1}=\tfrac{4}{e}\approx1.47" />, positive, just as an area must be.

# Part h: The endless region on the left

A region that never ends on the left can still have a finite area, just as <InlineMath math="1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\dots" /> never ends and still adds up to only <InlineMath math="2" />. **You compute such an area as a limit**: integrate up to a movable left bound <InlineMath math="r" />, then slide <InlineMath math="r" /> to the left.

Left of <InlineMath math="-\sqrt a" />, <InlineMath math="f_a>0" />, so the region lies above the axis. With the antiderivative from part g:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
B(a)&=\lim_{r\to-\infty}\int_{r}^{-\sqrt a}f_a(x)\,dx\\
&=\lim_{r\to-\infty}\left(F_a(-\sqrt a)-F_a(r)\right)
\end{aligned}" />

As <InlineMath math="r\to-\infty" />, <InlineMath math="F_a(r)=\left(r^2-2r+2-a\right)e^{r}\to0" />, because the exponential crushes the polynomial here too. What's left is

<BlockMath math="B(a)=F_a(-\sqrt a)=\left(a+2\sqrt a+2-a\right)e^{-\sqrt a}=2\left(\sqrt a+1\right)e^{-\sqrt a}" />

**The area is finite** for every <InlineMath math="a>0" />. Now compare it with <InlineMath math="A(a)" />. The term <InlineMath math="2(\sqrt a+1)e^{-\sqrt a}" /> sits in both expressions and drops out of their difference:

<BlockMath math="B(a)-A(a)=2\left(1-\sqrt a\right)e^{\sqrt a}" />

Since <InlineMath math="e^{\sqrt a}>0" />, you get <InlineMath math="B(a)=A(a)" /> **only for <InlineMath math="a=1" />** (both equal <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4}{e}" />). Moreover, <InlineMath math="B(a)-A(a)>0" /> holds iff <InlineMath math="1-\sqrt a>0" />, so together with <InlineMath math="a>0" /> **the unbounded region is larger precisely for <InlineMath math="0<a<1" />**.
