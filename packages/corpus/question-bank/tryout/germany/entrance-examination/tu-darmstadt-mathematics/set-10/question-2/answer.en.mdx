# Given

A right triangle (*rechtwinkliges Dreieck*) has two known measurements:

- **Perimeter** (*Umfang*): <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" />.
- **Area** (*Flächeninhalt*): <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}^2" />.

# Asked

How long is its hypotenuse (*Hypotenuse*) <InlineMath math="c" />?

# Solution

There are three sides (<InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, and <InlineMath math="c" />), yet you **do not need to find the legs <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> first**. Expand <InlineMath math="(a + b)^2" />: inside it sit <InlineMath math="a^2 + b^2" /> (which turns straight into <InlineMath math="c^2" />) and <InlineMath math="2ab" /> (which comes directly from the area!). Even better, the <InlineMath math="c^2" /> terms cancel on both sides.

> Squaring the sum of the two legs combines the Pythagorean theorem and the triangle area at once, isolating the hypotenuse in a single linear equation.

## Eliminating both legs via the squared sum

Perimeter, area, and the Pythagorean theorem supply **three equations**:

<BlockMath math="a + b + c = 30, \qquad \frac{1}{2}ab = 30 \iff ab = 60, \qquad a^2 + b^2 = c^2" />

Express the sum <InlineMath math="a + b" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="c" /> and square both sides:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= a^2 + b^2 + 2ab \\
(30 - c)^2 &= c^2 + 2(60) \\
900 - 60c + c^2 &= c^2 + 120 \\
780 &= 60c \quad \Longrightarrow \quad c = 13\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

The <InlineMath math="c^2" /> terms cancel out cleanly, giving <InlineMath math="c = 13\text{ cm}" /> right away.

## Checking all three sides in the figure

With <InlineMath math="c = 13" />, we get <InlineMath math="a + b = 17" /> and <InlineMath math="ab = 60" />, matching the classic **Pythagorean triple** (<InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="12" />, <InlineMath math="13" />).
