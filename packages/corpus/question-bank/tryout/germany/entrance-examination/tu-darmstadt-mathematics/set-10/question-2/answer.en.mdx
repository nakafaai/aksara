# Three equations for three sides

Call the legs <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> and the hypotenuse <InlineMath math="c" />. Perimeter, area and Pythagoras give **three equations**:

<BlockMath math="a+b+c=30,\qquad \tfrac12ab=30,\qquad a^2+b^2=c^2" />

# Use <InlineMath math="(a+b)^2" /> to get rid of the legs

You don't need <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> separately. Square <InlineMath math="a+b=30-c" /> and substitute **<InlineMath math="ab=60" /> and <InlineMath math="a^2+b^2=c^2" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(a+b)^2&=a^2+b^2+2ab \\
(30-c)^2&=c^2+120 \\
900-60c+c^2&=c^2+120 \\
780&=60c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=13
\end{aligned}" />

# Check with the legs

With <InlineMath math="c=13" />, <InlineMath math="a+b=17" /> and <InlineMath math="ab=60" />, so <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> solve <InlineMath math="t^2-17t+60=0" />: <InlineMath math="12" /> and <InlineMath math="5" />. Indeed <InlineMath math="12^2+5^2=169=13^2" />. The hypotenuse is **<InlineMath math="13\text{ cm}" /> long**.

# Why the other answers are wrong

- <InlineMath math="14\text{ cm}" /> forgets the factor <InlineMath math="\frac12" /> in the area, so <InlineMath math="ab=30" />: then <InlineMath math="900-60c=60" /> and <InlineMath math="c=14" />.
- <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> is **the longer leg**, not the hypotenuse.
- <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> is half the perimeter.
- <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" /> divides the perimeter by three, as if the triangle were equilateral.
- <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" /> is the shorter leg.
