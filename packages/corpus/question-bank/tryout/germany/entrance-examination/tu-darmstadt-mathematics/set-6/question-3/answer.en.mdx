# Two altitudes are enough

All three altitudes meet in one point. So **two altitudes** are enough for its coordinates, and the third serves as a check.

The side <InlineMath math="\overline{AB}" /> lies on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. The altitude from <InlineMath math="C" /> is perpendicular to it, so it's the vertical line <InlineMath math="x=2" />.

# The altitude from <InlineMath math="A" />

The side <InlineMath math="\overline{BC}" /> has slope <InlineMath math="m_{BC}=\frac{6-0}{2-8}=-1" />. A perpendicular line has slope <InlineMath math="1" />, because **the slopes of perpendicular lines multiply to <InlineMath math="-1" />**. So the altitude from <InlineMath math="A(0\mid0)" /> is <InlineMath math="y=x" />.

# Why the other answers are wrong

- <InlineMath math="H(4\mid2)" /> is where the **perpendicular bisectors** meet, the circumcentre.
- <InlineMath math="H\left(\frac{10}{3}\mid2\right)" /> is the centroid, where the medians meet.
- <InlineMath math="H(2\mid6)" /> is the corner <InlineMath math="C" />. The altitude from <InlineMath math="C" /> passes through <InlineMath math="C" />, but the other altitudes don't.
- <InlineMath math="H(2\mid4)" /> comes from giving the altitude from <InlineMath math="A" /> slope <InlineMath math="2" />, that is not taking the negative reciprocal of <InlineMath math="-1" />.
- <InlineMath math="H(6\mid2)" /> swaps the coordinates of the corner <InlineMath math="C" /> to <InlineMath math="(6\mid2)" /> and then uses the altitude <InlineMath math="x=6" />.
