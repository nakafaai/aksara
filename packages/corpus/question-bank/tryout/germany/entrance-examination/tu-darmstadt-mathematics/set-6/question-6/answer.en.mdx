# Given

A **company** (*Betrieb*) increases its output (*Produktion*) by the same percentage two years in a row until output stands <InlineMath math="44\,\%" /> above the starting level. In the third year, output drops by that exact same percentage.

# Asked

By what percentage does output at the end of the third year exceed the initial output?

# Solution

Picture starting with <InlineMath math="100" /> crates. Two identical <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> jumps lift the stack from <InlineMath math="100" /> to <InlineMath math="120" /> and then to <InlineMath math="144" /> crates. When year three trims <InlineMath math="20\,\%" />, **that cut slices one fifth off the larger <InlineMath math="144" />-crate pile**, not off <InlineMath math="100" />, so the drop hurts more than a single hike!

> Consecutive percentage changes always multiply onto the latest value, so extract the annual growth rate via a square root first and then multiply by the decrease factor.

Find the annual growth rate <InlineMath math="p > 0" /> from two consecutive hikes reaching <InlineMath math="1.44" />.

<BlockMath math="(1 + p)^2 = 1.44 \implies 1 + p = 1.2 \implies p = 0.2 = 20\,\%" />

## Computing the third year output level

Apply the <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> decline to **the updated level <InlineMath math="1.44" />** by multiplying by <InlineMath math="0.8" />.

<BlockMath math="1.44 \cdot (1 - 0.2) = 1.44 \cdot 0.8 = 1.152" />

Notice that year three finishes below year one (<InlineMath math="120" />), because taking <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> off <InlineMath math="144" /> cuts more than adding <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> onto <InlineMath math="120" />.

## Checking the result and wrong choices

Quick factor check: multiplying <InlineMath math="1.2 \cdot 1.2 \cdot 0.8" /> gives <InlineMath math="1.152" />, leaving output **still <InlineMath math="15.2\,\%" /> above the starting level**.

- <InlineMath math="24\,\%" /> subtracts <InlineMath math="20" /> percentage points straight from <InlineMath math="44\,\%" /> instead of scaling the new level.
- <InlineMath math="22\,\%" /> halves <InlineMath math="44\,\%" /> for the annual rate and then subtracts linearly.
- <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> assumes one <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> rise and one <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> drop cancel each other out.
- <InlineMath math="12.32\,\%" /> plugs the wrong annual rate <InlineMath math="22\,\%" /> into the third-year decrease.
- <InlineMath math="0\,\%" /> assumes the single decrease wipes out both earlier increases.

# Answer

At the end of the third year, output is **<InlineMath math="15.2\,\%" />** above the initial level.
