# Given

A cyclist rides out and back along **the exact same route**:

- Constant outbound speed (*Hinweg*): <InlineMath math="20\text{ km/h}" />.
- Overall average speed (*Durchschnittsgeschwindigkeit*) for the round trip: <InlineMath math="24\text{ km/h}" />.

# Asked

At what constant speed does she ride on the return trip (*Rückweg*)?

# Solution

At first glance, <InlineMath math="28\text{ km/h}" /> looks tempting because the midpoint of <InlineMath math="20" /> and <InlineMath math="28" /> is <InlineMath math="24" />. Think about the clock, though: because she goes slower on the way out, **she spends more time at the slow speed than at the fast speed**. Her return leg must be faster than <InlineMath math="28" /> to make up the extra minutes.

> Average speed is always total distance divided by total time, never the simple arithmetic mean of two speeds over equal distances.

## Writing total distance over total time

Let <InlineMath math="s" /> be the one-way distance and <InlineMath math="v" /> the return speed. The round trip covers <InlineMath math="2s" />, and the total time is the sum of outbound and return times. Notice that **the distance <InlineMath math="s" /> cancels right out**:

<BlockMath math="\frac{2s}{\frac{s}{20} + \frac{s}{v}} = 24 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{2}{\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{v}} = 24" />

## Solving the reciprocal equation

Rearrange to isolate **the reciprocal of the return speed**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{v} &= \frac{2}{24} = \frac{1}{12} \\
\frac{1}{v} &= \frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{5 - 3}{60} = \frac{1}{30}
\end{aligned}" />

So the return speed is exactly <InlineMath math="v = 30\text{ km/h}" />. The harmonic-mean curve below shows how returns diminish as return speed increases.

Concrete check with <InlineMath math="s = 60\text{ km}" />: outbound takes <InlineMath math="3" /> hours and return takes <InlineMath math="2" /> hours. Covering <InlineMath math="120\text{ km}" /> in <InlineMath math="5" /> hours yields exactly <InlineMath math="24\text{ km/h}" />.

## Checking the distractors

See the intuitive trap behind **each wrong choice**:

| Option | Why it is wrong |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="28\text{ km/h}" /> | Uses the ordinary mean <InlineMath math="\frac{20 + v}{2} = 24" />, ignoring that the slow outbound leg takes longer. |
| <InlineMath math="28.8\text{ km/h}" /> | Uses the geometric mean <InlineMath math="\frac{24^2}{20}" /> instead of the harmonic mean. |
| <InlineMath math="40\text{ km/h}" /> | Doubles the outbound speed (that is the upper ceiling of the average, not the return speed). |
| <InlineMath math="12\text{ km/h}" /> | Misreads the intermediate reciprocal <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{12}" /> directly as the answer. |
| Not possible at any speed | Comes from forgetting the factor <InlineMath math="2" /> for the round trip, leading to <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{24}" />. |

# Answer

She rides back at a constant speed of **<InlineMath math="30\text{ km/h}" />**.
