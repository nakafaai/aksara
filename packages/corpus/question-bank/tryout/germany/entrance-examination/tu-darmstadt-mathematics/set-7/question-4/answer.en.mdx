# Given

Two points <InlineMath math="A(1 \mid 5)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(5 \mid 1)" /> are given along with a line <InlineMath math="g" /> described by:

<BlockMath math="y = 2x - 1" />

Point <InlineMath math="P" /> lies on line <InlineMath math="g" /> and is **equidistant** from <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />.

# Asked

How far is point <InlineMath math="P" /> from point <InlineMath math="A" />?

# Solution

Imagine <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are two friends' houses and you want a fair meeting spot equally far from both. Every fair spot sits on one straight dividing road right down the middle: the **perpendicular bisector** (*Mittelsenkrechte*) of segment <InlineMath math="\overline{AB}" />. Point <InlineMath math="P" /> is simply where that dividing road crosses line <InlineMath math="g" />.

> Every point equidistant from two fixed points lies on their perpendicular bisector, so the target point is the intersection of that bisector with the given line.

## Finding the perpendicular bisector

Notice that <InlineMath math="A(1 \mid 5)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(5 \mid 1)" /> simply swap <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />, meaning they mirror each other across <InlineMath math="y = x" />. You can confirm this directly using the **midpoint and perpendicular slope**:

<BlockMath math="M = \left(\frac{1 + 5}{2} \;\middle|\; \frac{5 + 1}{2}\right) = (3 \mid 3), \qquad m_{AB} = \frac{1 - 5}{5 - 1} = -1" />

A line perpendicular to slope <InlineMath math="-1" /> has slope <InlineMath math="1" /> and passes through <InlineMath math="M(3 \mid 3)" />:

<BlockMath math="y - 3 = 1 \cdot (x - 3) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad y = x" />

## Intersecting the bisector with the given line

Since <InlineMath math="P" /> lies on both <InlineMath math="y = x" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 2x - 1" />, **set the two line equations equal**:

<BlockMath math="2x - 1 = x \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 1, \quad y = 1" />

So the meeting point is exactly <InlineMath math="P(1 \mid 1)" />.

Even better, segment <InlineMath math="\overline{PA}" /> runs straight up and segment <InlineMath math="\overline{PB}" /> runs straight across, so their lengths read off cleanly.

## Computing and verifying the distance

Compute both distances from <InlineMath math="P(1 \mid 1)" /> to **verify the equal-distance condition**:

<BlockMath math="\overline{PA} = \sqrt{(1 - 1)^2 + (5 - 1)^2} = 4, \qquad \overline{PB} = \sqrt{(5 - 1)^2 + (1 - 1)^2} = 4" />

Both distances match at <InlineMath math="4" /> units.

## Checking the distractors

Notice other lengths in the diagram that are easy to **confuse with <InlineMath math="\overline{PA}" />**:

| Option | Why it is wrong |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}" /> | Uses line <InlineMath math="AB" /> itself (slope <InlineMath math="-1" />) instead of the bisector, giving an unequal intersection. |
| <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2}" /> | Distance from <InlineMath math="A" /> to midpoint <InlineMath math="M(3 \mid 3)" />, which does not lie on line <InlineMath math="g" />. |
| <InlineMath math="4\sqrt{2}" /> | Length of the entire segment <InlineMath math="\overline{AB}" />, not <InlineMath math="\overline{PA}" />. |
| <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" /> | Distance from <InlineMath math="P(1 \mid 1)" /> to the origin <InlineMath math="(0 \mid 0)" />. |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | Plugs the midpoint's <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> into line <InlineMath math="g" />, giving <InlineMath math="(3 \mid 5)" /> which is far from <InlineMath math="B" />. |

# Answer

Point <InlineMath math="P" /> is **<InlineMath math="4" />** units away from point <InlineMath math="A" />.
