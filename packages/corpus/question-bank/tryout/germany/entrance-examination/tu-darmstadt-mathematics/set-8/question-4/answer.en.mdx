# Given

A triangle has vertices at:

<BlockMath math="A(0 \mid 0), \qquad B(6 \mid 2), \qquad C(k \mid 6)" />

Its **area (*Flächeninhalt*) equals <InlineMath math="15" />**, and <InlineMath math="k" /> is a real number.

# Asked

Which values can <InlineMath math="k" /> take?

# Solution

Picture segment <InlineMath math="\overline{AB}" /> fixed on the plane while vertex <InlineMath math="C(k \mid 6)" /> slides freely along the horizontal rail <InlineMath math="y = 6" />. That rail crosses the extended line <InlineMath math="AB" /> at one spot where the triangle flattens to area zero. Sliding <InlineMath math="C" /> away to **either the left or the right** grows the area symmetrically, so there must be **two distinct positions for <InlineMath math="C" />** with area <InlineMath math="15" />.

> The coordinate area formula always carries an absolute value, so a given area produces two valid vertices on opposite sides of the base line.

## Setting up the area formula with absolute value

With one vertex at the origin <InlineMath math="(0 \mid 0)" />, the area formed with <InlineMath math="(x_1 \mid y_1)" /> and <InlineMath math="(x_2 \mid y_2)" /> is half the absolute determinant. **Keeping the absolute value bars is essential** to capture both sides of line <InlineMath math="AB" />:

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2}\lvert 6 \cdot 6 - k \cdot 2 \rvert = \frac{1}{2}\lvert 36 - 2k \rvert" />

## Solving the absolute value equation

Set the area equal to <InlineMath math="15" /> and **split into both sign branches**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{2}\lvert 36 - 2k \rvert = 15 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \lvert 36 - 2k \rvert = 30" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
36 - 2k = 30 &\quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2k = 6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad k = 3 \\
36 - 2k = -30 &\quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2k = 66 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad k = 33
\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

See both area-<InlineMath math="15" /> triangles along the horizontal rail <InlineMath math="y = 6" /> below.

Both checks confirm area <InlineMath math="15" />. Notice also the midpoint <InlineMath math="k = 18" />, exactly where <InlineMath math="C" /> becomes collinear with <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />.

## Checking the distractors

See how the wrong choices **drop one branch or forget the factor of one half**:

| Option | Why it is wrong |
| --- | --- |
| only <InlineMath math="k = 3" /> | Drops the absolute value and misses the second triangle on the right. |
| only <InlineMath math="k = 33" /> | Takes only the negative branch and misses the first triangle on the left. |
| <InlineMath math="k = 10.5" /> or <InlineMath math="k = 25.5" /> | Forgets the factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> and solves <InlineMath math="\lvert 36 - 2k \rvert = 15" />, giving area <InlineMath math="7.5" />. |
| <InlineMath math="k = -3" /> or <InlineMath math="k = 3" /> | Puts the absolute value on <InlineMath math="k" /> alone instead of the entire determinant. |
| only <InlineMath math="k = 18" /> | Makes <InlineMath math="36 - 2k = 0" />, putting all three points on one line with area zero. |

# Answer

The valid values are **<InlineMath math="k = 3" /> or <InlineMath math="k = 33" />**.
