# Work with a sample number

All the data are percentages, and so is the change you want. That means you can start with a convenient number, for example <InlineMath math="100" /> members. The key point: the two shares refer to **different bases**, <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> to the old and <InlineMath math="25\,\%" /> to the new number of members.

# The numbers before and after

# Measure the change against the old value

The number of adults goes from <InlineMath math="80" /> to <InlineMath math="90" />. A percentage change is measured **against the starting value**:

<BlockMath math="\frac{90-80}{80}=\frac{10}{80}=0.125=12.5\,\%" />

Even though the adults' share falls from <InlineMath math="80\,\%" /> to <InlineMath math="75\,\%" />, their number rose by <InlineMath math="12.5\,\%" />.

# Why the other answers are wrong

- "fell by <InlineMath math="5\,\%" />" reads the change of the share from <InlineMath math="80\,\%" /> to <InlineMath math="75\,\%" /> as a change of the number. Those are **<InlineMath math="5" /> percentage points of a different base**.
- "fell by <InlineMath math="6.25\,\%" />" compares the shares, <InlineMath math="\frac{75}{80}=0.9375" />, so again shares instead of numbers.
- "rose by <InlineMath math="20\,\%" />" carries the growth of the whole club over to the adults.
- "fell by <InlineMath math="12.5\,\%" />" has the right number but the wrong direction: <InlineMath math="90" /> is more than <InlineMath math="80" />.
- "rose by <InlineMath math="50\,\%" />" is the change for the young members, from <InlineMath math="20" /> to <InlineMath math="30" />.
