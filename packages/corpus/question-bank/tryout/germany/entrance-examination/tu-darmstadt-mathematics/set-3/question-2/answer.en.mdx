For an equation with fractions, **find the domain first**, because a number that makes a denominator zero cannot be a solution. Since <InlineMath math="x^2-2x=x(x-2)" />, the numbers <InlineMath math="x=0" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" /> are excluded.

Then multiply both sides by the common denominator <InlineMath math="x(x-2)" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x(x+4)&=12 \\
x^2+4x-12&=0 \\
(x+6)(x-2)&=0 \\
x=-6\ \text{or}\ x&=2
\end{aligned}" />

The number <InlineMath math="2" /> is excluded, because it makes both denominators zero. That leaves <InlineMath math="x=-6" />.

As a check, the left side is <InlineMath math="\frac{-2}{-8}=\frac14" /> and the right side is <InlineMath math="\frac{12}{36+12}=\frac{12}{48}=\frac14" />.

So the solution set is <InlineMath math="L=\{-6\}" />.

- <InlineMath math="L=\{2\}" /> keeps exactly the excluded number and throws away the valid solution.
- <InlineMath math="L=\{6\}" /> appears if the signs in the factorisation are swapped, <InlineMath math="(x-6)(x+2)" />, and only the positive number is kept.
- <InlineMath math="L=\{-2;\,6\}" /> belongs to <InlineMath math="(x-6)(x+2)=x^2-4x-12" />, that is, to a swapped sign in the linear term.
- <InlineMath math="L=\{-6;\,2\}" /> forgets the domain. At <InlineMath math="x=2" /> neither side is defined.
- <InlineMath math="L=\emptyset" /> also discards <InlineMath math="-6" />, although that number makes no denominator zero.
