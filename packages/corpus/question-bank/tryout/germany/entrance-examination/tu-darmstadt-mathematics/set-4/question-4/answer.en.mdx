# Find the pieces of the hypotenuse

The altitude splits the hypotenuse into two pieces <InlineMath math="p" /> and <InlineMath math="q" />. Two facts hold for them: they add up to the hypotenuse, and by the **altitude theorem** their product is the square of the altitude:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
p+q&=13,\qquad p\cdot q=h^2=36 \\
p\,(13-p)&=36\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p^2-13p+36=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (p-4)(p-9)=0
\end{aligned}" />

So the pieces are <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm}" />.

# Find the legs

By the **leg theorem**, the square of each leg is the hypotenuse times the piece next to that leg:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a^2&=13\cdot4=52,\qquad a=2\sqrt{13} \\
b^2&=13\cdot9=117,\qquad b=3\sqrt{13}
\end{aligned}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="a^2+b^2=52+117=169=13^2" />.

# Add up the perimeter

<BlockMath math="u=13+2\sqrt{13}+3\sqrt{13}=(13+5\sqrt{13})\text{ cm}" />

**The perimeter is** <InlineMath math="(13+5\sqrt{13})\text{ cm}" />, about <InlineMath math="31.0\text{ cm}" />.

# Why the other answers are wrong

- <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> takes the familiar sides <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="12" />, <InlineMath math="13" />. That triangle has **a different altitude**: <InlineMath math="\frac{5\cdot12}{13}=\frac{60}{13}\ne6" />.
- <InlineMath math="26\text{ cm}" /> adds the pieces <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="9" /> to the hypotenuse as if they were the legs.
- <InlineMath math="(13+\sqrt{481})\text{ cm}" /> uses <InlineMath math="ab=2\cdot13\cdot6=156" />. In fact <InlineMath math="ab=c\cdot h=78" />, because both products are twice the area.
- <InlineMath math="5\sqrt{13}\text{ cm}" /> is only the sum of the legs, without the hypotenuse.
- <InlineMath math="(13+2\sqrt{13})\text{ cm}" /> counts only one leg.
