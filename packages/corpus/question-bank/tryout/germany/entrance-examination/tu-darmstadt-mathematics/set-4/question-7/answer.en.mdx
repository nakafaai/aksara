# Given

The **price of an item** (*Preis einer Ware*) is first raised by a percentage <InlineMath math="p" />, and then the new price is cut by twice that percentage (<InlineMath math="2p" />). In the end, the item costs <InlineMath math="12\,\%" /> less than before the increase.

# Asked

By what percentage was the price raised in the first step?

# Solution

A discount taken after a price hike cuts deeper than a discount on the original price because **the second cut also trims off part of the added markup**! That is why consecutive percentage changes always multiply their growth factors rather than simply subtracting.

> Consecutive percentage changes multiply their factors step by step against the latest price, and only a positive root represents an initial price increase.

Write the percentage increase as a decimal <InlineMath math="p > 0" />. The hike multiplies the price by <InlineMath math="1 + p" />, the cut multiplies it by <InlineMath math="1 - 2p" />, and the final factor is <InlineMath math="0.88" />.

<BlockMath math="(1 + p)(1 - 2p) = 0.88" />

## Solving the quadratic and picking the positive root

Expand the product and apply the quadratic formula to obtain **both candidate values for <InlineMath math="p" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1 - p - 2p^2 &= 0.88 \\
2p^2 + p - 0.12 &= 0 \\
p &= \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 0.96}}{4} = \frac{-1 \pm 1.4}{4}
\end{aligned}" />

This yields <InlineMath math="p = 0.1" /> or <InlineMath math="p = -0.6" />. Because the first step is an increase, we keep <InlineMath math="p = 0.1" /> (<InlineMath math="10\,\%" />).

## Testing with a sample price and checking wrong choices

Check with a starting price of <InlineMath math="100" />: up <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> gives <InlineMath math="110" />, then down <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> lands on **exactly <InlineMath math="88" />**, which is <InlineMath math="12\,\%" /> cheaper than the start.

- <InlineMath math="12\,\%" /> subtracts percentages linearly (<InlineMath math="p - 2p" />), ignoring that the discount acts on the raised price.
- <InlineMath math="20\,\%" /> is the percentage decrease in the second step, not the initial increase.
- <InlineMath math="60\,\%" /> takes the magnitude of the rejected negative root <InlineMath math="-0.6" />.
- <InlineMath math="6\,\%" /> merely cuts <InlineMath math="12\,\%" /> in half.
- <InlineMath math="4\,\%" /> divides <InlineMath math="12\,\%" /> by three as if the net change were <InlineMath math="3p" />.

# Answer

The price was first raised by **<InlineMath math="10\,\%" />**.
