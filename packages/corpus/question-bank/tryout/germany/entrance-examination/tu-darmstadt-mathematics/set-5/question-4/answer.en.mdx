# Given

A **merchant** (*Händler*) spends a total of <InlineMath math="600\text{ €}" /> on shirts (*Hemden*) that all cost the same price. Had each shirt been <InlineMath math="5\text{ €}" /> cheaper, the same <InlineMath math="600\text{ €}" /> would have bought exactly <InlineMath math="4" /> more shirts.

# Asked

Find the actual price <InlineMath math="p" /> of one shirt.

# Solution

On a fixed <InlineMath math="600\text{ €}" /> budget, a <InlineMath math="5\text{ €}" /> discount stretches your money further: **the shirt count at the cheaper price minus the shirt count at the actual price must equal <InlineMath math="4" />**.

> With a fixed budget, lowering the unit price increases quantity, so always subtract the higher-price quantity from the lower-price quantity to keep the difference positive.

Let the actual shirt price be <InlineMath math="p" /> euros (<InlineMath math="p > 5" />), which gives the quantity-difference equation below.

<BlockMath math="\frac{600}{p - 5} - \frac{600}{p} = 4" />

## Solving the quadratic and keeping the positive price

Multiply both sides by <InlineMath math="p(p - 5)" /> and **divide through by <InlineMath math="4" />** to keep the factoring clean.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
600p - 600(p - 5) &= 4p(p - 5) \\
3000 &= 4p^2 - 20p \\
p^2 - 5p - 750 &= 0 \\
(p - 30)(p + 25) &= 0
\end{aligned}" />

Since a price must be positive, <InlineMath math="p = -25" /> drops out and <InlineMath math="p = 30" /> remains. Watch on the graph how the extra-shirt curve crosses <InlineMath math="y = 4" /> once.

## Checking the purchase and wrong choices

Quick purchase check: at <InlineMath math="30\text{ €}" /> each you get <InlineMath math="20" /> shirts, and at <InlineMath math="25\text{ €}" /> each you get <InlineMath math="24" /> shirts, **exactly <InlineMath math="4" /> more shirts**.

- <InlineMath math="25\text{ €}" /> is the hypothetical discounted price rather than the actual price paid.
- <InlineMath math="20\text{ €}" /> confuses the number of shirts bought (<InlineMath math="20" />) with the price.
- No price matches arises when you reverse the subtraction order, making the left side negative for every <InlineMath math="p > 5" />.
- <InlineMath math="24\text{ €}" /> confuses the cheaper-price shirt count (<InlineMath math="24" />) with the price.
- <InlineMath math="150\text{ €}" /> divides the <InlineMath math="600\text{ €}" /> budget directly by the <InlineMath math="4" /> extra shirts.

# Answer

One shirt cost **<InlineMath math="30\text{ €}" />**.
