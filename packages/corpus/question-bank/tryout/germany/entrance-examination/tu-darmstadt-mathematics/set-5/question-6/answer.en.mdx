# Given

A **balance** (*Guthaben*) of <InlineMath math="10\,000\text{ €}" /> is invested for two years with compound interest (*Zinseszins*). The second-year interest rate is <InlineMath math="2" /> percentage points higher than the first-year rate (<InlineMath math="P\,\%" /> and <InlineMath math="(P + 2)\,\%" />), and the final balance is exactly <InlineMath math="11\,235\text{ €}" />.

# Asked

Find the interest rate in the first year (*Zinssatz im ersten Jahr*).

# Solution

With compound interest, your first year's interest earns interest of its own in the second year, so the two growth factors multiply together. Because the starting balance <InlineMath math="10\,000" /> equals <InlineMath math="100 \cdot 100" />, **distributing one factor of a hundred into each parenthesis clears every fraction instantly**!

> Two-year compound interest multiplies consecutive growth factors, and a starting capital of ten thousand absorbs both denominators of one hundred to give an integer quadratic equation.

Write the final balance equation for first-year rate <InlineMath math="P" /> percent.

<BlockMath math="10\,000 \cdot \left(1 + \frac{P}{100}\right)\left(1 + \frac{P + 2}{100}\right) = 11\,235" />

## Solving the integer quadratic

Multiply each bracket by <InlineMath math="100" /> and factor to obtain **the first-year interest rate**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(100 + P)(102 + P) &= 11\,235 \\
P^2 + 202P + 10\,200 &= 11\,235 \\
P^2 + 202P - 1\,035 &= 0 \\
(P - 5)(P + 207) &= 0
\end{aligned}" />

Since a rate of <InlineMath math="-207\,\%" /> makes no financial sense, only <InlineMath math="P = 5" /> (<InlineMath math="5\,\%" />) survives. See on the graph how close the equal-rate curve at <InlineMath math="P = 6" /> comes to the target.

Notice the neat algebra behind that <InlineMath math="1\text{ €}" /> gap: <InlineMath math="5\,\%" /> followed by <InlineMath math="7\,\%" /> yields one euro less than two <InlineMath math="6\,\%" /> years because <InlineMath math="(a - 1)(a + 1)" /> sits one below <InlineMath math="a^2" />.

## Checking yearly balances and wrong choices

Verify year by year for **rates <InlineMath math="5\,\%" /> then <InlineMath math="7\,\%" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{Year 1: } &10\,000 \cdot 1.05 = 10\,500\text{ €} \\
\text{Year 2: } &10\,500 \cdot 1.07 = 11\,235\text{ €}
\end{aligned}" />

- <InlineMath math="6\,\%" /> assumes the same rate in both years, giving <InlineMath math="11\,236\text{ €}" /> instead of <InlineMath math="11\,235\text{ €}" />.
- <InlineMath math="7\,\%" /> is the second-year interest rate rather than the first-year rate.
- <InlineMath math="5.175\,\%" /> ignores compounding by adding both rates linearly to <InlineMath math="12.35\,\%" />.
- <InlineMath math="6.175\,\%" /> splits the total <InlineMath math="12.35\,\%" /> two-year gain evenly across two years.
- <InlineMath math="12.35\,\%" /> is the combined two-year growth rather than a single year's rate.

# Answer

The interest rate in the first year was **<InlineMath math="5\,\%" />**.
