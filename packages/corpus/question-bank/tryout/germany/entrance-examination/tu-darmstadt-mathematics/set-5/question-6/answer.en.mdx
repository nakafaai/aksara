# Write both years as factors

Call the first year's rate <InlineMath math="P" /> percent. Then the balance grows by the factor <InlineMath math="1+\frac{P}{100}" /> in the first year and by <InlineMath math="1+\frac{P+2}{100}" /> in the second. **The first year's interest earns interest in the second year**, so the two factors are multiplied:

<BlockMath math="10{,}000\cdot\left(1+\frac{P}{100}\right)\left(1+\frac{P+2}{100}\right)=11{,}235" />

# A quadratic equation in <InlineMath math="P" />

Since <InlineMath math="10{,}000=100\cdot100" />, you can multiply each factor by <InlineMath math="100" />. That gives **an equation in whole numbers**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(100+P)(102+P)&=11{,}235 \\
P^2+202P+10{,}200&=11{,}235 \\
P^2+202P-1{,}035&=0 \\
(P-5)(P+207)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

The factorisation works because <InlineMath math="5\cdot207=1{,}035" /> and <InlineMath math="207-5=202" />.

# Discard the negative solution

A rate of <InlineMath math="-207\,\%" /> is **not a sensible interest rate**, because nothing would be left after the first year. So <InlineMath math="P=5" />:

# Why the other answers are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6\,\%" /> assumes **two equal rates**. That gives <InlineMath math="10{,}000\cdot1.06^2=11{,}236\text{ €}" />, almost the same amount, but the rates must differ by <InlineMath math="2" /> percentage points.
- <InlineMath math="7\,\%" /> is the second year's rate.
- <InlineMath math="5.175\,\%" /> works without compound interest: <InlineMath math="P+(P+2)=12.35" />.
- <InlineMath math="6.175\,\%" /> splits the total growth of <InlineMath math="12.35\,\%" /> evenly over two years.
- <InlineMath math="12.35\,\%" /> is the growth over both years together, not one year's rate.
