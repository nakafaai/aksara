# Given

We are given two **logarithm values** in different bases.

<BlockMath math="\log_2 3 = a, \qquad \log_3 5 = b" />

# Asked

Express the term (*Term*) <InlineMath math="\log_{15} 20" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" />.

# Solution

Think of two rulers with different units: one measured in powers of <InlineMath math="2" /> and one in powers of <InlineMath math="3" />. To compare everything directly, **move every logarithm onto one common ruler**, namely base <InlineMath math="2" />.

> Changing a logarithm's base means measuring both numbers on one shared logarithmic ruler and dividing the two heights.

See how <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="20" /> all sit cleanly along one curve <InlineMath math="y = \log_2 x" /> below.

On the base-<InlineMath math="2" /> scale, <InlineMath math="20" /> sits slightly higher than <InlineMath math="15" />, so their ratio must lie just above <InlineMath math="1" />.

## Chaining bases into base two

The number <InlineMath math="5" /> appears only in <InlineMath math="b = \log_3 5" />. Use the **logarithm chain rule** to jump from base <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="5" /> via <InlineMath math="3" />, then split <InlineMath math="20 = 4 \cdot 5" /> and <InlineMath math="15 = 3 \cdot 5" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\log_2 5 &= \log_2 3 \cdot \log_3 5 = ab \\
\log_2 20 &= \log_2 4 + \log_2 5 = 2 + ab \\
\log_2 15 &= \log_2 3 + \log_2 5 = a + ab
\end{aligned}" />

Substitute both base-two values into the change-of-base formula.

<BlockMath math="\log_{15} 20 = \frac{\log_2 20}{\log_2 15} = \frac{2 + ab}{a + ab}" />

## Numerical check and wrong choices

Test with <InlineMath math="a \approx 1.585" /> and <InlineMath math="b \approx 1.465" /> to confirm **the ratio makes complete sense**.

<BlockMath math="\frac{2 + 1.585 \times 1.465}{1.585 + 1.585 \times 1.465} \approx \frac{4.322}{3.907} \approx 1.106" />

- Replacing <InlineMath math="ab" /> with <InlineMath math="b" /> in both places treats <InlineMath math="b" /> as base <InlineMath math="2" /> instead of base <InlineMath math="3" />.
- Leaving <InlineMath math="a + b" /> in the denominator chains the base in the numerator only and forgets the denominator.
- Putting <InlineMath math="2ab" /> in the numerator multiplies logarithms instead of adding them.
- Putting <InlineMath math="a^2 b" /> in the denominator makes the same product mistake in the denominator.
- Flipping the fraction puts <InlineMath math="2 + ab" /> below, which computes <InlineMath math="\log_{20} 15" /> instead.

# Answer

The expression equal to <InlineMath math="\log_{15} 20" /> is shown below.

<BlockMath math="\frac{2 + ab}{a + ab}" />
