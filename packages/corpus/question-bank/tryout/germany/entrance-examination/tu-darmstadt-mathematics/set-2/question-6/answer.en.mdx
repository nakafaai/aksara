The vertex (*Scheitelpunkt*) is easiest to read from the **vertex form** <InlineMath math="y=(x-d)^2+e" />, which you get by completing the square. Halve the coefficient of <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, to get <InlineMath math="3" />. Then add its square <InlineMath math="9" /> and subtract it again straight away.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y&=x^2+6x+9-9+7 \\
&=(x+3)^2-2
\end{aligned}" />

The expression <InlineMath math="(x+3)^2" /> is never negative and equals zero exactly at <InlineMath math="x=-3" />. So the smallest <InlineMath math="y" />-value is <InlineMath math="-2" />, and the vertex is <InlineMath math="(-3\mid-2)" />.

As a check, the points <InlineMath math="(-6\mid7)" /> and <InlineMath math="(0\mid7)" /> lie on the parabola and have the same <InlineMath math="y" />-value. The vertex lies exactly halfway between them, at <InlineMath math="x=-3" />, and <InlineMath math="(-3)^2+6\cdot(-3)+7=-2" />.

So the vertex is <InlineMath math="(-3\mid-2)" />.

- <InlineMath math="(-3\mid7)" /> has the right <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate but takes the constant term <InlineMath math="7" /> as the <InlineMath math="y" />-value instead of substituting <InlineMath math="x=-3" />.
- <InlineMath math="(3\mid34)" /> comes from the wrong sign of the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate, because the vertex lies at <InlineMath math="x=-\frac{b}{2a}=-3" />.
- <InlineMath math="(-6\mid7)" /> takes the coefficient <InlineMath math="6" /> with a minus sign as the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate without halving it. This point does lie on the parabola, but it is not the lowest point.
- <InlineMath math="(-3\mid2)" /> swaps the signs while completing the square and computes <InlineMath math="9-7=2" /> instead of <InlineMath math="-9+7=-2" />.
- <InlineMath math="(-3\mid16)" /> adds the square used for completing instead of subtracting it again, because <InlineMath math="9+7=16" />.
