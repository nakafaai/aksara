For a quadratic inequality, find the **zeros** first. The opening of the parabola then decides where the expression is negative. The expression factors, because <InlineMath math="-3\cdot2=-6" /> and <InlineMath math="-3+2=-1" />.

<BlockMath math="x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)" />

The zeros are <InlineMath math="x=-2" /> and <InlineMath math="x=3" />. The parabola <InlineMath math="y=x^2-x-6" /> opens upward, because the coefficient of <InlineMath math="x^2" /> is positive. So it lies below the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis **between** the zeros and above it outside them. Since <InlineMath math="=0" /> is also allowed, the zeros themselves belong to the solution.

<BlockMath math="-2\le x\le3" />

As a check, <InlineMath math="x=0" /> gives <InlineMath math="-6\le0" />, which holds, while <InlineMath math="x=4" /> gives <InlineMath math="16-4-6=6" />, which is not <InlineMath math="\le0" />.

So the solution set is <InlineMath math="-2\le x\le3" />.

- <InlineMath math="-3\le x\le2" /> comes from reversing the signs of the zeros, that is, from <InlineMath math="(x+3)(x-2)" />. That product equals <InlineMath math="x^2+x-6" /> instead.
- <InlineMath math="x\le-2" /> or <InlineMath math="x\ge3" /> describes where the expression is greater than or equal to zero, the solution of <InlineMath math="x^2-x-6\ge0" />.
- <InlineMath math="-2<x<3" /> drops the endpoints, although <InlineMath math="\le" /> allows the expression to equal zero.
- <InlineMath math="x\le3" /> and <InlineMath math="x\ge-2" /> treat the factors separately and forget the other condition each time. For example, <InlineMath math="x=-5" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="x\le3" />, but <InlineMath math="25+5-6=24" /> is not <InlineMath math="\le0" />.
