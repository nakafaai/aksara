Two percentage changes in a row cannot simply be added or subtracted, because the second change refers to **a different base value**. The <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> decrease is taken from the higher level in summer, not from the level at the start of the year. So work with factors: an increase of <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> means the factor <InlineMath math="1.1" />, and a decrease of <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> means the factor <InlineMath math="0.9" />.

Let <InlineMath math="n" /> be the number of members (*Mitglieder*) at the start of the year. At the end of the year:

<BlockMath math="n\cdot1.1\cdot0.9=n\cdot0.99" />

The factor <InlineMath math="0.99" /> means that the membership at the end of the year is <InlineMath math="99\,\%" /> of the original number. So the number fell by <InlineMath math="1\,\%" />.

A numerical example confirms this. With <InlineMath math="500" /> members, <InlineMath math="50" /> join by the summer, which makes <InlineMath math="550" />. Of these, <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> is exactly <InlineMath math="55" />, so <InlineMath math="495" /> members remain. That is <InlineMath math="5" /> fewer than at the start, and <InlineMath math="5" /> is <InlineMath math="1\,\%" /> of <InlineMath math="500" />.

So the number of members fell by <InlineMath math="1\,\%" /> overall.

- An increase of <InlineMath math="21\,\%" /> counts both changes as increases, because <InlineMath math="1.1\cdot1.1=1.21" />.
- An increase of <InlineMath math="1\,\%" /> has the right size but the wrong direction. The factor <InlineMath math="0.99" /> is less than <InlineMath math="1" />.
- No change comes from simply combining the percentages: <InlineMath math="10\,\%-10\,\%=0\,\%" />. The two percentages refer to different base values, though.
- A decrease of <InlineMath math="0.1\,\%" /> misplaces the decimal point. The difference <InlineMath math="1-0.99=0.01" /> corresponds to <InlineMath math="1\,\%" />.
- A decrease of <InlineMath math="10\,\%" /> only takes the second change into account.
