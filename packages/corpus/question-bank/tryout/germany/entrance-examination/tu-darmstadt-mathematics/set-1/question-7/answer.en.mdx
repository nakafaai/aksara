# What happens to the salt at each step

Pure water carries no salt. So every time <InlineMath math="x" /> litres of solution are taken out, **the fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{x}{40}" /> of the salt present at that moment goes with them**, and then the volume goes back to <InlineMath math="40" /> litres. After one step, the fraction of salt left is <InlineMath math="1-\frac{x}{40}" />.

# Setting up the equation

After two steps, the concentration has been multiplied by that factor twice:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
0.2\cdot\left(1-\tfrac{x}{40}\right)^2&=0.05 \\
\left(1-\tfrac{x}{40}\right)^2&=0.25 \\
1-\tfrac{x}{40}&=0.5 \\
x&=20
\end{aligned}" />

The negative root <InlineMath math="1-\frac{x}{40}=-0.5" /> gives <InlineMath math="x=60" />, and **that's impossible because the container only holds <InlineMath math="40" /> litres**.

# A check with the amount of salt

At first there are <InlineMath math="0.2\cdot40=8" /> units of salt. Taking out <InlineMath math="20" /> litres removes half, leaving <InlineMath math="4" />. Taking out <InlineMath math="20" /> litres again removes half of that, leaving <InlineMath math="2" />. The final concentration is <InlineMath math="\frac{2}{40}=5\%" />. **It matches the problem**.

So <InlineMath math="x=20" /> litres.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="15" /> litres assumes both steps lower the concentration by the same amount: <InlineMath math="0.2\left(1-\frac{2x}{40}\right)=0.05" />. **The second step only removes the salt that's left**, not the original salt.
- <InlineMath math="30" /> litres assumes there's only one replacement: <InlineMath math="0.2\left(1-\frac{x}{40}\right)=0.05" />.
- <InlineMath math="10" /> litres gives <InlineMath math="0.2\cdot0.75^2=11.25\%" />, still too high.
- <InlineMath math="25" /> and <InlineMath math="35" /> litres give about <InlineMath math="2.8\%" /> and <InlineMath math="0.3\%" />, too low.
