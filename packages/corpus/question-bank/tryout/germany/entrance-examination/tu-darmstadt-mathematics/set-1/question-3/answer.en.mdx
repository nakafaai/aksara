First bring the quadratic equation into **standard form with zero on one side**. Only then can you factor it or use a solution formula.

<BlockMath math="2x^2-3x-2=0" />

To factor, look for two numbers with product <InlineMath math="2\cdot(-2)=-4" /> and sum <InlineMath math="-3" />. They are <InlineMath math="-4" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />. Use them to split the middle term.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2x^2-4x+x-2&=0 \\
2x(x-2)+1\cdot(x-2)&=0 \\
(2x+1)(x-2)&=0
\end{aligned}" />

A product is zero when one factor is zero. From <InlineMath math="2x+1=0" /> you get <InlineMath math="x=-\frac12" />, and from <InlineMath math="x-2=0" /> you get <InlineMath math="x=2" />. The discriminant <InlineMath math="(-3)^2-4\cdot2\cdot(-2)=25" /> is positive and confirms two different solutions.

As a check: for <InlineMath math="x=-\frac12" />, <InlineMath math="2\cdot\frac14=\frac12" /> and <InlineMath math="3\cdot\left(-\frac12\right)+2=\frac12" />. For <InlineMath math="x=2" />, <InlineMath math="8=6+2" />.

So the solutions are <InlineMath math="x=-\frac12" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" />.

- Only <InlineMath math="x=2" /> drops the second solution. A quadratic equation with a positive discriminant has two solutions.
- <InlineMath math="x=\frac12" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-2" /> belong to <InlineMath math="(2x-1)(x+2)=2x^2+3x-2" />. The sign of the middle term is reversed there.
- <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" /> solve <InlineMath math="x^2-x-2=0" />. The factor <InlineMath math="2" /> in front of <InlineMath math="x^2" /> was lost.
- <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-2" /> combine the lost factor <InlineMath math="2" /> with a sign error.
- No real solution is wrong, because the discriminant <InlineMath math="25" /> is positive.
