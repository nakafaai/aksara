# Given

Two fixed points lie in the coordinate plane: <InlineMath math="A(1 \mid 3)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(7 \mid 1)" />. A third point <InlineMath math="P(x \mid 0)" /> moves along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis so that triangle <InlineMath math="ABP" /> is a **right-angled triangle** (*rechtwinkliges Dreieck*).

# Asked

Find all real values of <InlineMath math="x" /> for which triangle <InlineMath math="ABP" /> has a right angle.

# Solution

The problem never fixes which corner holds the right angle, so imagine placing a set square at **each of the three vertices**: at <InlineMath math="A" />, at <InlineMath math="B" />, or at <InlineMath math="P" />.

> Two line segments are perpendicular exactly when the dot product of their direction vectors equals zero.

Look at how two perpendicular lines through <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> together with the Thales circle on diameter <InlineMath math="AB" /> hit the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at four points.

Each green point on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis creates a distinct right triangle. Now compute all four coordinates algebraically using direction vectors.

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \overrightarrow{AP} = \begin{pmatrix} x - 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \overrightarrow{BP} = \begin{pmatrix} x - 7 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" />

## Right angle at A or B

A right angle at <InlineMath math="A" /> sets the dot product of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AP}" /> to zero, while a right angle at <InlineMath math="B" /> sets the dot product of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BP}" /> to zero.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
6(x - 1) + (-2)(-3) &= 0 \implies 6x = 0 \implies x = 0 \\
-6(x - 7) + 2(-1) &= 0 \implies 40 - 6x = 0 \implies x = \frac{20}{3}
\end{aligned}" />

**Each case yields one unique point** because both equations are linear.

## Right angle at P

A right angle at <InlineMath math="P" /> means <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PA}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PB}" /> are perpendicular. Geometrically, <InlineMath math="P" /> lies on the Thales circle centered at <InlineMath math="(4 \mid 2)" /> with radius <InlineMath math="\sqrt{10}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(1 - x)(7 - x) + 3 \cdot 1 &= 0 \\
x^2 - 8x + 10 &= 0 \\
x &= 4 \pm \sqrt{6}
\end{aligned}" />

Collinearity check: line <InlineMath math="AB" /> crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis only at <InlineMath math="x = 10" />. Because none of our four solutions equals <InlineMath math="10" />, **all four points form genuine triangles**.

## Why other choices fail

- Listing only <InlineMath math="4 \pm \sqrt{6}" /> **misses the right angles** at <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />.
- Listing only <InlineMath math="0" /> or <InlineMath math="\frac{20}{3}" /> misses the right angle at <InlineMath math="P" />.
- Getting <InlineMath math="\frac{22}{3}" /> comes from a sign slip in the dot product at <InlineMath math="B" />.
- Including <InlineMath math="x = 10" /> puts <InlineMath math="A" />, <InlineMath math="B" /> and <InlineMath math="P" /> on one straight line, leaving no triangle.
- Getting <InlineMath math="x = 2" /> comes from flipping the sign of <InlineMath math="(-2)(-3)" /> in the case at <InlineMath math="A" />.

# Answer

Triangle <InlineMath math="ABP" /> is right-angled exactly for the four values below.

<BlockMath math="x = 0, \quad x = 4 - \sqrt{6}, \quad x = 4 + \sqrt{6}, \quad x = \frac{20}{3}" />
