# Given

We are given a quadratic equation with a real parameter <InlineMath math="a" />.

<BlockMath math="x^2 - 2ax + a + 6 = 0" />

The problem asks for **two distinct positive real solutions** (*zwei verschiedene positive reelle Lösungen*).

# Asked

Find all real values of <InlineMath math="a" /> for which the equation has two distinct positive real roots.

# Solution

Picture the upward-opening parabola defined by the left-hand side. For the equation to have two distinct positive roots, the curve must **cross the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at two separate points to the right of zero**.

> Two real numbers are both positive exactly when their product is positive so they share a sign, and their sum is positive.

At <InlineMath math="a = 4" />, the vertex lies in the fourth quadrant and both intercepts are positive. At <InlineMath math="a = 3" />, the curve touches the axis once, and at <InlineMath math="a = -3" /> both roots are negative.

## Three conditions at once

Since the leading coefficient is <InlineMath math="1" />, combine the **discriminant** (*Diskriminante*) with Vieta's formulas for the sum and product of the roots.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
D &= (-2a)^2 - 4(1)(a + 6) = 4(a - 3)(a + 2) > 0 \\
x_1 + x_2 &= 2a > 0 \\
x_1 x_2 &= a + 6 > 0
\end{aligned}" />

Solving each inequality gives three ranges of <InlineMath math="a" /> that must hold together.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
D > 0 &\iff a < -2 \quad \text{or} \quad a > 3 \\
x_1 + x_2 > 0 &\iff a > 0 \\
x_1 x_2 > 0 &\iff a > -6
\end{aligned}" />

## Intersecting and checking

All three conditions must hold at the same time, so take their **intersection**. The branch <InlineMath math="a < -2" /> fails <InlineMath math="a > 0" />, leaving only <InlineMath math="a > 3" />.

Test concrete values on both sides of the boundary to verify.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a = 4 &\implies x^2 - 8x + 10 = 0 \implies x = 4 \pm \sqrt{6} > 0 \\
a = 3 &\implies (x - 3)^2 = 0 \implies x = 3 \quad \text{(only one root)} \\
a = -3 &\implies x^2 + 6x + 3 = 0 \implies x = -3 \pm \sqrt{6} < 0
\end{aligned}" />

## Why other choices fail

- Choosing <InlineMath math="a < -2" /> or <InlineMath math="a > 3" /> checks only the discriminant and allows negative roots when <InlineMath math="a < -2" />.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="a > 0" /> checks only the root sum, yet at <InlineMath math="a = 1" /> the discriminant is negative so no real roots exist.
- Including the interval between <InlineMath math="-6" /> and <InlineMath math="-2" /> **drops the positive-sum condition**, letting <InlineMath math="a = -3" /> produce two negative roots.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="a \ge 3" /> includes <InlineMath math="a = 3" />, where the equation has only one repeated root.
- Choosing the interval between <InlineMath math="-2" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> flips the discriminant sign and picks the region with no real roots at all.

# Answer

The equation has two distinct positive real solutions exactly when <InlineMath math="a > 3" />.
