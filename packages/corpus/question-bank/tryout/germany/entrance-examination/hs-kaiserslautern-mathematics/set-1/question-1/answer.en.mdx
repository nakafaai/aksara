# What the drying cannot touch

Picture a wet sponge left in the sun. The water leaves, but the sponge itself stays just as heavy as its dry self. The apricots behave the same way. **The dry matter keeps its mass**: <InlineMath math="15\,\%" /> of <InlineMath math="50\text{ kg}" /> is <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ kg}" />, before and after drying.

After drying, this dry matter makes up <InlineMath math="75\,\%" /> of the fruit, because only <InlineMath math="25\,\%" /> is still water. So the dried apricots weigh

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
0.75\cdot m&=7.5\text{ kg}\\
m&=10\text{ kg}
\end{aligned}" />

Subtracting the percentages, <InlineMath math="85-25=60" />, and taking <InlineMath math="60\,\%" /> of the mass away gives <InlineMath math="20\text{ kg}" />, twice too much. A percentage only means something next to its whole, and here the whole shrinks while you dry.

# Adding up the costs

Now count the money like a till receipt. The fresh fruit costs <InlineMath math="50\cdot3.00=150\text{ €}" />. The water that leaves is <InlineMath math="50-10=40\text{ kg}" />, so drying costs <InlineMath math="40\cdot0.40=16\text{ €}" />. **All costs together come to <InlineMath math="166\text{ €}" />**.

# From the profit to the price

A profit of <InlineMath math="50\,\%" /> of the costs means the takings must be one and a half times the costs, <InlineMath math="1.5\cdot166=249\text{ €}" />. **Shared over <InlineMath math="10\text{ kg}" />, one kilogram must sell for <InlineMath math="24.90\text{ €}" />**.
