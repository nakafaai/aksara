# Price and bikes on one seesaw

Price and demand sit on a seesaw: push the price up by <InlineMath math="1\text{ €}" /> and <InlineMath math="4" /> customers walk away. With price <InlineMath math="p" /> the shop rents <InlineMath math="n=40-4(p-12)=88-4p" /> bikes, and **each bike earns <InlineMath math="p-2" /> euros after cleaning**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
G(p)&=(p-2)(88-4p)\\
&=-4p^2+96p-176
\end{aligned}" />

# The top of the parabola is out of reach

The parabola opens downwards, and its top sits halfway between its zeros <InlineMath math="p=2" /> and <InlineMath math="p=22" />, at <InlineMath math="p=12" /> with <InlineMath math="G=400\text{ €}" />. **But at <InlineMath math="12\text{ €}" /> the shop would need <InlineMath math="40" /> bikes, and it owns only <InlineMath math="36" />**. So <InlineMath math="88-4p\le36" /> forces <InlineMath math="p\ge13" />.

# The best allowed price

Right of its top the parabola only falls, so on <InlineMath math="p\ge13" /> the best price is the edge itself. **At <InlineMath math="13\text{ €}" /> all <InlineMath math="36" /> bikes go out and <InlineMath math="G=11\cdot36=396\text{ €}" />**. The answer <InlineMath math="400\text{ €}" /> uses four bikes the shop does not have.
