# Given

**A circular drainage** pipe has a water surface width of <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ m}" /> when the water is <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> deep. After a storm the surface is again <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ m}" /> wide, now sitting higher in the pipe.

# Asked

By how many centimetres has the water level risen?

# Solution

> Inside any circle, two horizontal chords of equal length sit **at the exact same distance below and above the centre**.

## Finding the radius

Hold a ruler across a round plate: the ruler is a chord, and the line from the centre to the middle of the ruler meets it at a right angle. **Half the water surface, <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ m}" />, the distance from the centre down to the water, <InlineMath math="r-0.8" />, and the radius <InlineMath math="r" /> form a right triangle**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
(r-0.8)^2+1.2^2&=r^2\\
-1.6r+0.64+1.44&=0\\
r&=1.3\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

Taking half the width, <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ m}" />, as the radius forgets that the water does not reach the centre.

## The mirror image above the centre

So the low water surface lies <InlineMath math="1.3-0.8=0.5\text{ m}" /> below the centre. A circle looks the same upside down, so **the only other chord of length <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ m}" /> that is parallel to the water lies <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ m}" /> above the centre**.

Both equal-width waterlines sit **symmetrically below and above the circle centre**, so the rise in water level equals **twice** the distance from the centre.

## The rise

From <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ m}" /> below to <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ m}" /> above the centre is <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" />. **The water rose by <InlineMath math="100" /> centimetres**, from a depth of <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> to <InlineMath math="1.8\text{ m}" />.

# Answer

The water level has risen by <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}" />.
