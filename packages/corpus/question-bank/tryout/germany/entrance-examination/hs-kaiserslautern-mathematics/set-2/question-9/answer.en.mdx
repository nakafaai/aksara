# Two arrivals become one point

Write Anna's arrival as <InlineMath math="x" /> and Ben's as <InlineMath math="y" />, in minutes after 17:00, both between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="40" />. **Each pair of arrivals is one point of a square with side <InlineMath math="40" />, and because every moment is equally likely, a probability is a share of its area <InlineMath math="1600" />**. It is like a raindrop landing anywhere on a square tile: the chance of hitting a region is that region's share of the tile.

# Ben first: one clean cut

If Ben is there first, so <InlineMath math="y<x" />, only his patience counts. **He waits <InlineMath math="20" /> minutes, so the two miss each other when <InlineMath math="x-y>20" />**. That is the corner below the line <InlineMath math="y=x-20" />, a right triangle with legs <InlineMath math="20" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A_1&=\tfrac12\cdot20\cdot20=200
\end{aligned}" />

# Anna first: her edge bends

Anna leaves at the later of two times: 17:30, or <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes after she arrives. Up to <InlineMath math="x=20" /> the 17:30 deadline is later, after that her <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes win. **So her edge is not one straight line: it runs flat at <InlineMath math="y=30" /> until <InlineMath math="x=20" /> and then climbs along <InlineMath math="y=x+10" /> to the corner <InlineMath math="(30\mid40)" />**. Above it Ben comes too late, a strip of <InlineMath math="20" /> by <InlineMath math="10" /> and a small triangle with legs <InlineMath math="10" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A_2&=20\cdot10+\tfrac12\cdot10\cdot10=250\\
P&=\frac{1600-200-250}{1600}=\frac{1150}{1600}=0.71875
\end{aligned}" />

The two meet with probability <InlineMath math="0.71875" />. Letting Anna wait only <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes turns the upper corner into a triangle of <InlineMath math="450" /> and gives <InlineMath math="0.59375" />. If she always left at 17:30, even right after a late arrival, you would get <InlineMath math="0.65625" />.
