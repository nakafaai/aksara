# Three equations for three amounts

Call the kilograms of the sorts <InlineMath math="a, b, c" />. Protein and fat are counted in percent times kilograms. **The total, the protein and the fat each give one equation**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a+b+c&=100\\
12a+18b+30c&=2100\\
8a+6b+2c&=500
\end{aligned}" />

# The fat condition repeats the others

Subtract twelve times the first equation from the second. That gives <InlineMath math="6b+18c=900" />, so <InlineMath math="b=150-3c" /> and then <InlineMath math="a=2c-50" />. **Putting both into the fat equation turns it into <InlineMath math="500=500" />, true for every <InlineMath math="c" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
b&=150-3c\\
a&=100-b-c=2c-50\\
8(2c-50)+6(150-3c)+2c&=500
\end{aligned}" />

It is like two witnesses telling the same story, the second one adds nothing. The reason sits in the data: for every sort the fat is <InlineMath math="12" /> minus a third of the protein, and so is the target, <InlineMath math="5=12-7" />. **So there is not one mix but a whole family, with <InlineMath math="c" /> free**. Amounts cannot be negative, so <InlineMath math="2c-50\ge0" /> and <InlineMath math="150-3c\ge0" /> give <InlineMath math="25\le c\le50" />.

# The cheapest member of the family

Now the price chooses. **The cost grows by <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ €}" /> with every extra kilogram of C, so the cheapest mix uses as little C as allowed**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
K&=0.4(2c-50)+0.3(150-3c)+0.6c\\
&=25+0.5c\\
K(25)&=37.5\text{ €}
\end{aligned}" />

That mix is <InlineMath math="75\text{ kg}" /> of B and <InlineMath math="25\text{ kg}" /> of C with no A at all. The other end of the family, <InlineMath math="50\text{ kg}" /> each of A and C, costs <InlineMath math="50\text{ €}" />.
