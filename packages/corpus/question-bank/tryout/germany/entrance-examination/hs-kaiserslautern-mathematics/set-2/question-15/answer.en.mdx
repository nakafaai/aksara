# Count trips, not litres

A tanker that holds <InlineMath math="3000" /> litres has to drive out again each time it fills up. **Each farm needs as many trips as <InlineMath math="3000" /> litres fit into its milk, rounded up**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n_A&=\left\lceil\tfrac{2500}{3000}\right\rceil=1,\qquad n_B=\left\lceil\tfrac{10\,000}{3000}\right\rceil=4\\
n_C&=\left\lceil\tfrac{5500}{3000}\right\rceil=2,\qquad n_D=\left\lceil\tfrac{3200}{3000}\right\rceil=2
\end{aligned}" />

Farm <InlineMath math="D" /> is the trap: <InlineMath math="3200" /> litres are just over one load, so it needs <InlineMath math="2" /> trips.

# Distance as a sum of absolute values

Every trip runs from the collection point at <InlineMath math="x" /> to one farm and back. **Counted one way, the day's trips add up to a sum of absolute values, and the daily distance is twice as long**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=1\cdot|x|+4\,|x-6|+2\,|x-10|+2\,|x-16|\\
D(x)&=2f(x)
\end{aligned}" />

Picture walking along the road: one step to the right brings you closer to every trip on your right and farther from every trip on your left. **So the slope of <InlineMath math="f" /> is the number of trips on the left minus the number on the right**.

# The best point is forbidden

Without the zone, the lowest point of <InlineMath math="f" /> is where the slope turns from negative to positive. Counting from the left there are <InlineMath math="1" />, then <InlineMath math="1+4=5" /> trips, more than half of the <InlineMath math="9" />, and that happens at farm <InlineMath math="B" />. So the minimum is at kilometre <InlineMath math="6" /> with <InlineMath math="f(6)=34" />. **But kilometre <InlineMath math="6" /> lies inside the protection zone, so only its two edges are left**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
5\le x\le6&:\quad 1-(4+2+2)=-7,\qquad f(5)=34+7=41\\
6\le x\le9&:\quad (1+4)-(2+2)=1,\qquad f(9)=34+3=37
\end{aligned}" />

# The far edge wins

The edge at kilometre <InlineMath math="5" /> is closer to the minimum, but <InlineMath math="f" /> is steep there: every kilometre adds <InlineMath math="7" />. Towards kilometre <InlineMath math="9" /> every kilometre adds only <InlineMath math="1" />. **So the collection point goes to kilometre <InlineMath math="9" />, and the tanker drives <InlineMath math="2\cdot37=74\text{ km}" /> a day**.

The nearer edge at kilometre <InlineMath math="5" /> would give <InlineMath math="82\text{ km}" />, and leaving out the way back gives only <InlineMath math="37\text{ km}" />. Weighting by thousands of litres instead of trips gives <InlineMath math="160.8" />, a number that is not even a distance. Counting <InlineMath math="D" /> as one trip lands at <InlineMath math="60\text{ km}" />.
