# Given

**In a forest**, <InlineMath math="75" /> identical logs are stacked so each layer has one fewer log than the layer below it and the top layer has at least <InlineMath math="1" /> log. The yard fits at most <InlineMath math="16" /> logs across the bottom, the first layer is <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> high, each extra layer adds <InlineMath math="26\text{ cm}" />, and the stack can be at most <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> high.

# Asked

How many logs lie in the bottom layer?

# Solution

> Double the stack to turn the staircase of consecutive integers into a clean rectangle whose area is <InlineMath math="150" />, so **only divisor pairs of <InlineMath math="150" /> can even exist**.

## A stack is a staircase of numbers

Look at the stack from the front: each layer is one log shorter than the one below, like the steps of a staircase. **With <InlineMath math="n" /> layers and <InlineMath math="a" /> logs on top, the bottom layer has <InlineMath math="a+n-1" /> logs, and the total is the number of layers times the average layer**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n\cdot\frac{a+(a+n-1)}{2}&=75\\
n\,(2a+n-1)&=150
\end{aligned}" />

## Splitting one hundred fifty into two factors

Both factors are whole numbers, and <InlineMath math="2a+n-1" /> is bigger than <InlineMath math="n" /> because <InlineMath math="a\ge1" />. **Their difference <InlineMath math="2a-1" /> is odd, so one factor is even and the other odd**. That leaves exactly six ways to write <InlineMath math="150" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n=1&:\ 2a=150,\ a=75\\
n=2&:\ 2a+1=75,\ a=37\\
n=3&:\ 2a+2=50,\ a=24\\
n=5&:\ 2a+4=30,\ a=13\\
n=6&:\ 2a+5=25,\ a=10\\
n=10&:\ 2a+9=15,\ a=3
\end{aligned}" />

## Width and height pick the stack

The bottom layers hold <InlineMath math="75, 38, 26, 17, 15" /> and <InlineMath math="12" /> logs. **Only <InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="12" /> fit into the <InlineMath math="16" /> places side by side**. Now the height decides between six and ten layers:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
30+26\,(n-1)&\le200\\
n-1&\le6.5
\end{aligned}" />

So at most seven layers, and ten layers would stand <InlineMath math="264\text{ cm}" /> tall. Stopping after the width test leaves two answers, <InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="12" />. **The stack has six layers, and its bottom layer holds <InlineMath math="15" /> logs**.

# Answer

There are <InlineMath math="15" /> logs in the bottom layer.
