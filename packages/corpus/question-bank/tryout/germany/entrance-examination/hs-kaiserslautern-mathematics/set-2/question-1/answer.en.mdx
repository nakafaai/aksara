# Given

**In the first referendum**, turnout is <InlineMath math="60\,\%" />, <InlineMath math="4\,\%" /> of cast ballots are invalid, and <InlineMath math="40\,\%" /> of valid ballots vote yes. In the second referendum, the electorate falls by <InlineMath math="4\,\%" />, turnout rises by <InlineMath math="15" /> percentage points, invalid ballots drop to <InlineMath math="2\,\%" />, and the yes share rises by <InlineMath math="2" /> percentage points.

# Asked

By what percentage is the number of yes votes higher in the second referendum than in the first?

# Solution

> Think of each stage as a multiplier gate, because **a rise in percentage points adds directly to the old share** before you take the ratio of the two chains.

## A yes vote passes four gates

Picture a sieve with four layers. Everyone entitled to vote is poured in at the top, but only those who go to vote pass the first layer, only valid ballots pass the second, and only the yes ballots pass the last. **So the number of yes votes is the electorate times three shares**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
J_1&=E\cdot0.60\cdot0.96\cdot0.40\\
J_2&=0.96E\cdot0.75\cdot0.98\cdot0.42
\end{aligned}" />

Here <InlineMath math="E" /> is the first electorate. For the second vote the electorate is <InlineMath math="4\,\%" /> smaller, which is the factor <InlineMath math="0.96" /> in front.

## Points are not percent

If your mark goes from <InlineMath math="60" /> to <InlineMath math="75" /> out of a hundred, you gained <InlineMath math="15" /> points, yet you scored a quarter more than before. **A rise of <InlineMath math="15" /> percentage points turns the turnout <InlineMath math="60\,\%" /> into <InlineMath math="75\,\%" />, the factor <InlineMath math="1.25" />, and <InlineMath math="2" /> points lift the yes share from <InlineMath math="40\,\%" /> to <InlineMath math="42\,\%" />, the factor <InlineMath math="1.05" />**. Reading them as <InlineMath math="1.15" /> and <InlineMath math="1.02" /> is the classic slip here.

Divide the two chains. The unknown electorate cancels, and so do the two numbers <InlineMath math="0.96" /> hiding in different places:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{J_2}{J_1}&=0.96\cdot\frac{0.75}{0.60}\cdot\frac{0.98}{0.96}\cdot\frac{0.42}{0.40}\\
&=0.98\cdot1.25\cdot1.05=1.28625
\end{aligned}" />

## How large the rise is

A factor of <InlineMath math="1.28625" /> means the old amount plus <InlineMath math="0.28625" /> of it. **The yes votes grew by <InlineMath math="28.625\,\%" />**. Simply adding the changes, <InlineMath math="-4+15+2" /> or similar, mixes percent with percentage points and cannot give this.

# Answer

The number of yes votes increased by <InlineMath math="28.625\,\%" />.
