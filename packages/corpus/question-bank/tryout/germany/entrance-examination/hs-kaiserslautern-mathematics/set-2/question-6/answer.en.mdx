# Given

**A slope rises** uniformly at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> to the horizontal. A forester stands at the foot <InlineMath math="F" /> while a vertical spruce stands <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" /> up the slope. She sees the treetop at an elevation angle of <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> to the horizontal, and the sun stands behind her in the same vertical plane at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> above the horizon.

# Asked

How long is the spruce's shadow measured along the slope?

# Solution

> Chase the angles first before reaching for trigonometry, because **the tree triangle is isosceles and the shadow triangle is right-angled**, turning the whole problem into simple <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />-<InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> geometry.

## Angles at the feet of the forester

Name the trunk's foot <InlineMath math="T" /> and its top <InlineMath math="S" />. The slope is a ramp tilted <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> up, and the forester's gaze is tilted <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> up. **So between the ramp and the gaze there are only <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> left**. At <InlineMath math="T" /> the trunk points straight up, <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> above the horizontal, while the slope back down to <InlineMath math="F" /> points <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> below it:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\angle TFS&=60^\circ-30^\circ=30^\circ\\
\angle FTS&=90^\circ+30^\circ=120^\circ\\
\angle FST&=180^\circ-30^\circ-120^\circ=30^\circ
\end{aligned}" />

## Two equal angles and two equal sides

A triangle with two equal angles is isosceles: the sides facing those angles match. **The angles at <InlineMath math="F" /> and <InlineMath math="S" /> are both <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />, so the trunk <InlineMath math="TS" /> is as long as <InlineMath math="FT" />, which is <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" />**. A right triangle with the <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" /> laid flat on the ground would ignore the rising slope and make the spruce far too tall.

## A right angle on the slope

The sun sits behind the forester, so its rays come down at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> and travel uphill. Call the tip of the shadow <InlineMath math="P" />. **At <InlineMath math="P" /> the ray, <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> above the horizontal looking back to the sun, and the slope, <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> below it looking down to <InlineMath math="T" />, add up to exactly <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />**. At <InlineMath math="T" /> the trunk and the uphill slope enclose <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\angle STP&=90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ\\
\angle TPS&=60^\circ+30^\circ=90^\circ\\
\overline{TP}&=\overline{TS}\cdot\cos60^\circ=24\cdot\tfrac12=12\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

On flat ground the same spruce would throw a shadow of <InlineMath math="13.9\text{ m}" />, but the rising slope catches it early, after <InlineMath math="12\text{ m}" />.

The spruce casts a <InlineMath math="12\text{ m}" /> shadow along the slope.
