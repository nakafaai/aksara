# Given

**On an optical bench**, a thin converging lens of unknown focal length <InlineMath math="f" /> forms a sharp image of a square slide on a screen at fixed distance <InlineMath math="L_1" />. Shifting the lens <InlineMath math="36\text{ cm}" /> toward the screen forms a second sharp image with <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{16}" /> the area. Next, a <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> square slide and a <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> square screen are placed <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}" /> apart.

# Asked

What is the area of the sharp image of the new slide that fits completely on the screen, in <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^2" />?

# Solution

> Because light paths are reversible, **sliding the lens simply swaps object distance <InlineMath math="g" /> and image distance <InlineMath math="b" />**, turning the area ratio <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{16}" /> directly into the focal length <InlineMath math="f = 24\text{ cm}" />.

## Swapping the two distances

Hold a magnifying glass between a lamp and a wall: once you find a sharp spot close to the lamp, there is a twin spot equally close to the wall. **Because the lens equation is symmetric in <InlineMath math="g" /> and <InlineMath math="b" />, the second sharp position simply swaps them, so <InlineMath math="g_2=b_1" /> and <InlineMath math="b_2=g_1" />**. Moving the lens towards the screen makes <InlineMath math="g_2>g_1" />, so <InlineMath math="b_1>g_1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac1g+\frac1{L_1-g}=\frac1f\ &\Longleftrightarrow\ g\,(L_1-g)=f\,L_1\\
g_2=b_1,\qquad b_2=g_1,\qquad \left(\frac{a_2'}{a_1'}\right)^2=\left(\frac{g_1/b_1}{b_1/g_1}\right)^2=\left(\frac{g_1}{b_1}\right)^4=\frac1{16}
\end{aligned}" />

Look closely at that fourth power. The first image is enlarged by <InlineMath math="\tfrac{b_1}{g_1}" /> while the second is shrunk by <InlineMath math="\tfrac{g_1}{b_1}" />, so the ratio of their sides is already <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{g_1}{b_1}\right)^2" />, and squaring for the area brings it to the fourth power.

## Finding the focal length

**Taking the fourth root gives <InlineMath math="\tfrac{b_1}{g_1}=2" />, and the shift of <InlineMath math="36\text{ cm}" /> fixes both distances and the focal length**. Treating the area ratio as a plain side ratio would give <InlineMath math="\tfrac{b_1}{g_1}=4" /> and a wrong focal length of <InlineMath math="9.6\text{ cm}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{b_1}{g_1}=2,\qquad b_1-g_1=36\ &\Longrightarrow\ g_1=36\text{ cm},\quad b_1=72\text{ cm}\\
\frac1f=\frac1{36}+\frac1{72}=\frac{3}{72}\ &\Longrightarrow\ f=24\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

## Checking both sharp positions on the new bench

Now <InlineMath math="L_2=100\text{ cm}" /> with the same lens <InlineMath math="f=24\text{ cm}" />. **Solving the rational equation again produces two sharp positions**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac1g+\frac1{100-g}=\frac1{24}\ &\Longleftrightarrow\ g^2-100g+2400=0\\
(g-40)(g-60)=0\ &\Longrightarrow\ g=40\text{ cm}\quad\text{or}\quad g=60\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

Now test the <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> screen against both positions. At <InlineMath math="g=40\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="b=60\text{ cm}" />, the image side is <InlineMath math="12\cdot\tfrac{60}{40}=18\text{ cm}" />, which spills past the screen and is ruled out (the trap area <InlineMath math="324\text{ cm}^2" />). At <InlineMath math="g=60\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="b=40\text{ cm}" />, the side is <InlineMath math="12\cdot\tfrac{40}{60}=8\text{ cm}" />, which fits cleanly. **The sharp image has an area of <InlineMath math="8^2=64\text{ cm}^2" />**.

# Answer

The sharp image on the screen has an area of **<InlineMath math="64\text{ cm}^2" />**.
