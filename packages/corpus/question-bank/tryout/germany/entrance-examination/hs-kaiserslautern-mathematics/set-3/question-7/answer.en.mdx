# Given

**A two-stage rocket** carries a <InlineMath math="50\text{ kg}" /> payload with stage 1 dry mass <InlineMath math="200\text{ kg}" /> (<InlineMath math="u_1=4\text{ km/s}" />), stage 2 dry mass <InlineMath math="50\text{ kg}" /> (<InlineMath math="u_2=2\text{ km/s}" />), and <InlineMath math="600\text{ kg}" /> total fuel split as <InlineMath math="T_1 + T_2 = 600" />. Total speed gain must equal <InlineMath math="2\ln(10)\text{ km/s}" /> with <InlineMath math="2\ln(2)\text{ km/s}" />.

# Asked

How many kilograms of fuel T1 does the first stage receive?

# Solution

> Because stage 1 has **twice the exhaust speed of stage 2**, combining both logarithms squares stage 1's mass ratio and collapses the rocket equation into one clean quadratic in <InlineMath math="T_2" />.

## Tracking the masses before and after jettisoning

Think of a hiker who drops a heavy empty water bottle before the final climb: stage two never carries stage one's empty <InlineMath math="200\text{ kg}" /> hull. **Call stage two's fuel <InlineMath math="x=T_2" />, so stage one gets <InlineMath math="T_1=600-x" />, and write the four masses in terms of <InlineMath math="x" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\text{start},1}=50+50+200+600=900\text{ kg},\qquad m_{\text{end},1}=900-(600-x)=300+x\\
m_{\text{start},2}=100+x,\qquad m_{\text{end},2}=50+50=100\text{ kg}
\end{aligned}" />

## Collapsing the logarithms into one quadratic

**Divide both sides by <InlineMath math="2" /> and use <InlineMath math="2\ln A+\ln B=\ln(A^2 B)" /> so every logarithm disappears at once**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
4\ln\!\left(\frac{900}{300+x}\right)+2\ln\!\left(\frac{100+x}{100}\right)=2\ln(10)\\
\left(\frac{900}{300+x}\right)^2\cdot\frac{100+x}{100}=10\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 810\,(100+x)=(300+x)^2\\
x^2-210x+9000=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (x-60)(x-150)=0
\end{aligned}" />

Two fuel splits hit the exact target speed: <InlineMath math="x=60\text{ kg}" /> and <InlineMath math="x=150\text{ kg}" />. Notice that forgetting the exponent <InlineMath math="2" /> on stage one's ratio breaks the equation completely.

The peak sits at <InlineMath math="x=100\text{ kg}" />, so the horizontal target line cuts the curve once on each side of the dashed boundary.

## Applying the minimum speed rule for stage two

**Stage two must contribute at least <InlineMath math="2\ln(2)\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{s}}" /> on its own, which rules out <InlineMath math="x=60\text{ kg}" /> because <InlineMath math="1.6 < 2" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
2\ln\!\left(\frac{100+x}{100}\right)\ge2\ln(2)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \frac{100+x}{100}\ge2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x\ge100\\
T_2=150\text{ kg}\ \Longrightarrow\ T_1=600-150=450\text{ kg}
\end{aligned}" />

As a check, with <InlineMath math="T_1=450\text{ kg}" /> and <InlineMath math="T_2=150\text{ kg}" />, stage one has ratio <InlineMath math="\tfrac{900}{450}=2" /> and stage two has ratio <InlineMath math="\tfrac{250}{100}=2.5" />, giving <InlineMath math="2\ln 10" />. Keeping <InlineMath math="x=60\text{ kg}" /> would give the trap <InlineMath math="540\text{ kg}" />. **Stage one receives <InlineMath math="450\text{ kg}" /> of fuel**.

# Answer

The first stage receives <InlineMath math="450\text{ kg}" /> of fuel.
