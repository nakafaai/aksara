# A line that touches twice

Lay a ruler across two bumps of a crumpled blanket and it rests on each bump at a single point. **A line <InlineMath math="y=mx+c" /> touches a downward parabola when line and parabola share exactly one point, so the quadratic for their meeting points has discriminant zero**. For the second hill write <InlineMath math="u=x-10" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
4-\tfrac14x^2=mx+c\ &\Longleftrightarrow\ \tfrac14x^2+mx+c-4=0,\qquad c=m^2+4\\
9-\tfrac14u^2=mu+10m+c\ &\Longleftrightarrow\ \tfrac14u^2+mu+10m+c-9=0,\qquad c=m^2-10m+9
\end{aligned}" />

# One line for both hills

**Both conditions describe the same rope, so the two expressions for <InlineMath math="c" /> are equal and the squares cancel**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m^2+4&=m^2-10m+9\\
m&=0.5,\qquad c=4.25\\
x_1&=-2m=-1,\qquad x_2=10-2m=9
\end{aligned}" />

The rope touches at <InlineMath math="(-1\mid3.75)" /> and <InlineMath math="(9\mid8.75)" />.

# The widest gap sits in the valley

Between the contact points the gap <InlineMath math="d(x)" /> is rope minus <InlineMath math="f" /> up to <InlineMath math="x=4" /> and rope minus <InlineMath math="g" /> after it. Each piece is an upward parabola, so on its stretch it is largest at an end. **The ends are the contact points, where <InlineMath math="d=0" />, and the valley at <InlineMath math="x=4" />, where both hills meet the ground**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
f(4)&=g(4)=0\\
d(4)&=0.5\cdot4+4.25-0=6.25
\end{aligned}" />
