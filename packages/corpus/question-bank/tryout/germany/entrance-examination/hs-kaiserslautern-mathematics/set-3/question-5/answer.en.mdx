# Given

**An open hollow cone** points straight down with top inner radius <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" /> and inner height <InlineMath math="24\text{ cm}" />. A solid metal sphere of radius <InlineMath math="4.5\text{ cm}" /> is dropped inside first, followed by a second sphere of radius <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> lowered centrally on top.

# Asked

What is the shortest distance between the surfaces of the two spheres in centimetres?

# Solution

> Check **where each sphere actually stops**: the small sphere wedges against the slanted wall inside a clean <InlineMath math="3" />-<InlineMath math="4" />-<InlineMath math="5" /> triangle, while the huge sphere is too wide and catches right on the circular top rim.

## How the small sphere wedges inside

Drop a marble into a paper cone: it stops when its sides press against the slanted wall, well above the pointed tip. **In the axial cross-section, the wall makes a half-angle <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> with the vertical axis, fixed by a <InlineMath math="3" />-<InlineMath math="4" />-<InlineMath math="5" /> right triangle**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
s=\sqrt{18^2+24^2}=30\text{ cm},\qquad \sin\alpha=\tfrac{18}{30}=\tfrac35,\qquad \cos\alpha=\tfrac{24}{30}=\tfrac45
\end{aligned}" />

The radius to the contact point stands perpendicular to the wall, so <InlineMath math="y_1\sin\alpha=r_1" />. **We also check that the contact height <InlineMath math="y_{B,1}=y_1\cos^2\alpha" /> actually lies below the <InlineMath math="24\text{ cm}" /> rim**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y_1=\frac{r_1}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{4.5}{0.6}=7.5\text{ cm},\qquad y_{B,1}=y_1\cos^2\alpha=7.5\cdot\tfrac{16}{25}=4.8\text{ cm}<24\text{ cm}\\
y_{\text{top},1}=7.5+4.5=12\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

The small sphere wedges cleanly inside the funnel, and its top sits at <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> above the tip (not <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm}" /> as if it touched the tip).

## Why the large sphere misses the cone wall

**Apply the same wall formula to the <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> sphere and the contact check fails: the tangent point would sit at <InlineMath math="32\text{ cm}" />, well above the <InlineMath math="24\text{ cm}" /> rim where there is no wall left**. So the large sphere slides further down until it catches on the circular rim of radius <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y_{2,\text{wall}}=\frac{30}{0.6}=50\text{ cm}\ \Longrightarrow\ y_{B,2}=50\cdot\tfrac{16}{25}=32\text{ cm}>24\text{ cm}\\
y_2=24+\sqrt{30^2-18^2}=24+24=48\text{ cm},\qquad y_{\text{bottom},2}=48-30=18\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

Because the large sphere's lowest point sits at <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" />, above the small sphere's top at <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" />, the two spheres never collide and the large sphere truly rests on the rim. **The shortest distance between their surfaces along the axis is <InlineMath math="18-12=6\text{ cm}" />**. Using the infinite-wall formula without checking the rim gives the trap value <InlineMath math="20-12=8\text{ cm}" />.

# Answer

The shortest distance between the two sphere surfaces is **<InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" />**.
