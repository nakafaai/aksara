# Given

**A filmed basketball shot** follows a parabola with five measured heights at <InlineMath math="x=0,1,2,3,4\text{ m}" />: <InlineMath math="1.85, 3.25, 3.85, 4.25, 4.25\text{ m}" />. Exactly one reading is wrong while the other four are exact.

# Asked

What is the greatest height above the ground reached by the ball's centre, in metres?

# Solution

> On equally spaced steps along any parabola, **the change of the change (second difference) must stay completely constant**, which immediately exposes the one faulty reading like a bent rung on a ladder.

## Even steps give a parabola away

Walk down a staircase where every step is shorter than the last by one fixed amount: that is how a parabola changes when <InlineMath math="x" /> moves in equal steps. **For <InlineMath math="y(x)" /> the first differences of the heights change by one fixed number, the second difference <InlineMath math="2a" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\Delta y&:\quad 1.40,\ 0.60,\ 0.40,\ 0.00\\
\Delta^2 y&:\quad -0.80,\ -0.20,\ -0.40
\end{aligned}" />

Those second differences do not match, which immediately reveals the wrong reading.

## Tracking down the wrong reading

A height that is off by <InlineMath math="\delta" /> shifts each second difference it belongs to: by <InlineMath math="-2\delta" /> when it sits in the middle and by <InlineMath math="+\delta" /> when it sits at an end. **Only a mistake at <InlineMath math="x=1" /> leaves the last difference <InlineMath math="-0.40" /> intact while shifting the first two by <InlineMath math="-2\delta" /> and <InlineMath math="+\delta" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
-0.80&=-0.40-2\delta\\
-0.20&=-0.40+\delta\\
\delta&=0.20
\end{aligned}" />

Both equations agree on <InlineMath math="\delta=0.20" />, so the true height at <InlineMath math="x=1" /> is <InlineMath math="3.25-0.20=3.05\text{ m}" />. An error at <InlineMath math="x=0" /> would touch only the first difference and one at <InlineMath math="x=4" /> only the last, while at <InlineMath math="x=2" /> or <InlineMath math="x=3" /> the untouched differences contradict each other.

## The highest point

The heights at <InlineMath math="x=3" /> and <InlineMath math="x=4" /> are equal and both exact, so the apex sits halfway between them at <InlineMath math="x=3.5" />. **The second difference <InlineMath math="2a=-0.40" /> gives <InlineMath math="a=-0.2" />, and substituting <InlineMath math="(3\mid4.25)" /> fixes the top**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
y&=-0.2\,(x-3.5)^2+H\\
4.25&=-0.2\cdot0.25+H\\
H&=4.30
\end{aligned}" />

The ball's centre climbs to **<InlineMath math="4.3\text{ m}" />**. Fitting a parabola through the first three readings, including the wrong one, peaks at <InlineMath math="3.875\text{ m}" /> near <InlineMath math="x=2.25" /> and already misses the reading at <InlineMath math="x=3" />.

# Answer

The ball reaches a maximum height of <InlineMath math="4.3\text{ m}" />.
