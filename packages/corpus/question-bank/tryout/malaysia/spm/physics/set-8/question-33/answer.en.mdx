# The force acts on the length of bar between the rails, and friction takes its share before the bar accelerates

A current across a magnetic field feels the force <InlineMath math="F=BIL" />, where <InlineMath math="L" /> is the length of conductor **in the field carrying the current**: the <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ m}" /> of bar between the rails, not the length of the rails. Friction opposes the motion, so only the **resultant**, <InlineMath math="F-2.0\text{ N}" />, accelerates the bar, whose mass must be in kilograms. The rest is uniform acceleration from rest over <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m}" />.

The field pushes the bar with <InlineMath math="10\text{ N}" />. After friction, the bar accelerates at <InlineMath math="400\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, leaves the rails at about <InlineMath math="35\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> and spends about <InlineMath math="0.087\text{ s}" /> on them.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="39\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> **ignores the friction**, so all <InlineMath math="10\text{ N}" /> seems to accelerate the bar.
- <InlineMath math="150\text{ N}" /> uses the **length of the rails** in <InlineMath math="BIL" />, but only the bar between the rails carries current across the field.
- <InlineMath math="1.1\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> uses the mass as **<InlineMath math="20\text{ kg}" />** instead of <InlineMath math="0.020\text{ kg}" />.
