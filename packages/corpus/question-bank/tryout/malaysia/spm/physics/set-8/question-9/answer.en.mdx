# A moon inside the synchronous orbit outruns the ground, so it rises in the west

No value of <InlineMath math="G" /> or of Mars's mass is given, but the surface data give <InlineMath math="GM=gR^2" />. Kepler's law, <InlineMath math="T^2=\dfrac{4\pi^2r^3}{GM}" />, then gives the radius at which a satellite keeps pace with the turning planet. Phobos orbits **well inside** that radius, so it goes round **faster than Mars turns**. Seen from the ground it overtakes the observer from west to east: it rises in the west, and it comes round again only when it has gained one full turn on the ground.

A synchronous satellite would orbit <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^{7}\text{ m}" /> from the centre of Mars. Phobos takes <InlineMath math="7.7\text{ h}" />, so it laps the turning ground, rising in the west about every <InlineMath math="11\text{ h}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- **Every <InlineMath math="7.7\text{ h}" />** ignores that the observer is turning too, so Phobos needs longer than one orbit to come round again.
- **Every <InlineMath math="5.8\text{ h}" />** **adds** the two rates, as if Phobos and the ground moved in opposite directions.
- **In the east** is right for our Moon, which orbits slower than the Earth turns, but Phobos orbits faster than Mars turns.
