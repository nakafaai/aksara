# Kinetic energy becomes electrical energy, and the battery's voltage turns that energy into charge

Only part of the car's kinetic energy, <InlineMath math="\tfrac12mv^2" />, comes back: the rest is lost as heat. Electrical energy delivered at a steady voltage is **charge times voltage**, <InlineMath math="E=QV" />, so the charge pushed into the battery is the energy divided by <InlineMath math="400\text{ V}" />. Spreading that charge over the <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ s}" /> stop gives the average current, <InlineMath math="I=\dfrac{Q}{t}" />.

The battery gets back <InlineMath math="180\text{ kJ}" />, which is <InlineMath math="450\text{ C}" /> of charge at <InlineMath math="400\text{ V}" />, delivered at an average of <InlineMath math="90\text{ A}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="300\text{ kJ}" /> returns **all** the kinetic energy, ignoring that only <InlineMath math="60\%" /> comes back.
- <InlineMath math="2250\text{ A}" /> **multiplies** the charge by the time instead of dividing.
- <InlineMath math="9.0\text{ kJ}" /> uses <InlineMath math="\tfrac12mv" />, **forgetting to square the speed**, and the slip carries into the charge and the current.
