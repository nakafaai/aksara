# Pascal's principle carries the pressure, but a column of oil adds its own weight on the way down

Pressure applied to the oil is passed on everywhere, but the oil also has weight. The small piston is <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ m}" /> **below** the load piston, so the pressure there is larger by <InlineMath math="\rho gh" />. The oil is incompressible, so the volume pushed in by the small piston equals the volume that lifts the load. The work done includes lifting the load and also **lifting that volume of oil** the <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ m}" /> up to the load cylinder.

About <InlineMath math="120\text{ N}" /> on the small piston holds the load. Pushing it <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ m}" /> raises the load <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />, and that takes about <InlineMath math="240\text{ J}" />: <InlineMath math="200\text{ J}" /> for the load and <InlineMath math="36\text{ J}" /> to lift the oil to the upper floor.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="100\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="200\text{ J}" /> **ignore the <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ m}" /> column of oil**, as if both pistons were at the same height.
- <InlineMath math="82\text{ N}" /> **subtracts** the oil's pressure, as if the small piston were above the load piston.
- <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ mm}" /> divides the load's rise by the area ratio **the wrong way**: the small piston must move further, not less.
