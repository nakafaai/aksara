# At the equator part of gravity is used up keeping things on the circle

A visitor at the equator moves in a circle once every day, which needs a **centripetal force** towards the centre. Gravity supplies it, so the scale only has to push up with **what is left over**: <InlineMath math="mg-m\dfrac{4\pi^2R}{T^2}" />. At a pole the visitor just turns on the spot and the scale reads the full <InlineMath math="mg" />. If the day were short enough, gravity would be used up entirely and the scale would read zero: the visitor would be in orbit at ground level.

The scale reads about <InlineMath math="380\text{ N}" />, about <InlineMath math="21\%" /> less than the <InlineMath math="480\text{ N}" /> at a pole. With a day of about <InlineMath math="1.4\text{ h}" /> the visitor would float.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="580\text{ N}" /> **adds** the centripetal acceleration, but the circular motion uses up part of gravity, so the scale pushes less.
- <InlineMath math="480\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="0\%" /> assume the spin **does not affect** the reading, but the visitor at the equator is moving in a circle.
- <InlineMath math="0.22\text{ h}" /> solves <InlineMath math="T=\sqrt{R/g}" />, **forgetting the <InlineMath math="2\pi" />** from the circumference.
