# What holds the roof down

A saucepan lid stays on because of its own weight and, on a pressure cooker, a clamp as well. The roof is held down the same two ways, by its weight of <InlineMath math="12\text{ kN}" /> and by fixings worth at most <InlineMath math="30\text{ kN}" />, <InlineMath math="42\text{ kN}" /> in all. Spread over <InlineMath math="8.0\times10=80\text{ m}^2" />, that is <InlineMath math="42000/80" /> pascals. **The roof lifts once the air underneath pushes <InlineMath math="525\text{ Pa}" /> harder than the air on top**. Leaving out the weight gives <InlineMath math="375\text{ Pa}" />, as if only the fixings held the roof.

# Fast air over the top

Hold a strip of paper under your lower lip and blow across its top, and the strip rises, because fast-moving air presses less. Over the roof the air moves at <InlineMath math="1.5u" />, so with the windows shut the roof feels a pressure difference of <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\rho\,(1.5u)^2" />. Setting that equal to <InlineMath math="525\text{ Pa}" /> gives <InlineMath math="u=19.7\text{ m s}^{-1}" />, and multiplying by <InlineMath math="3.6" /> turns it into kilometres per hour. **With every window shut, the roof lifts in a wind of <InlineMath math="71.0\text{ km h}^{-1}" />**. Using the wind speed itself for the air over the roof gives <InlineMath math="106\text{ km h}^{-1}" />, because it skips the speeding up over the roof edge.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\tfrac12\rho\,(1.5u)^2&=525\ \Rightarrow\ u^2=\frac{525}{0.6\times2.25}=389\ \Rightarrow\ u=19.7\text{ m s}^{-1}=71.0\text{ km h}^{-1}\\
\tfrac12\rho u^2\,(2.25+1)&=525\ \Rightarrow\ u^2=\frac{525}{0.6\times3.25}=269\ \Rightarrow\ u=16.4\text{ m s}^{-1}=59.1\text{ km h}^{-1}
\end{aligned}" />

# A broken window pushes from inside

Blow into a paper bag through its open mouth and it puffs out. The broken window does that to the workshop. Wind brought to rest inside raises the inside pressure by <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\rho u^2" />, while the air over the roof still pulls <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\rho\,(1.5u)^2" /> off the top. The two now add up, <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\rho u^2\,(2.25+1)=525" />, so <InlineMath math="u=16.4\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. **With the window facing the wind broken, the roof lifts at <InlineMath math="59.1\text{ km h}^{-1}" />**. Thinking that the broken window lets the wind suck air out of the workshop, so that the inside pressure falls, gives <InlineMath math="95.2\text{ km h}^{-1}" />, and that is exactly backwards for a window that faces the wind.
