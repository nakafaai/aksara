# Two emfs face each other, so only their difference drives the current, and charging pushes the battery's terminal voltage above its emf

With positive joined to positive, the two emfs **oppose** each other round the loop: only their **difference**, <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ V}" />, drives current, through both internal resistances in series. The current flows **into** the battery's positive terminal, the reverse of discharging, so the voltage across its internal resistance now **adds** to its emf. Of the charger's emf power, the part stored is the battery's emf times the current.

A current of <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ A}" /> flows, and the battery's terminals sit at <InlineMath math="12.5\text{ V}" /> while it charges, above its own emf. Of the charger's <InlineMath math="75\text{ W}" />, <InlineMath math="60\text{ W}" />, or <InlineMath math="80\%" />, is stored, and the rest heats the two internal resistances.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="11.5\text{ V}" /> **subtracts** the internal voltage as when the battery is supplying current, but here the current is driven into it.
- <InlineMath math="45\text{ A}" /> **adds** the two emfs, as if they were connected the same way round, positive to negative.
- <InlineMath math="83\%" /> compares the battery's **terminal voltage** with the charger's emf, counting the heat in the battery's own resistance as stored.
