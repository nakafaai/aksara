# Spring energy pays for the climb and the friction along the whole path, including while the spring is still pushing

The spring stores <InlineMath math="\tfrac12kx^2" />. Even while the spring is pushing, the ball is **already climbing and rubbing** over those <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ cm}" />. After that it slows under the slope's pull, <InlineMath math="mg\sin10°" />, plus the <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ N}" /> resistance. For the least pull-back, the energy stored must just cover the climb and friction over <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ m}" /> **plus the pull-back distance itself**, which gives a quadratic in <InlineMath math="x" />.

The ball leaves the spring at about <InlineMath math="2.8\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> and reaches the bumper at about <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. A pull-back of about <InlineMath math="6.7\text{ cm}" /> would just get it there.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.6\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> and <InlineMath math="6.5\text{ cm}" /> ignore the **climb and friction while the spring is pushing**, as if the ball started from the spring's end.
- <InlineMath math="1.9\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> and <InlineMath math="5.9\text{ cm}" /> **ignore the resistive force**, leaving only the climb to slow the ball.
- **Stopping before the bumper** uses the ball's **whole weight** against the motion instead of its component along the slope, <InlineMath math="mg\sin10°" />.
