# Friction can push either way, and a falling mass pulls with less than its weight

Down the slope the block feels <InlineMath math="mg\sin30°=20\text{ N}" />. At rest, friction supplies whatever is needed **up or down the slope**, so the rope's pull may lie anywhere from <InlineMath math="20-8.0" /> to <InlineMath math="20+8.0\text{ N}" />, and at rest the rope's pull is the hanging weight. Once the block slides up, the hanging mass **accelerates downwards**, so the rope pulls on it with less than its weight: <InlineMath math="T=m(g-a)" />. That is what lets a hanging mass heavier than <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ kg}" /> move the block without breaking the rope.

<BlockMath math="\text{at rest: }12\text{ N}\le mg\le28\text{ N}\Rightarrow 1.2\text{ kg}\le m\le2.8\text{ kg}" />

<BlockMath math="\text{sliding up: }a=\frac{10m-20-6.0}{m+4.0},\qquad T=m(10-a)=\frac{66m}{m+4.0}" />

<BlockMath math="\frac{66m}{m+4.0}\le30\Rightarrow36m\le120\Rightarrow m\le3.3\text{ kg}" />

The block rests for any hanging mass from <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ kg}" /> to <InlineMath math="2.8\text{ kg}" />. Heavier masses drag it up, and the rope survives up to about <InlineMath math="3.3\text{ kg}" />, because the accelerating mass pulls with less than its own weight.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ kg}" /> lets friction act **only up the slope**, but it also resists the block being dragged upwards.
- <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ kg}" /> sets the rope's tension **equal to the hanging weight**, which is true only when nothing accelerates.
- <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ kg}" /> uses the **<InlineMath math="8.0\text{ N}" /> limit of static friction** while the block is sliding, when friction is <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ N}" />.
