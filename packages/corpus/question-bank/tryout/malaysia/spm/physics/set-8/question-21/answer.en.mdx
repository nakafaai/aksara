# Part of the push presses into the ramp

Push a heavy suitcase up a sloping driveway with your arms level, and you feel some of your effort squash it against the ground instead of moving it uphill. The worker's horizontal push <InlineMath math="P" /> splits the same way. The part <InlineMath math="P\cos30^\circ" /> acts up the ramp, and the part <InlineMath math="P\sin30^\circ" /> presses the crate into the ramp. So the normal force is <InlineMath math="mg\cos30^\circ+P\sin30^\circ" />, not just <InlineMath math="433\text{ N}" />, and friction is <InlineMath math="0.40" /> times that. At a steady speed the forces along the ramp balance. **The push that keeps the crate sliding steadily up is <InlineMath math="635\text{ N}" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P\cos30^\circ&=mg\sin30^\circ+0.40\,(mg\cos30^\circ+P\sin30^\circ)\\
0.866P-0.200P&=250+173.2\ \Rightarrow\ P=\frac{423.2}{0.666}=635\text{ N}\\
f&=0.40\,(433+0.500\times635)=0.40\times751=300\text{ N}
\end{aligned}" />

Leaving the normal force at <InlineMath math="mg\cos30^\circ" /> gives only <InlineMath math="489\text{ N}" />, because it forgets that the push itself makes the crate rub harder. Turning <InlineMath math="P\sin30^\circ" /> the other way, as if the push lifted the crate off the ramp, gives <InlineMath math="397\text{ N}" />.

# Friction grows with the push

Press a book onto a table with your palm and it gets much harder to slide. Here the push adds <InlineMath math="0.500\times635=318\text{ N}" /> to the <InlineMath math="433\text{ N}" /> that the crate's weight already presses into the ramp, so the normal force is <InlineMath math="751\text{ N}" />. **Friction on the moving crate is <InlineMath math="0.40\times751=300\text{ N}" />**. That is far more than the <InlineMath math="173\text{ N}" /> the crate would feel if the worker pushed parallel to the ramp.

# When pushing harder stops helping

Push a toy car straight down onto the floor and it never rolls, however hard you press. On a steeper ramp, a horizontal push points more into the ramp and less along it. Each extra newton of push gives <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> newton of drive up the ramp but adds <InlineMath math="0.40\sin\theta" /> newton of friction against it. Once <InlineMath math="0.40\sin\theta" /> catches up with <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" />, a bigger push gains nothing, and that happens when <InlineMath math="\tan\theta=1/0.40=2.5" />. **Above <InlineMath math="68.2^\circ" />, no horizontal push can move the crate up the ramp**. The angle <InlineMath math="21.8^\circ" />, from <InlineMath math="\tan\theta=0.40" />, is a different limit. It is the slope at which the crate would start to slide down by itself with no push at all.
