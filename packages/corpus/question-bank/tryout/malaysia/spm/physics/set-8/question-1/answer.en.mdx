# Put the zero error back first

A bathroom scale that shows <InlineMath math="-1\text{ kg}" /> with nobody on it weighs everyone <InlineMath math="1\text{ kg}" /> too light, so you add that kilogram back. The closed calipers show <InlineMath math="-0.02\text{ cm}" />, so every reading is <InlineMath math="0.02\text{ cm}" /> too small. **The coil is really <InlineMath math="2.86+0.02=2.88\text{ cm}" /> long**. The <InlineMath math="40" /> turns lie side by side, and each turn adds one wire's thickness, so the diameter is <InlineMath math="2.88/40=0.0720\text{ cm}" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.720\text{ mm}" />. Subtracting the <InlineMath math="0.02\text{ cm}" /> instead gives <InlineMath math="2.84\text{ cm}" /> and a wire of <InlineMath math="0.710\text{ mm}" />, too thin.

# From resistance to resistivity

Water flows more easily through a fat hose than a thin one, and what counts is the size of the hole. The wire's cross-section is a circle whose radius is half the diameter, <InlineMath math="0.360\text{ mm}" />. **Its area is <InlineMath math="A=\pi r^2=3.14\times(0.360\times10^{-3})^2=4.07\times10^{-7}\text{ m}^2" />**. Resistivity is the resistance the metal would have as a piece <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> long with <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}^2" /> of cross-section, so <InlineMath math="\rho=RA/L=2.4\times4.07\times10^{-7}/2.00=4.9\times10^{-7}\,\Omega\text{ m}" />. Putting the diameter in place of the radius makes the area four times too big and gives <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^{-6}\,\Omega\text{ m}" />.

# More turns shrink the error

To find the thickness of one sheet of paper you measure a thick stack, because the ruler's small error is then shared by many sheets. The coil works the same way. The <InlineMath math="\pm0.01\text{ cm}" /> is spread over <InlineMath math="N" /> turns, so the diameter is uncertain by <InlineMath math="0.01/(0.0720N)" /> of itself. **The diameter is squared in the area, so its percentage uncertainty counts twice in the resistivity**. With <InlineMath math="40" /> turns that is <InlineMath math="2\times0.01/2.88=0.69\%" />, more than allowed. Asking for <InlineMath math="2\times0.01/(0.0720N)\le0.0050" /> gives <InlineMath math="N\ge55.6" />. Turns come whole, and <InlineMath math="55" /> turns still give <InlineMath math="0.505\%" />, so the student needs <InlineMath math="56" />. Forgetting the factor of two halves the answer to <InlineMath math="28" /> turns.
