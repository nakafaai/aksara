# Alpha decay takes two protons and two neutrons

Snap four beads off a necklace of <InlineMath math="241" /> beads, two of them red, and the necklace keeps <InlineMath math="237" /> beads with two fewer red ones. An alpha particle is a helium nucleus, so the mass number drops by <InlineMath math="4" /> and the atomic number by <InlineMath math="2" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{241}_{95}\text{Am}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{237}_{93}\text{Np}+{}^{4}_{2}\text{He}
\end{aligned}" />

**Americium-241 decays into neptunium-237**. Writing curium-241 uses the beta-minus rule, in which a neutron turns into a proton, so the mass number stays and the atomic number rises.

# A short gap stops the count halfway

A bowling ball rolling down a long alley keeps knocking pins over until it runs out of energy. Cut the alley short and the ball hits the back wall with energy to spare, and those pins are never knocked. An alpha particle spends its <InlineMath math="5.5\text{ MeV}" /> evenly over <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ cm}" /> of air, so a <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}" /> gap takes only half of it before the far plate swallows the rest. Half of the <InlineMath math="37\,000" /> decays a second send their alpha across.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
E&=5.5\text{ MeV}\times\frac{2.0}{4.0}=2.75\text{ MeV}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{2.75\times10^{6}}{35}=7.86\times10^{4}\text{ ion pairs}\\
I&=18\,500\times7.86\times10^{4}\times1.6\times10^{-19}=2.33\times10^{-10}\text{ A}
\end{aligned}" />

**With the plates <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}" /> apart, <InlineMath math="2.33\times10^{-10}\text{ A}" /> flows**. If you count the whole <InlineMath math="5.5\text{ MeV}" /> here, you get <InlineMath math="4.65\times10^{-10}\text{ A}" />, as if the alpha particle could keep ionising inside the metal plate.

# Beyond the range the extra air adds nothing

Give the bowling ball an alley longer than it can roll, and it stops on its own, the same number of pins down whether the alley is <InlineMath math="20" /> or <InlineMath math="30" /> metres long. With the plates <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ cm}" /> apart, every alpha particle stops after <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ cm}" /> and leaves all its energy in the air, <InlineMath math="1.57\times10^{5}" /> ion pairs each.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
I&=18\,500\times\frac{5.5\times10^{6}}{35}\times1.6\times10^{-19}=4.65\times10^{-10}\text{ A}
\end{aligned}" />

**At <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ cm}" /> the current is <InlineMath math="4.65\times10^{-10}\text{ A}" />**, exactly double the narrow gap, not two and a half times. Scaling the <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}" /> current up by <InlineMath math="5.0/2.0" /> would ask each alpha particle for <InlineMath math="6.9\text{ MeV}" />, more energy than it ever had, and that is where <InlineMath math="5.81\times10^{-10}\text{ A}" /> comes from.
