# Alpha decay removes two protons and two neutrons, and <InlineMath math="864" /> years is two half-lives

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus, <InlineMath math="{}^{4}_{2}\text{He}" />, so alpha decay lowers the nucleon number by <InlineMath math="4" /> and the proton number by <InlineMath math="2" />, which **changes the element**. Activity halves every half-life, and <InlineMath math="864" /> years is **two** half-lives. Alpha particles are the most strongly **ionising** radiation, so even a weak source makes a measurable current, and their **short range** means smoke particles readily stop them while they cannot escape the detector's case.

Americium-241 decays into neptunium-237, which has <InlineMath math="93" /> protons. After <InlineMath math="864" /> years the activity is about <InlineMath math="9.3\text{ kBq}" />. Alpha is chosen because it ionises air strongly, giving a clear current, yet is stopped within a few centimetres, so smoke blocks it and it stays inside the detector.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="18.5\text{ kBq}" /> halves the activity only **once**, but <InlineMath math="864" /> years is two half-lives.
- **Passing easily through smoke** has it backwards: the detector works because smoke **blocks** the alpha particles.
- **Americium-237** lowers the nucleon number but **keeps the proton number**, yet losing two protons makes a new element.
