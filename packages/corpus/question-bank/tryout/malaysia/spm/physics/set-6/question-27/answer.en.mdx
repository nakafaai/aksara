# Use the absolute pressure at depth, then Boyle’s law on the way up

At <InlineMath math="30" /> m the water adds <InlineMath math="1030 \times 9.8 \times 30 = 303" /> kPa to the atmosphere, so the air in her lungs is at <InlineMath math="403" /> kPa, the **absolute** pressure. At the surface it would try to fill <InlineMath math="6.0 \times 403 \div 100 = 24" /> litres.

It doubles to <InlineMath math="12" /> litres once the pressure has halved to <InlineMath math="201" /> kPa, which leaves <InlineMath math="101" /> kPa of water above her, a depth of <InlineMath math="10" /> m. The lungs are at risk **long before the surface**.

**Breathing out on the way up**: at the surface the air would fill <InlineMath math="24" /> litres, so keeping only <InlineMath math="6.0" /> litres means keeping a quarter of the air. She must breathe out the other <InlineMath math="75\%" />, the share set by the pressure ratio <InlineMath math="100 \div 403" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> and <InlineMath math="67\%" /> use only the water’s pressure and forget the **atmosphere** above it.
- <InlineMath math="25\%" /> is the share she **keeps**, not the share she breathes out.
- <InlineMath math="50\%" /> stops at the depth where the air has doubled, but it keeps **expanding** all the way up.
