# Set the whole drop’s energy equal to the energy in the stretched cord

At the lowest point the jumper is at rest, so the gravitational energy lost over the **whole drop**, <InlineMath math="15 + x" /> metres, equals the energy in the cord: <InlineMath math="686(15 + x) = 200x^2" />.

Solving <InlineMath math="200x^2 - 686x - 10\,290 = 0" /> gives <InlineMath math="x = 9.1" /> m, so the lowest point is <InlineMath math="15 + 9.1 = 24" /> m down. There the cord pulls with <InlineMath math="400 \times 9.1 = 3.6" /> kN, more than **five times** the jumper’s weight.

**The fastest moment**: the jumper keeps speeding up after the cord goes taut, until the cord’s pull equals the weight, at <InlineMath math="x = 686 \div 400 = 1.7" /> m. There <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = 686 \times 16.7 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 400 \times 1.7^2 = 1.09 \times 10^4" /> J, so <InlineMath math="v = 17.6" /> m/s, **faster** than the <InlineMath math="17.1" /> m/s at the moment the cord goes taut.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="17\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> is the speed when the cord **goes taut**, but the jumper still speeds up until the pull equals the weight.
- <InlineMath math="4.3\text{ kN}" /> adds the weight to the cord’s pull, but the question asks only for the **cord’s force**.
- <InlineMath math="0.69\text{ kN}" /> is the pull with the jumper **hanging at rest**, not at the bottom of the fall.
