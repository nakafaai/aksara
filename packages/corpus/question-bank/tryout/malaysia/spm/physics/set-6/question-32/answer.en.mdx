# Add up the length of wire in the field, then use the force on a wire and Newton’s second law

In a radial field every piece of wire is at right angles to the field, and the forces all point **along the axis**, so they add. The wire in the field is <InlineMath math="100 \times 2\pi \times 0.020 = 12.6" /> m long, and <InlineMath math="F = BIL = 0.50 \times 0.20 \times 12.6 = 1.3" /> N.

With the mass in kilograms, <InlineMath math="a = 1.26 \div 0.010 = 130\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, about thirteen times <InlineMath math="g" />, which lets the cone follow rapid changes in the current.

**Holding the cone out**: the rim pushes back with <InlineMath math="2000 \times 0.0010 = 2.0" /> N at <InlineMath math="1.0" /> mm, so the coil must give <InlineMath math="2.0" /> N, which needs <InlineMath math="I = 2.0 \div (0.50 \times 12.6) = 0.32" /> A, using the **same length of wire**.

**Switching on suddenly**: the <InlineMath math="1.26" /> N force would hold the cone at <InlineMath math="1.26 \div 2000 = 0.63" /> mm, but the cone arrives there moving and overshoots. With no damping the work done by the force equals the rim’s stored energy at the far point, <InlineMath math="Fx = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2" />, so <InlineMath math="x = 2F \div k = 1.3" /> mm, **twice** the resting push.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="32\text{ A}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.013\text{ mm}" /> count the wire of **one turn**, not all one hundred.
- <InlineMath math="0.16\text{ A}" /> takes the rim’s force as <InlineMath math="1.0" /> N, slipping the **millimetre** to a different power of ten.
- <InlineMath math="0.63\text{ mm}" /> is where the cone would **rest**, not how far it swings.
