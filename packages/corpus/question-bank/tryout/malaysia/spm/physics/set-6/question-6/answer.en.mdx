# Find the speed from the Earth fall, then let the Moon’s weaker gravity act on it

Falling <InlineMath math="1.5" /> m on the Earth gives <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 1.5} = 5.48" /> m/s. On the Moon <InlineMath math="g = 1.67\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, so the ball rises <InlineMath math="5.48^2 \div (2 \times 1.67) = 9.0" /> m, **six times** the Earth drop.

It takes <InlineMath math="5.48 \div 1.67 = 3.3" /> s to stop at the top and the same again to fall back, so it returns after <InlineMath math="6.6" /> s, the **whole round trip**.

**Down to the ground**: the ball passes the hand at <InlineMath math="5.48" /> m/s going down, then falls <InlineMath math="1.8" /> m more: <InlineMath math="v^2 = 30 + 2 \times 1.67 \times 1.8 = 36" />, so <InlineMath math="v = 6.0" /> m/s, **only a little faster** in the weak gravity.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.1\text{ s}" /> and <InlineMath math="8.1\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> use the **Earth’s** gravity, not the Moon’s.
- <InlineMath math="3.3\text{ s}" /> is only the time to the **top**, not back to the hand.
- <InlineMath math="5.5\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> stops at the hand and forgets the extra <InlineMath math="1.8" /> m of **falling** to the ground.
