# Take out the decay first, then the dilution gives the volume

In <InlineMath math="15" /> hours, one half-life, the whole dose has fallen to <InlineMath math="4000" /> counts per second, **wherever it is**. Spread through the blood it gives <InlineMath math="0.80" /> per cubic centimetre, so the blood fills <InlineMath math="4000 \div 0.80 = 5000" /> cubic centimetres, <InlineMath math="5.0" /> litres.

Thirty hours later is two more half-lives, so a sample then gives a quarter as much: <InlineMath math="0.80 \div 4 = 0.20" /> counts per second.

**If some sodium had left**: only <InlineMath math="0.90 \times 4000 = 3600" /> counts per second would be spread through the blood, so the same <InlineMath math="0.80" /> per cubic centimetre would mean <InlineMath math="3600 \div 0.80 = 4500" /> cubic centimetres, <InlineMath math="4.5" /> litres. Ignoring the leak **overstates** the volume.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="10\text{ L}" /> compares the dose before it decayed with the blood after, ignoring the **half-life** that passed.
- <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ L}" /> lets the dose decay **twice** in the fifteen hours.
- <InlineMath math="5.5\text{ L}" /> adds a tenth, but losing tracer leaves **less** to spread through the same readings.
