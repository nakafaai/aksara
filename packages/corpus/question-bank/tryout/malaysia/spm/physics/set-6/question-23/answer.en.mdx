# Compare the work put in with the useful work, then rebuild the push for the longer ramp

The push does <InlineMath math="300 \times 4.0 = 1200" /> J, but lifting the crate needs only <InlineMath math="784" /> J. The other <InlineMath math="416" /> J goes against friction over <InlineMath math="4.0" /> m, so the friction is <InlineMath math="104" /> N, even though the speed is **steady**.

On a <InlineMath math="5.0" /> m ramp, the share of the weight along the slope is <InlineMath math="784 \div 5.0 = 157" /> N. With the same friction the push must be <InlineMath math="157 + 104 = 261" /> N, **less** than before but not in proportion.

**Slipping back down**: the weight’s share along the ramp, <InlineMath math="784 \div 4.0 = 196" /> N, beats the friction of <InlineMath math="104" /> N, so the crate does slide. Of the <InlineMath math="784" /> J it loses, friction takes <InlineMath math="104 \times 4.0 = 416" /> J, leaving <InlineMath math="368" /> J, so <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{2 \times 368 \div 80} = 3.0" /> m/s.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="157\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="4.4\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> confuse a steady speed with no friction, but steady means the forces **balance**.
- <InlineMath math="457\text{ N}" /> takes the whole push as friction, forgetting the push also **lifts the crate**.
- <InlineMath math="240\text{ N}" /> scales the whole push by four fifths, but only the **weight share** changes with the ramp’s length.
