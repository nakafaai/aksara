# The light brings momentum at its power over c, and reflection doubles the push

The sail receives <InlineMath math="1360 \times 100 = 1.36 \times 10^5" /> W, and each joule of light carries <InlineMath math="1 \div c" /> of momentum, so <InlineMath math="4.5 \times 10^{-4}" /> kg m/s arrives each second. Reflected straight back, the light’s momentum **reverses**, so the force is twice that, <InlineMath math="9.1 \times 10^{-4}" /> N.

That gives the <InlineMath math="10" /> kg craft <InlineMath math="9.1 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, and in one day, <InlineMath math="86\,400" /> s, it gains <InlineMath math="7.8" /> m/s, tiny at first but growing **day after day**.

**The distance in a day**: from rest with a steady acceleration, <InlineMath math="s = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.1 \times 10^{-5} \times 86\,400^2 = 3.4 \times 10^5" /> m, about <InlineMath math="340" /> km, **half** of what the final speed alone would give.

**At the distance of Mars**: the sunlight spreads over <InlineMath math="1.5^2 = 2.25" /> times the area, so the push and the acceleration fall to <InlineMath math="9.07 \times 10^{-5} \div 2.25 = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. In a day the craft then covers <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 86\,400^2 = 1.5 \times 10^5" /> m, about <InlineMath math="150" /> km.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="3.9\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> and <InlineMath math="75\text{ km}" /> treat the sail as absorbing the light, but a mirror **reverses** the momentum.
- <InlineMath math="230\text{ km}" /> divides by <InlineMath math="1.5" />, but the sunlight thins out as the **square** of the distance.
- <InlineMath math="300\text{ km}" /> multiplies the final speed by the time, but the speed **grew from zero**.
