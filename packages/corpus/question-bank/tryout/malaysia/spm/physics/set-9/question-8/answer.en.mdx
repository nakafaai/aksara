# At the equator, gravity must also carry the explorer round

The day is <InlineMath math="7200" /> s, so the equator needs a centripetal acceleration of <InlineMath math="4\pi^2 \times 3.0 \times 10^6 \div 7200^2 = 2.28\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. Of the <InlineMath math="240" /> N pull of gravity, <InlineMath math="60 \times 2.28 = 137" /> N goes into that, and the scale reads the rest, <InlineMath math="103" /> N.

The explorer would float when the whole <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" /> is needed for the circle, at <InlineMath math="T = 2\pi\sqrt{3.0 \times 10^6 \div 4.0} = 5.4 \times 10^3" /> s, about <InlineMath math="1.5" /> hours, only a little **shorter** than the real day.

**Flying fast enough to float**: floating needs a speed round the planet of <InlineMath math="\sqrt{gR} = \sqrt{4.0 \times 3.0 \times 10^6} = 3.46" /> km/s. The ground at the equator **already moves** east at <InlineMath math="2\pi R \div T = 2.62" /> km/s, so the aircraft needs only <InlineMath math="0.85" /> km/s more.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="240\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="3.5\text{ km s}^{-1}" /> ignore the **spin** of the planet altogether.
- <InlineMath math="6.1\text{ km s}^{-1}" /> adds the ground’s speed, which would be right flying **westwards**, against the spin.
- <InlineMath math="3.5\text{ km s}^{-1}" /> is the speed needed over the planet’s centre, not **relative to the ground**, which already moves east.
