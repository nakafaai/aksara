# Divide the energy by c squared, then scale up to the Sun’s lifetime

Each second the Sun gives out <InlineMath math="3.8 \times 10^{26}" /> J, so it loses <InlineMath math="3.8 \times 10^{26} \div (3.0 \times 10^8)^2 = 4.2 \times 10^9" /> kg of mass every second.

Ten billion years is <InlineMath math="3.15 \times 10^{17}" /> s, so the total loss is <InlineMath math="1.3 \times 10^{27}" /> kg, a fraction <InlineMath math="1.3 \times 10^{27} \div 2.0 \times 10^{30} = 6.7 \times 10^{-4}" />: about <InlineMath math="0.067\%" />, a **tiny** share of the Sun.

**The hydrogen it burns**: the <InlineMath math="4.2 \times 10^9" /> kg that vanishes each second is only <InlineMath math="0.70\%" /> of the hydrogen fused, so the Sun fuses <InlineMath math="4.2 \times 10^9 \div 0.0070 = 6.0 \times 10^{11}" /> kg of hydrogen every second. Most of that mass **stays** as helium.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.9 \times 10^7\text{ kg}" /> multiplies by <InlineMath math="0.70\%" />, but the lost mass is the **small** part of the hydrogen fused.
- <InlineMath math="1.3 \times 10^{18}\text{ kg}" /> divides by <InlineMath math="c" /> instead of by <InlineMath math="c^2" />.
- <InlineMath math="4.2 \times 10^9\text{ kg}" /> twice assumes all the fused hydrogen **disappears**, but most of it becomes helium.
