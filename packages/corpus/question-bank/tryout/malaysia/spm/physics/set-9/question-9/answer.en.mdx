# The swing gives the field, and the field gives the mass

From the period, <InlineMath math="g = 4\pi^2 l \div T^2 = 4\pi^2 \times 0.80 \div 2.6^2 = 4.67" /> N/kg. Since <InlineMath math="g = GM \div R^2" />, the mass is <InlineMath math="4.67 \times (2.5 \times 10^6)^2 \div 6.67 \times 10^{-11} = 4.4 \times 10^{23}" /> kg.

At <InlineMath math="500" /> km up the distance from the centre is <InlineMath math="3.0 \times 10^6" /> m, and the field falls as the **square** of that distance: <InlineMath math="4.67 \times (2.5 \div 3.0)^2 = 3.2" /> N/kg.

**The satellite’s period**: in orbit the field supplies the centripetal force, <InlineMath math="v^2 \div r = 3.24" /> N/kg, so <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{3.24 \times 3.0 \times 10^6} = 3.12" /> km/s. One orbit takes <InlineMath math="2\pi \times 3.0 \times 10^6 \div 3120 = 6.0 \times 10^3" /> s, about <InlineMath math="1.7" /> hours.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.4" /> hours uses the **surface** field at the orbit’s radius, but the field there is weaker.
- <InlineMath math="1.5" /> hours uses the weaker field with the planet’s **radius**, not the orbit’s.
- <InlineMath math="1.1 \times 10^{23}\text{ kg}" /> drops the **2** from <InlineMath math="2\pi" /> in the pendulum’s period.
