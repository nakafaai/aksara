# Friction is the only push on the top block

The upper block is pushed only by friction, so it can accelerate at most <InlineMath math="6.0 \div 2.0 = 3.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. Moving together at that rate, both blocks need <InlineMath math="5.0 \times 3.0 = 15" /> N.

At <InlineMath math="24" /> N the blocks **slip**. The upper block still gets <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, while the lower gets <InlineMath math="(24 - 6.0) \div 3.0 = 6.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. The lower block draws ahead at <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" /> relative to the upper, so <InlineMath math="1.5 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.0 \times t^2" /> and <InlineMath math="t = 1.0" /> s.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ N}" /> takes the friction itself as the largest force, but the force must also speed up **both blocks**.
- <InlineMath math="0.71\text{ s}" /> uses the lower block’s <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" /> alone, but the upper block is **also speeding up**.
- Never sliding off assumes the blocks move together at <InlineMath math="24" /> N, which needs <InlineMath math="9.6" /> N of friction, **more** than the limit.
