# Subtract the two equations to remove the work function

For each frequency <InlineMath math="hf = W + eV" />. The work function is the **same** both times, so subtracting gives <InlineMath math="h \times 2.0 \times 10^{14} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.82" />, and <InlineMath math="h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}" /> J s.

At <InlineMath math="5.5 \times 10^{14}" /> Hz a photon carries <InlineMath math="hf = 3.61 \times 10^{-19}" /> J, or <InlineMath math="2.26" /> eV. Taking away the <InlineMath math="0.50" /> eV left as kinetic energy gives <InlineMath math="W = 1.8" /> eV.

**The longest wavelength**: a photon frees an electron only if it carries at least the work function, <InlineMath math="1.76" /> eV or <InlineMath math="2.81 \times 10^{-19}" /> J. The longest wavelength is <InlineMath math="\lambda = hc \div W = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8 \div 2.81 \times 10^{-19} = 7.0 \times 10^{-7}" /> m, about <InlineMath math="700" /> nm, at the **red** end of the spectrum.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.5\ \mu\text{m}" /> takes the electrons’ <InlineMath math="0.50" /> eV of **kinetic energy** as the work function.
- <InlineMath math="4.1 \times 10^{-15}" /> is <InlineMath math="h" /> in **electronvolt seconds**, not joule seconds: it leaves out the factor <InlineMath math="e" />.
- <InlineMath math="550\text{ nm}" /> uses the photon’s **whole energy** at the lower frequency, not the work function.
