# The induced current produces a force that opposes the motion

At <InlineMath math="3.0" /> m/s the rod generates <InlineMath math="0.40 \times 0.50 \times 3.0 = 0.60" /> V, driving <InlineMath math="0.30" /> A through the resistor. The field pushes on this current with <InlineMath math="0.40 \times 0.30 \times 0.50 = 0.060" /> N **against** the motion, so the same force is needed to keep it going.

In general the opposing force is <InlineMath math="B^2L^2v \div R = 0.020v" /> newtons. Down the tilted rails the rod is pulled by <InlineMath math="0.020 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30^\circ = 0.098" /> N, so it stops speeding up when <InlineMath math="0.020v = 0.098" />, at <InlineMath math="v = 4.9" /> m/s.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="9.8\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> balances the **whole** weight, but only the part along the rails pulls the rod.
- Speeding up forever forgets that the induced current **grows** with the speed and brakes the rod.
- <InlineMath math="0.12\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> leave the **length** out of the force <InlineMath math="BIL" />.
