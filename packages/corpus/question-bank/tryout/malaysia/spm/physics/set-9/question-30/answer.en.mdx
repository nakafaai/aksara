# Build one row, then put the rows in parallel

Each row adds its cells in series: <InlineMath math="5000 \times 0.15 = 750" /> V with <InlineMath math="5000 \times 0.25 = 1250\,\Omega" /> inside. The <InlineMath math="140" /> identical rows in parallel keep the <InlineMath math="750" /> V but **share** the current, so their internal resistance is <InlineMath math="1250 \div 140 = 8.9\,\Omega" />.

The current through the water is <InlineMath math="750 \div (800 + 8.9) = 0.93" /> A, and the potential difference across it is <InlineMath math="0.93 \times 800 = 742" /> V.

**A fish in parallel**: the fish and the water together make <InlineMath math="800 \times 200 \div 1000 = 160\,\Omega" />, so the organ now drives <InlineMath math="750 \div (160 + 8.9) = 4.4" /> A. The voltage across both falls to <InlineMath math="4.4 \times 160 = 710" /> V, and the fish carries <InlineMath math="710 \div 200 = 3.6" /> A, **more** than the water alone did.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.94\text{ A}" /> and <InlineMath math="3.8\text{ A}" /> leave out the **internal resistance**, which matters more once the load is smaller.
- <InlineMath math="6.6\text{ mA}" /> and <InlineMath math="26\text{ mA}" /> follow **one row** only, not the whole organ.
- <InlineMath math="0.74\text{ A}" /> shares the old <InlineMath math="0.93" /> A between fish and water, but the smaller load makes the organ drive **much more** current.
