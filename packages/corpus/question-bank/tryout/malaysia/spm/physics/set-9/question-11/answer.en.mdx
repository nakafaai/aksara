# Combine the gas laws, with temperatures in kelvin

For a fixed mass of gas <InlineMath math="pV \div T" /> stays the same. At <InlineMath math="50" /> kPa and <InlineMath math="250" /> K, <InlineMath math="V = 5.0 \times (100 \div 50) \times (250 \div 300) = 8.3" /> L. The lower pressure doubles the volume, but the **cold** takes a sixth back off.

At bursting, <InlineMath math="V = 10" /> L and <InlineMath math="T = 240" /> K, so <InlineMath math="p = 100 \times 5.0 \times 240 \div (300 \times 10) = 40" /> kPa.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="10\text{ L}" /> and <InlineMath math="50\text{ kPa}" /> use Boyle’s law alone, ignoring the **colder** air.
- <InlineMath math="42\text{ kPa}" /> keeps <InlineMath math="250" /> K for the burst, but the air there is at <InlineMath math="240" /> K.
- <InlineMath math="12\text{ L}" /> and <InlineMath math="63\text{ kPa}" /> turn the temperature ratio **upside down**, as if cooling made a gas expand.
