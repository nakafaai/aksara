# Combine the gas laws, with the temperature tied to the pressure

For the fixed mass of helium <InlineMath math="pV \div T" /> stays the same. At <InlineMath math="50" /> kPa the air has cooled by <InlineMath math="5 \times 6.5 = 32.5" /> K to <InlineMath math="267.5" /> K, so <InlineMath math="V = 5.0 \times (100 \div 50) \times (267.5 \div 300) = 8.9" /> L: halving the pressure doubles the volume, and the **cold** takes some back.

At bursting, <InlineMath math="V = 10" /> L, but the temperature there **depends on** the unknown pressure: <InlineMath math="T = 300 - 0.65(100 - p) = 235 + 0.65p" />. So <InlineMath math="10p \div (235 + 0.65p) = 500 \div 300" />, which gives <InlineMath math="p = 44" /> kPa, where the air is at <InlineMath math="264" /> K.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="10\text{ L}" /> and <InlineMath math="50\text{ kPa}" /> use Boyle’s law alone, ignoring the **colder** air.
- <InlineMath math="11\text{ L}" /> and <InlineMath math="55\text{ kPa}" /> turn the temperature ratio **upside down**, as if cooling made a gas expand.
- <InlineMath math="50\text{ kPa}" /> for the burst halves the pressure for double the volume, but the air there is **colder** still.
