# Keep the sign of f, solve for v, then scale the width

With <InlineMath math="u = 1000" /> cm and <InlineMath math="f = -50" /> cm, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{v} = -\tfrac{1}{50} - \tfrac{1}{1000} = -0.021" />, so <InlineMath math="v = -47.6" /> cm. The minus sign makes it a **virtual image** <InlineMath math="48" /> cm behind the mirror.

The magnification is <InlineMath math="47.6 \div 1000 = 0.048" />, so the <InlineMath math="1.8" /> m car looks <InlineMath math="180 \times 0.048 = 8.6" /> cm wide. The image is small, which is why drivers judge such cars to be farther away than they really are.

**Closing in**: at <InlineMath math="u = 200" /> cm, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{v} = -\tfrac{1}{50} - \tfrac{1}{200}" />, so <InlineMath math="v = -40" /> cm and <InlineMath math="m = 0.20" />: the car looks <InlineMath math="36" /> cm wide, <InlineMath math="4.2" /> times as wide, not the <InlineMath math="5" /> times its distance ratio suggests.

# Why the other options are wrong

- A real image <InlineMath math="53" /> cm in front drops the **minus sign** of the diverging mirror's focal length.
- <InlineMath math="1.8\text{ m}" /> treats the mirror as plane, but a convex mirror always diminishes.
- <InlineMath math="5" /> times takes the magnification as inversely proportional to the distance, but the image distance changes too.
