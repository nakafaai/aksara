# Find the planet's g, then scale the height and the time with the same take-off speed

<InlineMath math="g" /> goes as <InlineMath math="M \div R^2" />, so the planet's is <InlineMath math="0.10 \div 0.50^2 = 0.40" /> of the Earth's, the radius counting **squared**.

The same take-off speed <InlineMath math="u" /> reaches <InlineMath math="h = u^2 \div 2g" />, so the height grows <InlineMath math="2.5" /> times, to <InlineMath math="1.25" /> m. The time up and down, <InlineMath math="2u \div g" />, also grows <InlineMath math="2.5" /> times.

**A low orbit**: <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{gR}" /> becomes <InlineMath math="\sqrt{0.40 \times 0.50} = 0.45" /> of the Earth's, <InlineMath math="3.5\text{ km s}^{-1}" />. The planet's radius is <InlineMath math="3.2 \times 10^6" /> m, so one lap takes <InlineMath math="2\pi \times 3.2 \times 10^6 \div 3530 = 5.7 \times 10^3" /> s, about <InlineMath math="95" /> minutes.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ m}" /> and <InlineMath math="134" /> minutes take <InlineMath math="g" /> as <InlineMath math="M \div R" />, forgetting that the radius counts **squared**.
- <InlineMath math="42" /> minutes keeps the Earth's orbital speed of <InlineMath math="7.9\text{ km s}^{-1}" /> for the smaller planet.
- <InlineMath math="190" /> minutes uses the Earth's radius for the lap, but the planet is half as wide.
