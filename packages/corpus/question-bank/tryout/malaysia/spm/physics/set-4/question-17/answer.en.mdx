# Find the critical angle in each surrounding, then test the ray against it

In air <InlineMath math="\sin c = 1 \div 2.42" />, so <InlineMath math="c = 24^\circ" />, and the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> ray is totally reflected. That is why a diamond sparkles.

Under water the critical angle depends on **both** refractive indices: <InlineMath math="\sin c = 1.33 \div 2.42" />, so <InlineMath math="c = 33^\circ" />. Now <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> is below it and the ray escapes: <InlineMath math="\sin \theta = 2.42 \times \sin 30^\circ \div 1.33 = 0.91" />, so it leaves at <InlineMath math="66^\circ" />.

**The water surface**: with parallel surfaces the ray meets the water surface at the same <InlineMath math="66^\circ" />. Water's critical angle is <InlineMath math="\sin^{-1}(1 \div 1.33) = 49^\circ" />, so the ray is totally reflected there. Through parallel layers, <InlineMath math="n \sin\theta" /> stays <InlineMath math="2.42 \times \sin 30^\circ = 1.21" />, more than one, so the ray can never reach the air.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Water raises the critical angle of the diamond face to <InlineMath math="33^\circ" />, so the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> ray **does leave** the diamond.
- Escaping into the air ignores the water surface, where <InlineMath math="66^\circ" /> is beyond water's critical angle of <InlineMath math="49^\circ" />.
- <InlineMath math="16^\circ" /> turns the ratio of the refractive indices upside down, bending the ray towards the normal.
