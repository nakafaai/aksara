# The diode removes half the time, not half the voltage

An ideal diode lets through one half cycle in every two and blocks the other completely. During the half cycles it passes, the lamp sees **exactly the same voltage** as before, so the average power halves to <InlineMath math="12" /> W.

To get <InlineMath math="24" /> W back, the power during the conducting halves must double. Power goes as the voltage squared, so the voltage must rise by <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" />: <InlineMath math="12 \times \sqrt{2} = 17" /> V r.m.s.

**The peak current**: the lamp has <InlineMath math="12^2 \div 24 = 6.0\,\Omega" />. A <InlineMath math="17" /> V r.m.s. supply peaks at <InlineMath math="17 \times \sqrt{2} = 24" /> V, so the current peaks at <InlineMath math="24 \div 6.0 = 4.0" /> A, against <InlineMath math="2.8" /> A before the diode was added.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ W}" /> halves the voltage, but the diode leaves the voltage of each passing half cycle **unchanged**.
- <InlineMath math="24\text{ V}" /> doubles the voltage to double the power, but doubling the voltage would make the power four times as large.
- The diode is not invisible: it blocks every other half cycle, so the lamp cannot keep its full <InlineMath math="24" /> W.
