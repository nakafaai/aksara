# Find the radius for a quarter of g, subtract the Earth, then use that radius for the orbit

A quarter of the field needs <InlineMath math="r^2" /> four times as large, so <InlineMath math="r = 2R" />. That is <InlineMath math="2R - R = 6400" /> km **above the surface**, and the astronaut there feels <InlineMath math="60 \times 9.8 \div 4 = 150" /> N.

For an orbit of radius <InlineMath math="2R" />, <InlineMath math="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(2R)^3}{gR^2}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{8R}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{8 \times 6.4 \times 10^6}{9.8}} = 1.4 \times 10^4" /> s, about <InlineMath math="4.0" /> h.

**Passes over one point**: the satellite gains on the turning ground at <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{4.0} - \tfrac{1}{24} = \tfrac{5}{24}" /> of a turn per hour, so it laps the same point every <InlineMath math="4.8" /> hours, which is **five times a day**, not six.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6" /> times a day ignores that the point below **turns with the Earth** in the same direction.
- <InlineMath math="1.4\text{ h}" /> uses the Earth’s radius for the orbit, but a quarter of the field needs **twice that radius**.
- <InlineMath math="7" /> times a day adds the Earth’s turn, which would be right only for a satellite moving **westwards**.
