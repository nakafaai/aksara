# Find the tube's mass from the water, then the mass that the oil can hold at the same depth

In water the tube displaces <InlineMath math="12" /> cubic centimetres, so by **flotation** its mass is <InlineMath math="12" /> g. In oil the same <InlineMath math="12" /> g displaces <InlineMath math="15" /> cubic centimetres, so the oil's density is <InlineMath math="12 \div 15 = 0.80" /> g per cubic centimetre, or <InlineMath math="800" /> kg per cubic metre.

To float in oil with <InlineMath math="12" /> cm under, the tube must weigh as much as <InlineMath math="12" /> cubic centimetres of oil, <InlineMath math="9.6" /> g. The student must **remove** <InlineMath math="2.4" /> g of lead shot.

**In liquid X**: the re-weighted tube has a mass of <InlineMath math="9.6" /> g and now displaces <InlineMath math="10" /> cubic centimetres, so liquid X has a density of <InlineMath math="9.6 \div 10 = 0.96" /> g per cubic centimetre, or <InlineMath math="960" /> kg per cubic metre, **using the new mass**.

**The marks on the tube**: the <InlineMath math="9.6" /> g tube sinks <InlineMath math="9.6" /> cm in water and <InlineMath math="10" /> cm in liquid X, so the marks are only <InlineMath math="0.40" /> cm apart, which is why a real hydrometer has a **thin stem** to spread its scale.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1500\text{ kg m}^{-3}" /> follows from adding lead shot, but a deeper float in oil means the tube is **too heavy**.
- <InlineMath math="900\text{ kg m}^{-3}" /> removes <InlineMath math="3.0" /> g, using the extra depth with **water’s density** instead of oil’s.
- <InlineMath math="240\text{ kg m}^{-3}" /> removes the whole **new mass** instead of the difference.
