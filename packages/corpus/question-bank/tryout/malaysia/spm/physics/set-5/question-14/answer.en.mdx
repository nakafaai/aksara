# Each delay gives a distance, and the change in distance gives the storm's speed

The light arrives at once, so the thunder's delay is its travel time, **one way only**: <InlineMath math="340 \times 6.0 = 2040" /> m, about <InlineMath math="2.0" /> km, then <InlineMath math="340 \times 4.0 = 1360" /> m.

The storm came <InlineMath math="680" /> m closer in three minutes, which is <InlineMath math="180" /> s, so it approaches at <InlineMath math="680 \div 180 = 3.8" /> m/s.

**When it arrives**: from the second flash the front is still <InlineMath math="1360" /> m away, so at <InlineMath math="3.8" /> m/s it arrives after <InlineMath math="1360 \div 3.78 = 360" /> s, about **six minutes**.

**A one-second delay**: that means the front is <InlineMath math="340" /> m away, so it must still close <InlineMath math="1360 - 340 = 1020" /> m, which takes <InlineMath math="1020 \div 3.78 = 270" /> s, about **four and a half minutes**.

**Walking away**: the front is <InlineMath math="340" /> m behind and gains at <InlineMath math="3.78 - 1.5 = 2.3" /> m/s, so it catches the student after <InlineMath math="340 \div 2.3 = 150" /> s, about **two and a half minutes**.

# Why the other options are wrong

- About <InlineMath math="1.5" /> minutes divides by <InlineMath math="60" /> s, taking **three minutes** as one, which makes the storm far too fast.
- About <InlineMath math="1.0" /> minute adds the walking speed, but walking away **reduces** the closing speed.
- About <InlineMath math="1.5" /> minutes later ignores the student’s **own walking**.
