# Only the pressure difference pushes on the window, so invert the force limit to find the outside pressure

The cabin air pushes out and the outside air pushes in, so the window feels the **pressure difference**, <InlineMath math="55" /> kPa, outwards. The net force is <InlineMath math="55\,000 \times 0.060 = 3300" /> N.

The window can take <InlineMath math="4000 \div 0.060 = 66\,700" /> Pa of difference. With <InlineMath math="80" /> kPa inside, the outside must stay **at least** <InlineMath math="80 - 66.7 = 13" /> kPa.

**How high that is**: the outside pressure may fall by a factor of <InlineMath math="100 \div 13.3 = 7.5" />, just under <InlineMath math="2^3 = 8" />, so just under **three halvings**: <InlineMath math="2.9 \times 5.5 = 16" /> km.

**A lower cabin pressure**: the window still takes at most <InlineMath math="66.7" /> kPa of difference, so the outside may fall to <InlineMath math="75 - 66.7 = 8.3" /> kPa. That is a factor of <InlineMath math="12" />, about <InlineMath math="3.6" /> halvings, or <InlineMath math="3.6 \times 5.5 = 20" /> km, **higher than before**.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Still <InlineMath math="16\text{ km}" /> ignores that the window responds to the **difference** in pressure, not to the outside pressure alone.
- <InlineMath math="13\text{ km}" /> adds the drop in cabin pressure to the outside limit, but a lower cabin pressure **lowers** the outside limit.
- <InlineMath math="41\text{ km}" /> and <InlineMath math="66\text{ km}" /> count each factor as that many halvings, but each halving **divides by two**.
