# Only the vertical parts of the two tensions hold the weight, so a flatter rope pulls harder

The weight <InlineMath math="80 \times 9.81 = 785" /> N is shared by the **vertical components** of two ropes: <InlineMath math="2T \sin 10^\circ = 785" />, so <InlineMath math="T = 2.3" /> kN.

For the friend, <InlineMath math="2 \times 3000 \times \sin\theta = 100 \times 9.81" /> gives <InlineMath math="\sin\theta = 0.164" /> and <InlineMath math="\theta = 9.4^\circ" />. A smaller angle makes the tension **larger than the rating**, so the ropes must sag at least this much.

**The pull on the trees**: at the limit each rope carries <InlineMath math="3000" /> N at <InlineMath math="9.4^\circ" />, so its horizontal part is <InlineMath math="3000\cos 9.4^\circ = 2960" /> N. Almost all of the tension **pulls the trees towards each other**.

**How low the ends hang**: each rope spans <InlineMath math="(4.0 - 2.0) \div 2 = 1.0" /> m horizontally, so at <InlineMath math="9.4^\circ" /> its lower end is <InlineMath math="1.0 \times \tan 9.4^\circ = 0.17" /> m below the hook, **only seventeen centimetres** of sag for that safety.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="7.5^\circ" /> solves the limit with the **owner’s mass** instead of the friend’s.
- <InlineMath math="19^\circ" /> lets one rope carry the whole weight, but the **two ropes share** it.
- <InlineMath math="33\text{ cm}" /> gives each rope half the distance between the trees, forgetting the **hammock’s own length** between the ropes.
