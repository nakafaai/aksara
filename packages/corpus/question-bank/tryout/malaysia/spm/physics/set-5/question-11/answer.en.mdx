# At constant pressure the length follows the kelvin temperature

The tube has a fixed bore, so the length measures the volume. At constant pressure <InlineMath math="V \propto T" /> in **kelvin**: from <InlineMath math="300" /> K to <InlineMath math="360" /> K the column grows to <InlineMath math="10.0 \times 360 \div 300 = 12.0" /> cm.

For <InlineMath math="15.0" /> cm the kelvin temperature must be <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times <InlineMath math="300" /> K, which is <InlineMath math="450" /> K, or <InlineMath math="177^\circ\text{C}" />.

**Turning the tube over**: upright, the air holds up the atmosphere and the thread, <InlineMath math="76 + 5.0 = 81" /> cmHg. Upside down, the thread hangs below the air and **pulls against** the atmosphere, leaving <InlineMath math="76 - 5.0 = 71" /> cmHg. At constant temperature, <InlineMath math="81 \times 12.0 = 71 \times L" /> gives <InlineMath math="L = 13.7" /> cm.

**Cooling while upside down**: the pressure stays at <InlineMath math="71" /> cmHg, so Charles’s law applies again: <InlineMath math="13.7 \times 300 \div 360 = 11.4" /> cm, **longer than the original** <InlineMath math="10.0" /> cm because the pressure is lower.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="10.5\text{ cm}" /> inverts the pressure ratio, but a **lower pressure** lets the air expand.
- <InlineMath math="12.0\text{ cm}" /> ignores that the thread’s weight now acts **with the air** instead of on it.
- <InlineMath math="36.7\text{ cm}" /> scales by Celsius temperatures instead of **kelvin**.
