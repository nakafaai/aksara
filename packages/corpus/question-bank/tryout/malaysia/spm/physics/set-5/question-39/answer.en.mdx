# Turn the mass defect into energy, then share it by momentum conservation

The mass defect is <InlineMath math="8.80 \times 10^{-30}" /> kg, so <InlineMath math="E = \Delta m c^2 = 7.92 \times 10^{-13}" /> J, which is <InlineMath math="4.95" /> MeV.

The radium was at rest, so the alpha particle and the radon nucleus leave with **equal and opposite momenta**. Kinetic energy is <InlineMath math="p^2 \div 2m" />, so each share goes inversely with mass: the alpha particle takes <InlineMath math="4.95 \times 222 \div 226 = 4.86" /> MeV.

**The alpha particle’s speed**: <InlineMath math="4.86" /> MeV is <InlineMath math="7.78 \times 10^{-13}" /> J, and the alpha particle has a mass of <InlineMath math="4 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}" /> kg, so <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{2 \times 7.78 \times 10^{-13} \div 6.64 \times 10^{-27}} = 1.5 \times 10^7" /> m/s, **about a twentieth of the speed of light**.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="4.95\text{ MeV}" /> gives the alpha particle all the energy and ignores the **recoil** of the radon nucleus.
- <InlineMath math="0.088\text{ MeV}" /> is the radon’s share, because the **heavier** product moves slowly.
- <InlineMath math="2.48\text{ MeV}" /> splits the energy equally, but equal momenta give **unequal energies**.
