# The tension appears on both sides of the balance, so solve for it as an unknown

The rope's upward part, <InlineMath math="0.60T" />, **reduces the normal force** to <InlineMath math="490.5 - 0.60T" />, and the friction is a fifth of that. At steady speed the horizontal part balances it: <InlineMath math="0.80T = 0.20(490.5 - 0.60T)" />, so <InlineMath math="0.92T = 98.1" /> and <InlineMath math="T = 107" /> N.

Only the **horizontal component** moves along the floor, so the work is <InlineMath math="0.80 \times 107 \times 10 = 853" /> J.

**The rate of working**: only the horizontal part, <InlineMath math="0.80 \times 107 = 85" /> N, acts along the motion, so the power is <InlineMath math="85 \times 0.50 = 43" /> W, all of it **turned to heat by friction**.

**The steeper rope**: now <InlineMath math="0.60T = 0.20(490.5 - 0.80T)" />, so <InlineMath math="0.76T = 98.1" /> and <InlineMath math="T = 129" /> N. The extra lift cuts the friction, but **less of the pull is horizontal**, so the tension rises.

**Work per metre**: only the horizontal part does work. At <InlineMath math="37^\circ" /> it is <InlineMath math="0.80 \times 107 = 85" /> J per metre, at <InlineMath math="53^\circ" /> only <InlineMath math="0.60 \times 129 = 77" /> J per metre. The bigger tension does **less work**, because the steeper rope lifts more and so lowers the friction.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="164\text{ N}" /> takes the friction as a fifth of the full weight, ignoring the **lift from the rope**.
- <InlineMath math="107\text{ N}" /> swaps the sine and cosine at the steeper angle, so it **repeats the first balance**.
- More work at <InlineMath math="53^\circ" /> judges by the tension, but the work depends on the **horizontal component**.
