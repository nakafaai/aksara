# One photon's energy against the work function

One photon is absorbed by one electron, so an electron escapes only if **the energy of one photon exceeds the work function**. The excess becomes the maximum kinetic energy, <InlineMath math="K_{\text{max}}=hf-W" />. Work out the photon energy for each wavelength in eV.

Move the wavelength in the lab from <InlineMath math="400\text{ nm}" /> to <InlineMath math="600\text{ nm}" />, then change the intensity. Only the photon energy decides whether electrons leave, and brighter light only sends more of them.

Set each photon energy against the work function. Only a bar that rises above the line frees electrons, and the part above the line is the maximum kinetic energy.

A <InlineMath math="600\text{ nm}" /> photon does not carry enough energy. Raising the intensity only adds more photons, not more energy per photon, so still no electrons escape. So the maximum kinetic energy for <InlineMath math="400\text{ nm}" /> light is about <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ eV}" />, and <InlineMath math="600\text{ nm}" /> light emits no electrons even at double intensity.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="600\text{ nm}" /> light emits no electrons at all, because its photon energy of <InlineMath math="2.1\text{ eV}" /> is below the work function.
- <InlineMath math="3.1\text{ eV}" /> is the photon energy, without subtracting the work function.
- Intensity raises the number of electrons emitted but **does not change the maximum kinetic energy**.
