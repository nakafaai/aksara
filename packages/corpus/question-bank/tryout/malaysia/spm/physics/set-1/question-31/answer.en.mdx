# Resistance from length and cross-sectional area

The resistance of a wire is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area, <InlineMath math="R\propto\dfrac{l}{A}" />. Area is proportional to **the square of the diameter**, so twice the diameter gives four times the area.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{R_2}{R_1}&=\frac{l_2}{l_1}\cdot\frac{A_1}{A_2}=2\times\frac14=\frac12\\
\frac{P_2}{P_1}&=\frac{V^2/R_2}{V^2/R_1}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}=2
\end{aligned}" />

The voltage is fixed, so the power is inversely proportional to the resistance, <InlineMath math="P=\dfrac{V^2}{R}" />. The resistance halves, so the power doubles. So the second wire dissipates twice the power of the first.

# Why the other options are wrong

- The same assumes the area only doubles, but area goes with **the square of the diameter**.
- Half uses <InlineMath math="P=I^2R" /> as if the current were fixed, but it is the voltage that is fixed.
- Four times accounts for the area only and forgets the doubled length.
