# The heater's power from the liquid stage

The heater's power is not given, but you can find it from the stage where liquid K warms up. **The same power** then acts during melting. Use the temperature change of <InlineMath math="20^\circ\text{C}" />, not the final temperature, and the time in seconds.

Sketch the heating curve from the start of melting. The same heater runs through both stretches, so the energy in each stretch is proportional to its time, <InlineMath math="300\text{ s}" /> against <InlineMath math="120\text{ s}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Q_1&=mc\Delta\theta=(0.20)(2000)(20)=8000\text{ J}\\
P&=\frac{8000}{120}\approx66.7\text{ W}\\
L&=\frac{Pt}{m}=\frac{(66.7)(300)}{0.20}=1.0\times10^{5}\text{ J kg}^{-1}
\end{aligned}" />

A shortcut: melting lasts <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5.0}{2.0}=2.5" /> times as long, so it absorbs <InlineMath math="2.5\times8000=20{,}000\text{ J}" />, which you then divide by <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ kg}" />. So the specific latent heat of fusion of K is <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^{5}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^{4}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" /> is the total heat for melting, <InlineMath math="20{,}000\text{ J}" />, **not yet divided by the mass**.
- <InlineMath math="1.6\times10^{4}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" /> inverts the time ratio, using <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{5}" /> instead of <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{2}" />.
- <InlineMath math="5.0\times10^{5}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" /> uses the final temperature of <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> as the temperature change.
