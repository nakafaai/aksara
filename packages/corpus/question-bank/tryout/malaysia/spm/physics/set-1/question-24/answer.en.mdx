# Equal pressure and equal volume

Pascal's principle states that **pressure is transmitted equally throughout the liquid**, so force is proportional to cross-sectional area. The liquid is also incompressible, so the volume pushed by the small piston equals the volume that lifts the large piston.

Mark the shared pressure on the jack. The same <InlineMath math="75{,}000\text{ Pa}" /> pushes on both pistons, so the force grows with the area while the volume of liquid moved stays the same.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
F_2&=F_1\cdot\frac{A_2}{A_1}=150\times\frac{0.10}{0.0020}=7500\text{ N}\ \Rightarrow\ m=\frac{7500}{10}=750\text{ kg}\\
d_2&=d_1\cdot\frac{A_1}{A_2}=20\times\frac{0.0020}{0.10}=0.40\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

The force is multiplied by <InlineMath math="50" />, but the distance is divided by <InlineMath math="50" />, so the work at both pistons is the same, <InlineMath math="150\times0.20=7500\times0.0040=30\text{ J}" />. So the large piston can lift <InlineMath math="750\text{ kg}" /> and rises <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ cm}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Rising <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" /> assumes both pistons move the same distance, but it is **the volume of liquid moved that is equal**, not the distance.
- <InlineMath math="7500\text{ kg}" /> uses the force of <InlineMath math="7500\text{ N}" /> as a mass without dividing by <InlineMath math="g" />.
- <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ kg}" /> with <InlineMath math="1000\text{ cm}" /> inverts the area ratio in both steps.
