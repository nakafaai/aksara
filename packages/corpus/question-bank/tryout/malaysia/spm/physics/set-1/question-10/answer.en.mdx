# Does all the ice melt

Before finding the final temperature, **check whether all the ice can melt**. Compare the heat needed to melt all the ice with the heat the water gives out as it cools to <InlineMath math="0^\circ\text{C}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Q_{\text{melt}}&=(0.050)(3.36\times10^{5})=16{,}800\text{ J}\\
Q_{\text{water}}&=(0.200)(4200)(30)=25{,}200\text{ J}
\end{aligned}" />

Set the two amounts of heat side by side. The water's bar is taller than the melting bar, and the difference is the heat left to warm all the water above <InlineMath math="0^\circ\text{C}" />.

The water supplies enough, with <InlineMath math="25{,}200-16{,}800=8400\text{ J}" /> left over. That remaining heat warms all the water, which is <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ kg}" /> including the melted ice.

<BlockMath math="8400=(0.250)(4200)\,\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \theta=8^\circ\text{C}" />

Check the heat balance: the original water falls by <InlineMath math="22^\circ\text{C}" /> and gives out <InlineMath math="18{,}480\text{ J}" />, while the ice takes in <InlineMath math="16{,}800+(0.050)(4200)(8)=18{,}480\text{ J}" />. So the final temperature of the mixture is <InlineMath math="8^\circ\text{C}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> comes from warming only the original <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ kg}" /> of water, but **the melted ice warms up too**.
- <InlineMath math="24^\circ\text{C}" /> ignores the latent heat, as if the ice were just water at <InlineMath math="0^\circ\text{C}" />.
- <InlineMath math="0^\circ\text{C}" /> assumes some ice is left, but the water supplies more than enough heat to melt it all.
