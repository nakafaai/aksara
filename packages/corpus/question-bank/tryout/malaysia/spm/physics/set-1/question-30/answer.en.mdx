# The resistance is what stays fixed

The label <InlineMath math="240\text{ V},\ 1000\text{ W}" /> applies only at <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" />. At another voltage, **the resistance of the heater stays fixed**, not its power. So find the resistance from the label, then the power at <InlineMath math="120\text{ V}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
R&=\frac{V^2}{P}=\frac{240^2}{1000}=57.6\,\Omega\\
P'&=\frac{120^2}{57.6}=250\text{ W}=0.25\text{ kW}\\
E&=P't=(0.25)(2.0)=0.50\text{ kWh}
\end{aligned}" />

Half the voltage also halves the current, so the power drops to a quarter. So the heater uses <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ kWh}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ kWh}" /> assumes the power only halves, forgetting that **the current falls too** when the voltage falls.
- <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ kWh}" /> assumes the heater still runs at <InlineMath math="1000\text{ W}" />.
- <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ kWh}" /> finds the resistance with <InlineMath math="120\text{ V}" /> and then uses <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" />, which makes the power four times larger.
