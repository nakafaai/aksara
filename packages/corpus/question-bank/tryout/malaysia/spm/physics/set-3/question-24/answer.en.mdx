# Turn the mercury drop into pascals, then into a column of air

The reading **falls** by <InlineMath math="6.0" /> cm of mercury: <InlineMath math="\Delta p = h\rho g = 0.060 \times 13\,600 \times 10 = 8160" /> Pa.

That pressure difference is the weight per square metre of the air between the foot and the top, so <InlineMath math="h = \Delta p \div (\rho_{\text{air}} g) = 8160 \div 12 = 680" /> m. Air is about <InlineMath math="11\,000" /> times less dense than mercury, so each centimetre of mercury stands for about <InlineMath math="113" /> m of air.

**The sealed bag**: the flexible bag keeps its air at the outside pressure, so <InlineMath math="\dfrac{pV}{T}" /> stays the same. The lower pressure and the higher kelvin temperature both enlarge it: <InlineMath math="V = 2.00 \times \dfrac{76.0}{70.0} \times \dfrac{315}{300} = 2.28" /> L. The pressures can stay in cmHg, because only their ratio matters.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="50\text{ m}" /> uses the density of **water** instead of mercury for the barometer.
- <InlineMath math="1.93\text{ L}" /> turns the pressure ratio upside down, but a lower pressure lets the air **expand**.
- <InlineMath math="3.38\text{ L}" /> takes the ratio of the Celsius temperatures, but gas volume follows the kelvin temperature.
