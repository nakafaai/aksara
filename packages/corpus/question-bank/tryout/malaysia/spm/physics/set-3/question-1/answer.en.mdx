# Subtract a negative zero error, then cube the radius

The correct reading is the reading **minus the zero error**: <InlineMath math="6.38 - (-0.02) = 6.40" /> mm. A zero error below zero means every reading comes out too small.

Radius <InlineMath math="0.320" /> cm, so <InlineMath math="V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi (0.320)^3 = 0.1373\text{ cm}^3" /> and <InlineMath math="\rho = 1.070 \div 0.1373 = 7.80\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, the density of steel.

Without the correction the diameter is <InlineMath math="6.38" /> mm, the volume is smaller, and the density comes out at <InlineMath math="7.87\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />: <InlineMath math="(7.87 - 7.80) \div 7.80 = 0.9\%" /> too high. A diameter only <InlineMath math="0.3\%" /> short gives a density about three times that error, because the volume goes as the cube.

**Hidden cavity**: solid steel of that mass would fill <InlineMath math="1.070 \div 7.85 = 0.1363\text{ cm}^3" />. The ball fills <InlineMath math="0.1373\text{ cm}^3" />, so about <InlineMath math="0.00095\text{ cm}^3" />, or <InlineMath math="0.95\text{ mm}^3" />, is empty. With the uncorrected <InlineMath math="6.38" /> mm the ball would fill only <InlineMath math="0.1360\text{ cm}^3" />, less than its own steel, which is impossible.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="7.94\text{ g cm}^{-3}" /> adds the zero error the wrong way round, reading <InlineMath math="6.36" /> mm, which makes the ball look **denser than solid steel**.
- <InlineMath math="0.975\text{ g cm}^{-3}" /> uses the diameter as the radius, eight times the volume, so most of the ball would seem empty.
- <InlineMath math="7.87\text{ g cm}^{-3}" /> leaves out the zero error, and then the ball's volume is smaller than the volume of its own steel.
