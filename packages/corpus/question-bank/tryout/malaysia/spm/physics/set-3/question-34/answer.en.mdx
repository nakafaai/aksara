# Faster turning raises both the voltage and the frequency, and power goes as voltage squared

By Faraday's law the e.m.f. depends on the **rate of cutting flux**. Twice the speed doubles that rate, so the peak becomes <InlineMath math="12" /> V. One turn makes one cycle, so twice as many turns each second gives <InlineMath math="100" /> Hz.

With <InlineMath math="P = V^2 \div R" /> and a fixed resistance, doubling the voltage makes the power four times as large. The average power rises from <InlineMath math="(6.0 \div \sqrt{2})^2 \div 12 = 1.5" /> W to <InlineMath math="6.0" /> W.

**Fewer turns, two lamps**: halving the turns halves the e.m.f., so the doubled speed now gives a <InlineMath math="6.0" /> V peak, while the frequency stays <InlineMath math="100" /> Hz because it depends only on the turning rate. Each lamp in parallel gets the whole <InlineMath math="6.0" /> V peak and takes <InlineMath math="(6.0 \div \sqrt{2})^2 \div 12 = 1.5" /> W, so the generator supplies <InlineMath math="3.0" /> W in all.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.38\text{ W}" /> halves the original <InlineMath math="6.0" /> V instead of the new <InlineMath math="12" /> V, as if the faster turning no longer counted.
- The frequency is the number of turns each second, so it cannot stay at <InlineMath math="50" /> Hz.
- <InlineMath math="0.75\text{ W}" /> shares one lamp's power between two, but lamps **in parallel** each get the full voltage.
