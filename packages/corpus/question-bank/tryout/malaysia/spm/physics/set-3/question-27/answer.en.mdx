# The voltmeter joins the circuit

**A voltmeter in parallel with a resistor changes the resistance of that part of the circuit**, so it no longer shares the supply equally.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
10\text{ k}\Omega\parallel10\text{ k}\Omega&=5.0\text{ k}\Omega,\qquad V=12\times\frac{5.0}{5.0+10}=4.0\text{ V}\\
10\text{ k}\Omega\parallel1.0\text{ M}\Omega&=9.90\text{ k}\Omega,\qquad V=12\times\frac{9.90}{9.90+10}=5.97\text{ V}
\end{aligned}" />

A meter much more resistant than the resistor barely disturbs it, which is why voltmeters are made with large resistance.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ V}" /> **for both ignores the current the voltmeter draws**.
- <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ V}" /> for the <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ M}\Omega" /> meter rounds away the small loading that remains.
- <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ V}" /> splits the supply over three equal <InlineMath math="10\text{ k}\Omega" /> parts, as if the meter were in series.
