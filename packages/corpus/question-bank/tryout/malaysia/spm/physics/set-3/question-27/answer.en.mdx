The liquid has to enter a vein that is already <InlineMath math="2.0" /> kPa above atmospheric, and on the way it **loses <InlineMath math="7.0" /> kPa** in the narrow tube. The column must supply <InlineMath math="2.0 + 7.0 = 9.0" /> kPa.

**Raising the bag**: at <InlineMath math="0.857 + 0.20 = 1.057" /> m the liquid column gives <InlineMath math="1050 \times 10 \times 1.057 = 11.1" /> kPa. The vein still takes <InlineMath math="2.0" /> kPa, so the tube now has <InlineMath math="9.1" /> kPa instead of <InlineMath math="7.0" /> kPa, and the flow is <InlineMath math="9.1 \div 7.0 = 1.3" /> times as fast, more than the heights alone suggest.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="19\text{ cm}" /> only matches the vein pressure, so the drip would **barely flow** through the tube.
- <InlineMath math="67\text{ cm}" /> covers the tube's drop but forgets the vein pushes back.
- <InlineMath math="1.2" /> compares the heights, but the vein's <InlineMath math="2.0" /> kPa is a fixed back pressure, so the drop across the tube grows faster than the height.
