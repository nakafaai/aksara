# The whole length of wire in the field feels the force

In a radial field every part of every turn is **perpendicular to the field**, and the forces on all parts point the same way along the axis. So the force is <InlineMath math="F=BIL" /> with <InlineMath math="L" /> the **total length of wire**: <InlineMath math="50" /> circumferences. Newton's second law then gives the cone's acceleration, with the mass in kilograms.

<BlockMath math="L=50\times2\pi(0.010)=50\times0.0628=3.14\text{ m},\qquad F=BIL=0.80\times0.20\times3.14\approx0.50\text{ N}" />

<BlockMath math="a=\frac{F}{m}=\frac{0.50}{0.010}=50\text{ m s}^{-2}" />

The coil holds <InlineMath math="3.14\text{ m}" /> of wire, so it feels about <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ N}" />. On a <InlineMath math="10\text{ g}" /> cone that gives an acceleration of about <InlineMath math="50\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, five times <InlineMath math="g" />.

<BlockMath math="s=\tfrac12at^2=\tfrac12(50)(2.0\times10^{-3})^2=1.0\times10^{-4}\text{ m}=0.10\text{ mm}" />

Even with an acceleration of five times <InlineMath math="g" />, the cone moves only a tenth of a millimetre in <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ ms}" />, which is why a loudspeaker cone can vibrate hundreds of times a second.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ N}" /> uses the length of **one turn only**, forgetting the other <InlineMath math="49" />, and every later value shrinks with it.
- <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ N}" /> uses the **diameter instead of the radius** in the circumference.
- <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ m s}^{-2}" /> uses the mass as **<InlineMath math="10" /> instead of <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ kg}" />**.
