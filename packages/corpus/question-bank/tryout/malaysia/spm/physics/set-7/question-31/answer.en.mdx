# One thin track, one resistance

Think of each strip as a flat ribbon, thinner than a hair. A thin ribbon is a narrow road for the charge, so it fights the current hard. That fight is its **resistance**, and for a uniform track it is <InlineMath math="R=\rho L/A" />.

The road's cross-section is the width times the thickness, so watch the units: <InlineMath math="12\ \mu\text{m}" /> is <InlineMath math="12\times10^{-6}\text{ m}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A&=1.0\times10^{-3}\times12\times10^{-6}=1.2\times10^{-8}\text{ m}^2\\
R_1&=\frac{\rho L}{A}=\frac{2.0\times10^{-7}\times1.20}{1.2\times10^{-8}}=20\,\Omega,\qquad R_{10}=\frac{20}{10}=2.0\,\Omega
\end{aligned}" />

Ten strips side by side are ten roads, so together they make only <InlineMath math="2.0\,\Omega" />. That <InlineMath math="2.0\,\Omega" /> belongs to the whole grid. One strip on its own still has <InlineMath math="20\,\Omega" />.

# The battery keeps some volts for itself

A battery is a bit like a tank with a narrow outlet. Some of the push is used up just getting out. Here the battery and the wires take <InlineMath math="0.50\,\Omega" />, so **the strips never feel the full <InlineMath math="12.5\text{ V}" />**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
I&=\frac{12.5}{2.0+0.50}=5.0\text{ A},\qquad V=5.0\times2.0=10.0\text{ V}\\
P_1&=\frac{V^2}{R_1}=\frac{10.0^2}{20}=5.0\text{ W}
\end{aligned}" />

Each strip sits across the same <InlineMath math="10.0\text{ V}" />, because they are in parallel. Put the whole <InlineMath math="12.5\text{ V}" /> across a strip and you get <InlineMath math="7.8\text{ W}" />, which forgets the <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ V}" /> lost inside the battery and wires.

# From watts to melted frost

Melting ice takes energy even though it stays at <InlineMath math="0^\circ\text{C}" /> the whole time. That hidden energy is the **specific latent heat of fusion**. The ten strips give <InlineMath math="50\text{ W}" />, but only <InlineMath math="60\%" /> of it, <InlineMath math="30\text{ W}" />, reaches the frost.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Q&=mL=0.025\times3.34\times10^{5}=8350\text{ J}\\
t&=\frac{8350}{0.60\times50}=278\text{ s}\approx280\text{ s}
\end{aligned}" />

That is about four and a half minutes. Using the full <InlineMath math="50\text{ W}" /> gives <InlineMath math="170\text{ s}" />, as if no heat ever warmed the glass or slipped into the air.
