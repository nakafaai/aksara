# The air cancels on both sides, so only the water presses the hatch shut against the bolts

The water side feels the **atmosphere plus the water**, and the pipe side feels the **atmosphere**. The resultant force therefore comes from the water's pressure <InlineMath math="\rho gh" /> alone, times the hatch's area, which needs the **radius**, half the diameter. The bolts share that force, and their combined limit sets the deepest the hatch can be.

The water pushes the hatch with about <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^{5}\text{ N}" />, so each bolt holds about <InlineMath math="12.6\text{ kN}" />, inside its limit. The bolts together can hold <InlineMath math="1.2\times10^{5}\text{ N}" />, which the water reaches with the hatch's centre about <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" /> deep.

With the pipe pumped empty, nothing pushes back on the dry side, so the atmosphere pressing on the reservoir's surface now adds its full <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^{5}\text{ Pa}" /> to the load. The bolts reach their limit <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> sooner, with the centre about <InlineMath math="14\text{ m}" /> deep.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" /> for the sealed pipe forgets that **the atmosphere no longer cancels** once the air behind the hatch is pumped out.
- <InlineMath math="1.5\times10^{5}\text{ N}" /> uses the **total pressure** on the water side from the start, forgetting the air pushing back from the open pipe.
- <InlineMath math="4.0\times10^{5}\text{ N}" /> uses the **diameter as the radius**, making the area four times too large, so even the atmosphere alone seems to break the bolts.
