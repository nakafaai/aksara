# A small push, a big pull

Hang a wet towel on a washing line pulled tight, and the line still sags a little. The tighter the line, the less it sags and the harder it strains. The rope here behaves the same. **Each half pulls along its own length, and only their small sideways parts can hold back the <InlineMath math="400\text{ N}" /> push**. With the midpoint <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ m}" /> out over a half-span of <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ m}" />, <InlineMath math="\tan\theta=0.10" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin\theta=0.0995" />. Both halves share the push, so <InlineMath math="2T\sin\theta=400" /> and <InlineMath math="T=2.0\text{ kN}" />. That is five times the push, but still short of the car's <InlineMath math="2500\text{ N}" />. Leaving out the second half doubles the tension to <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ kN}" />, and then the car would already be out.

# The push that would free the car

Push the middle of a skipping rope held by two friends and you push against both of them at once. With the same sag, a bigger pull needs a push bigger in the same ratio. **For <InlineMath math="2500\text{ N}" /> the push must be <InlineMath math="2\times2500\times0.0995=500\text{ N}" />**, a quarter more than the driver gave. Using one half's sideways part alone gives <InlineMath math="250\text{ N}" />, but the push bends both halves together.

# Tighter wins, not farther

Sit in a hammock strung almost flat and its ropes pull on the trees far harder than in a saggy one. Pushing the midpoint farther out feels as if it should help. It does the opposite. **For <InlineMath math="400\text{ N}" /> to make <InlineMath math="2500\text{ N}" />, <InlineMath math="\sin\theta\le400/5000=0.080" />**, so the midpoint may sit at most <InlineMath math="5.0\tan4.6^\circ=0.40\text{ m}" /> to the side. Scaling <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ m}" /> up by <InlineMath math="2500/2010" /> to <InlineMath math="0.62\text{ m}" /> makes the rope slacker and the pull weaker.
