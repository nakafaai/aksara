# The force is the momentum the belt gives the sand each second

Each second the belt must take <InlineMath math="20\text{ kg}" /> of sand from **zero horizontal speed** to <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. Force is the rate of change of momentum, so the motor's extra force is the momentum given per second. Power is that force times the belt's speed. Comparing it with the kinetic energy the sand gains each second shows that **only half** of the power ends up as motion: the rest is heat while the grains slip before they reach the belt's speed.

The motor pushes with an extra <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" /> and supplies <InlineMath math="45\text{ W}" />. The sand gains only <InlineMath math="22.5\text{ W}" /> of kinetic energy, so <InlineMath math="50\%" /> becomes motion and <InlineMath math="50\%" /> becomes heat. The half is no accident: <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\dot mv^2" /> is always half of <InlineMath math="\dot mv\cdot v" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="100\%" /> assumes **all the work becomes kinetic energy**, but the grains slip on the belt until they match its speed, and that friction produces heat.
- <InlineMath math="15\text{ N}" /> starts from the **kinetic energy per second**, <InlineMath math="22.5\text{ W}" />, and divides by the speed, so it misses the heat and halves the force.
- <InlineMath math="200\text{ N}" /> uses the **weight of the sand landing each second**, but the belt only has to give the sand horizontal momentum, and the weight acts vertically.
