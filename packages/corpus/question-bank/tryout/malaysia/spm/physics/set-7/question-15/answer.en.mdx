# Work out each wavelength and time, then compare with the hill

Waves bend around an obstacle most when their wavelength is **similar in size to the obstacle**. Each statement needs its own number from <InlineMath math="v=f\lambda" /> or <InlineMath math="t=\dfrac sv" />, and then a comparison.

<BlockMath math="t=\frac{3.0\times10^{4}}{3.0\times10^{8}}=1.0\times10^{-4}\text{ s},\qquad N=ft=60,\qquad \lambda_{50}=\frac{3.0\times10^{8}}{5.0\times10^{7}}=6.0\text{ m}" />

Statements I, II and III are correct: the AM wave matches the hill, the FM wave is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{167}" /> of it, and in the <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ ms}" /> the signal takes to cross <InlineMath math="30\text{ km}" /> the transmitter completes <InlineMath math="60" /> cycles. Statement IV gets the <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ m}" /> right but the **conclusion wrong**: <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ m}" /> is still less than an eightieth of the hill, nothing like the <InlineMath math="500\text{ m}" /> AM wave.

# Why the other options are wrong

- **I, II, III and IV** accepts statement IV, but doubling a tiny wavelength still leaves it tiny compared with the hill.
- **I and III only** rejects statement II, although <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ m}" /> is far less than <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" />, a hundredth of the hill.
- **II, III and IV only** rejects statement I, although the AM wavelength is exactly the hill's width.
