# Use the total pressure and kelvin temperatures in the gas law

The pressure on the bubble at the bottom is the **atmosphere plus the water above it**. Temperatures must be in **kelvin** in the gas law. Then the volume follows from <InlineMath math="\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}" />, and since volume grows with the radius cubed, the radius grows by the **cube root** of the volume factor.

<BlockMath math="p_1=1.0\times10^{5}+1000(10)(20)=3.0\times10^{5}\text{ Pa},\qquad V_2=2.0\times\frac{3.0}{1.0}\times\frac{300}{280}\approx6.4\text{ cm}^3" />

<BlockMath math="\frac{r_2}{r_1}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{6.4}{2.0}}=\sqrt[3]{3.2}\approx1.5" />

The pressure falls to a third, which alone would triple the volume. The warming from <InlineMath math="280\text{ K}" /> to <InlineMath math="300\text{ K}" /> adds about <InlineMath math="7\%" /> more, giving about <InlineMath math="6.4\text{ cm}^3" />. The volume grows <InlineMath math="3.2" /> times, so the radius grows about <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6.0\text{ cm}^3" /> **ignores the warming** of the bubble as it rises.
- <InlineMath math="23\text{ cm}^3" /> uses **degrees Celsius**, <InlineMath math="27/7" />, in place of kelvin.
- <InlineMath math="4.3\text{ cm}^3" /> uses only the **water pressure, <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^{5}\text{ Pa}" />**, against <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^{5}\text{ Pa}" /> at the top, leaving out the atmosphere at the bottom.
