# Pressure comes from the water above the tap

Dive to the bottom of a pool and your ears feel all the water above you. A tap feels the same thing, the water standing between it and the tower's surface. **On floor <InlineMath math="3" /> that column is <InlineMath math="25-9.0=16\text{ m}" /> tall, so the gauge reads <InlineMath math="1000\times10\times16=1.6\times10^{5}\text{ Pa}" />**. # The evening level

Scoop water out of a wide basin and the level hardly drops. The wider the basin, the smaller the drop. **Here <InlineMath math="30\text{ m}^3" /> spread over <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}^2" /> is a drop of <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ m}" />**, so the surface sits at <InlineMath math="22\text{ m}" /> and taps up to <InlineMath math="17\text{ m}" /> still work. Floor <InlineMath math="5" /> at <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> is now the top working floor. Reading <InlineMath math="30\text{ m}^3" /> as a <InlineMath math="30\text{ m}" /> drop would empty the tank on paper and leave no floor working at all.

# When floor <InlineMath math="6" /> stops
