# The same power at a higher voltage needs a smaller current

The current in the cables is <InlineMath math="I=\dfrac PV" />. The heat wasted in the cables is <InlineMath math="I^2R" />, which depends on the **current squared**, not on the transmission voltage directly. Stepping the voltage up <InlineMath math="25" /> times makes the current <InlineMath math="25" /> times smaller, so the loss falls by **<InlineMath math="25^2=625" /> times**.

At <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ kV}" /> the cables waste <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ kW}" />, a tenth of the power. At <InlineMath math="25\text{ kV}" /> they waste only <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ W}" />, which is why power is transmitted at high voltage.

<BlockMath math="I^2(2.0)\le0.010\times50\,000=500\text{ W}\Rightarrow I\le\sqrt{250}\approx15.8\text{ A},\qquad V\ge\frac{50\,000}{15.8}\approx3.2\text{ kV}" />

Losing at most <InlineMath math="500\text{ W}" /> in <InlineMath math="2.0\,\Omega" /> limits the current to about <InlineMath math="15.8\text{ A}" />. Sending <InlineMath math="50\text{ kW}" /> with so little current needs at least about <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ kV}" />, so the loss falls with the square of the voltage, not in proportion to it.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="200\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="10\text{ kV}" /> make the loss fall in proportion to the voltage, **not with its square**.
- <InlineMath math="100\text{ W}" />, <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ kV}" /> use **<InlineMath math="IR" />**, which is the voltage drop along the cables in volts, not the power lost.
- <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ kW}" />, <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="2.2\text{ kV}" /> **halve the cable's resistance**, as if only one of the two wires had resistance.
