# The same power at a higher voltage needs a smaller current

The current in the cables is <InlineMath math="I=\dfrac PV" />. The heat wasted in the cables is <InlineMath math="I^2R" />, which depends on the **current squared**, not on the transmission voltage directly. Stepping the voltage up <InlineMath math="25" /> times makes the current <InlineMath math="25" /> times smaller, so the loss falls by **<InlineMath math="25^2=625" /> times**.

At <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ kV}" /> the cables waste <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ kW}" />, a tenth of the power. At <InlineMath math="25\text{ kV}" /> they waste only <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ W}" />, which is why power is transmitted at high voltage.

<BlockMath math="\frac{50\,000-5000}{50\,000}=90\%" />

At <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ kV}" /> the cables waste a tenth of the power, so only <InlineMath math="90\%" /> of the generator's <InlineMath math="50\text{ kW}" /> reaches the village.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="200\text{ W}" /> makes the loss fall in proportion to the voltage, **<InlineMath math="25" /> times instead of <InlineMath math="25^2" /> times**.
- <InlineMath math="100\text{ W}" />, <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="99.8\%" /> use **<InlineMath math="IR" />**, which is the voltage drop along the cables in volts, not the power lost.
- <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ kW}" />, <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="95\%" /> **halve the cable's effect**, as if only one of the two wires had resistance.
