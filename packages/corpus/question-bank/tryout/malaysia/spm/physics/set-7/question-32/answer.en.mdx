# Why the liquid metal gets a push

Picture the liquid alloy as a crowd of tiny wires lying across the channel, wall to wall. Each one carries current, and a current in a magnetic field gets a sideways shove. Here the shove points along the channel, so **the field pushes the liquid forward**, with no moving parts at all.

The current crosses the <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ cm}" /> width, so that width is the length of wire in the field, and the force is <InlineMath math="F=BIw" />.

# A force spread over the channel's face

Pressure is a force shared out over an area. The push acts on the liquid that fills the channel's cross-section, the little rectangle you would see looking down the pipe, <InlineMath math="w\times h" />. Watch what happens: the width cancels, and **only the channel height is left** under the line.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
F&=BIw=0.50\times500\times0.040=10\text{ N}\\
\Delta p&=\frac{F}{wh}=\frac{BI}{h}=\frac{0.50\times500}{0.010}=2.5\times10^{4}\text{ Pa}
\end{aligned}" />

Two tempting areas give the wrong pressure. Dividing by the channel's floor, <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}\times4.0\text{ cm}" />, gives <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ kPa}" />. Letting the current run up the <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ cm}" /> height instead of across the width gives <InlineMath math="6.3\text{ kPa}" />.

# How tall a column that push can hold

A column of liquid presses down with <InlineMath math="\rho g H" /> at its foot. The pump holds the column up as long as **its push matches that weight per area**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
H&=\frac{\Delta p}{\rho g}=\frac{2.5\times10^{4}}{850\times9.81}=3.0\text{ m}\\
I&=\frac{\rho g H h}{B}=\frac{850\times9.81\times4.5\times0.020}{0.50}=1.5\times10^{3}\text{ A}
\end{aligned}" />
