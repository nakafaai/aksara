# Include the internal resistance in the total, then follow the current through

The current depends on the **whole circuit**, including the battery's own <InlineMath math="1.0\,\Omega" />. When the <InlineMath math="3.0\,\Omega" /> branch fails, the parallel pair becomes the <InlineMath math="6.0\,\Omega" /> resistor alone, so the total resistance rises and the current falls. A smaller current loses less voltage inside the battery, so the **terminal voltage rises**, and the <InlineMath math="6.0\,\Omega" /> resistor, now carrying the whole current, heats up more.

The battery now delivers about <InlineMath math="1.1\text{ A}" />. The <InlineMath math="6.0\,\Omega" /> resistor dissipates about <InlineMath math="7.1\text{ W}" />, more than three times the <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ W}" /> it took before, and the terminal voltage rises to about <InlineMath math="10.9\text{ V}" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{total}}}=\frac{IV}{IE}=\frac{10.9}{12}\approx91\%" />

The same current flows through the battery and the outside resistors, so the share delivered outside is the terminal voltage over the emf: about <InlineMath math="91\%" />, up from <InlineMath math="86\%" /> before the failure.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ A}" /> and <InlineMath math="100\%" /> **ignore the internal resistance**, as if no energy were lost inside the battery.
- <InlineMath math="1.7\text{ A}" />, <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ W}" /> and <InlineMath math="86\%" /> describe the circuit **before the failure**.
- <InlineMath math="20\text{ W}" /> puts the **whole terminal voltage** across the <InlineMath math="6.0\,\Omega" /> resistor, forgetting the <InlineMath math="4.0\,\Omega" /> resistor in series with it.
