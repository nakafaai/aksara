# Each break shortens the string a little

Picture a line of people passing buckets. Swap one slow person for a quick helper and the whole line moves a little faster. A broken bulb does that, because its <InlineMath math="36\,\Omega" /> filament is replaced by a <InlineMath math="1.5\,\Omega" /> shunt. **With <InlineMath math="3" /> filaments broken the string has <InlineMath math="37\times36+3\times1.5=1336.5\,\Omega" />, so <InlineMath math="I=240/1336.5=0.180\text{ A}" />**. Forgetting the breaks leaves the first current, <InlineMath math="240/1440=0.167\text{ A}" />.

# Two kinds of part can overheat

A rope bridge can fail at a plank or at a knot, so you check both. **Each break raises the current, and with it both a lit bulb's power <InlineMath math="I^2\times36" /> and a shunt's power <InlineMath math="I^2\times1.5" />**. The bulbs reach <InlineMath math="1.25\text{ W}" /> only at <InlineMath math="0.186\text{ A}" />, but a shunt reaches <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ W}" /> already at <InlineMath math="0.183\text{ A}" />. After <InlineMath math="3" /> breaks the current is <InlineMath math="0.180\text{ A}" /> and every part is safe. After <InlineMath math="4" /> it is <InlineMath math="0.184\text{ A}" />. The bulbs still cope with <InlineMath math="1.22\text{ W}" />, but each shunt now takes <InlineMath math="0.051\text{ W}" />.

# The dark bulbs fail first

It is easy to watch the bright bulbs and forget the dark ones, like checking the lit rooms of a house and never the cupboard. **So at most <InlineMath math="3" /> filaments may break, and the next break overheats a shunt, not a lit bulb**. Checking only the bulbs allows <InlineMath math="4" /> breaks and blames a lit bulb.

I_3&=\frac{240}{37\times36+3\times1.5}=0.180\text{ A},\quad I_4=\frac{240}{36\times36+4\times1.5}=0.184\text{ A}\\
P_B&=0.184^2\times36=1.22\text{ W}\le1.25\text{ W}\\
P_S&=0.184^2\times1.5=0.051\text{ W}>0.050\text{ W}
\end{aligned}" />
