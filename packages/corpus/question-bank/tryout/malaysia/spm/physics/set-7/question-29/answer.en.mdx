# Reaching the melting point is not enough: the wire must also be given its latent heat

The current heats the wire at <InlineMath math="P=I^2R" />. To blow, the wire must first be **warmed to its melting point** and then **melted**, which takes the latent heat as well, at a steady <InlineMath math="327" /> °C. A smaller steady current never melts the wire if the heat it makes can escape as fast as it is produced, so the safe limit comes from setting <InlineMath math="I^2R" /> equal to the greatest heat loss.

The fault heats the wire at <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ W}" /> and melts it through in <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ s}" />. A steady current up to about <InlineMath math="12\text{ A}" /> never melts it, because the wire sheds the heat as fast as the current makes it.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.24\text{ s}" /> only **warms the wire to its melting point**, forgetting the latent heat needed to melt it.
- <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ W}" /> uses **<InlineMath math="P=IR" />**, which is not a power, and the slip carries into the time and the current.
- <InlineMath math="0.16\text{ s}" /> supplies **only the latent heat**, as if the wire were already at <InlineMath math="327" /> °C.
