# Count atoms, not milligrams

Each uranium atom that decays ends up, after a quick chain of steps, as exactly one lead atom. So the story is told in **atoms**, and grams come only at the end.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n_U&=\frac{4.70}{235}=0.0200\text{ mmol},\qquad n_{Pb}=\frac{12.42}{207}=0.0600\text{ mmol},\qquad n_0=0.0800\text{ mmol}
\end{aligned}" />

Adding the masses, <InlineMath math="4.70+12.42=17.1\text{ mg}" />, to get the starting uranium forgets that each decay throws away alpha and beta particles. A lead atom is lighter than the uranium atom it came from.

# A quarter left means two halvings

Now only <InlineMath math="0.0200" /> of the starting <InlineMath math="0.0800\text{ mmol}" /> is left. Half, then half again: **a quarter left means two half-lives**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{n_U}{n_0}&=\frac{0.0200}{0.0800}=\frac14=\left(\frac12\right)^2\ \Rightarrow\ t=2\times7.0\times10^{8}=1.4\times10^{9}\\
m_0&=0.0800\times235=18.8\text{ mg}
\end{aligned}" />

Reading the <InlineMath math="3:1" /> ratio of lead to uranium as three half-lives gives <InlineMath math="2.1\times10^{9}" /> years. Three half-lives would leave one eighth, with lead to uranium at <InlineMath math="7:1" />.

# The crystal when it was one half-life old

After one half-life, half the starting uranium, <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mmol}" />, had already turned into lead, which is <InlineMath math="0.0400\times207=8.28\text{ mg}" />. **Lead piles up fastest at the start**, because that is when there is the most uranium to decay. Halving today's lead to <InlineMath math="6.21\text{ mg}" /> pretends it built up at a steady rate.
