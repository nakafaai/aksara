# The tie holds the sign up

Picture a broom handle sticking straight out from a wall, with a bag hanging on its end and a string running up to the wall. The string pulls up and in, and the handle pushes out. Here the wall, the rod and the tie make a <InlineMath math="0.90" />, <InlineMath math="1.2" />, <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m}" /> triangle, so the tie rises at an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> with <InlineMath math="\sin\theta=0.60" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos\theta=0.80" />. **Only the tie can hold the sign up, so its upward part carries all <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" />**: <InlineMath math="0.60T=30" /> and <InlineMath math="T=50\text{ N}" />.

# The rod pushes back

Lean on a wall with straight arms and your arms push back along their length. The rod does the same job. The tie also tugs the rod's end in towards the wall, and **the rod pushes outwards just as hard, <InlineMath math="0.80\times50=40\text{ N}" />**. That is under the <InlineMath math="45\text{ N}" /> the rod can take, so this sign is safe. Swapping sine and cosine gives <InlineMath math="38\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="23\text{ N}" />, as if the tie climbed at the steeper angle.

# Which part gives up first

A chain snaps at its weakest link, and a bracket is no different. Hang twice the weight and every force in the bracket doubles. **Each newton of sign puts <InlineMath math="1.33\text{ N}" /> of push on the rod and <InlineMath math="1.67\text{ N}" /> of pull on the tie**. The rod hits its <InlineMath math="45\text{ N}" /> with a sign of <InlineMath math="45/1.33=34\text{ N}" />, while the tie would last until <InlineMath math="100/1.67=60\text{ N}" />. The weaker part sets the limit, so the bracket holds at most <InlineMath math="34\text{ N}" />. A <InlineMath math="60\text{ N}" /> sign would buckle the rod long before the tie snapped, and <InlineMath math="45\text{ N}" /> is the rod's own limit, not a sign.
