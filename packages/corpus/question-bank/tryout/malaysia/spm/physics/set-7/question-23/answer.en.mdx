# Only the vertical part of the tie's pull holds the sign up

The light rod pushes horizontally, so the sign's weight is held by the **vertical component** of the tie's tension alone. The tie, the rod and the wall form a right-angled triangle, so the tie's angle above the horizontal has <InlineMath math="\sin\theta=\dfrac{0.90}{1.5}" />. The tension is proportional to the load, so the breaking tension sets the **largest load in the same ratio**.

<BlockMath math="\sin\theta=\frac{0.90}{1.5}=0.60,\qquad T\sin\theta=40\Rightarrow T=\frac{40}{0.60}\approx67\text{ N}" />

<BlockMath math="W_{\max}=100\times0.60=60\text{ N}" />

The tie pulls with about <InlineMath math="67\text{ N}" />, more than the sign's weight, because it is inclined. It can hold a sign of at most <InlineMath math="60\text{ N}" /> before reaching <InlineMath math="100\text{ N}" />.

<BlockMath math="P=T\cos\theta=67\times0.80\approx53\text{ N}=\tfrac43W,\qquad \text{rod: }\tfrac43W\le45\Rightarrow W\le34\text{ N}<60\text{ N}" />

The rod's push is four thirds of the sign's weight, so the rod reaches its <InlineMath math="45\text{ N}" /> limit when the sign weighs only about <InlineMath math="34\text{ N}" />. The rod gives way long before the tie does.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="60\text{ N}" /> checks **only the tie**, but at that load the rod would be pushed with <InlineMath math="80\text{ N}" /> and buckle.
- <InlineMath math="50\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" /> use the **cosine for the vertical component**, <InlineMath math="\dfrac{1.2}{1.5}" />, which swaps the roles of the two components.
- <InlineMath math="45\text{ N}" /> takes the rod's limit as the **sign's weight**, but the rod is pushed harder than the sign is heavy.
