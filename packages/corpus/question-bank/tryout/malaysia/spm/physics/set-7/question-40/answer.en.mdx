# Compare one photon with one bond, not with a mole of bonds

A photon's energy is <InlineMath math="E=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}" />. The bond energy is given **per mole**, so divide by Avogadro's number to get the energy of **one bond**. A photon breaks a bond only if its energy is at least that much. Because photon energy falls as wavelength grows, the **longest** useful wavelength is the one whose photon energy just equals one bond's energy.

Each <InlineMath math="254\text{ nm}" /> photon carries about <InlineMath math="7.8\times10^{-19}\text{ J}" />, enough to break a bond that needs about <InlineMath math="5.8\times10^{-19}\text{ J}" />. Light up to about <InlineMath math="340\text{ nm}" /> can still break such bonds, and longer wavelengths cannot, however bright.

<BlockMath math="N=\frac{P}{E}=\frac{5.0}{7.83\times10^{-19}}\approx6.4\times10^{18}\text{ s}^{-1}" />

Each second the lamp sends out <InlineMath math="5.0\text{ J}" /> in packets of <InlineMath math="7.83\times10^{-19}\text{ J}" />, about <InlineMath math="6.4\times10^{18}" /> photons, each able to break one bond.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="190\text{ nm}" /> scales the wavelength **the wrong way**: lower photon energy means a longer wavelength, not a shorter one.
- <InlineMath math="5.8\times10^{-19}\text{ J}" /> is the **energy of one bond**, not of one photon from the lamp, so the photon count comes out too high.
- <InlineMath math="254\text{ nm}" /> assumes only the **lamp's own wavelength** can break the bond, but its photons have energy to spare.
