# Surface gravity scales as mass over radius squared, and it sets both the air's weight and the barometer

From <InlineMath math="g=\dfrac{GM}{R^2}" />, the surface gravity scales with the **mass divided by the square of the radius**. Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air above each square metre, <InlineMath math="P=\dfrac{mg}{A}" />, so the mass of that air depends on Mars's own <InlineMath math="g" />. A barometer balances the air with a column of mercury, <InlineMath math="P=\rho gh" />, and the **same weaker gravity** pulls on the mercury too.

<BlockMath math="g_M=9.8\times\frac{0.107}{0.532^2}\approx3.7\text{ m s}^{-2},\qquad m=\frac{PA}{g_M}=\frac{600\times1}{3.7}\approx160\text{ kg}" />

<BlockMath math="h=\frac{P}{\rho g_M}=\frac{600}{13\,600\times3.7}\approx0.012\text{ m}=12\text{ mm}" />

Gravity on Mars is about <InlineMath math="3.7\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. About <InlineMath math="160\text{ kg}" /> of air presses on each square metre, and a mercury barometer there would stand only about <InlineMath math="12\text{ mm}" /> tall.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="4.5\text{ mm}" /> uses **Earth's gravity for the mercury**, but on Mars the column is pulled down by Mars's weaker gravity.
- <InlineMath math="61\text{ kg}" /> divides the pressure by **Earth's <InlineMath math="g" />**, but the air's weight comes from the gravity of the planet it sits on.
- <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ m s}^{-2}" /> divides by the radius **without squaring it**, and the slip carries into the air mass and the column height.
