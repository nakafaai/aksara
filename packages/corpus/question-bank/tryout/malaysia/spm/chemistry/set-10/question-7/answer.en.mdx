# Heating separates the water from the salt, and the anhydrous salt then sets both the sulfate precipitate and the silver nitrate

The water lost is <InlineMath math="2.44-2.08=0.36\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" />, and the anhydrous salt is <InlineMath math="2.08\div208=0.0100\text{ mol}" />, so **each formula unit carried two waters**, <InlineMath math="x=2" />. Each barium gives one barium sulfate, <InlineMath math="0.0100\times233=2.33\text{ g}" />. **Each formula unit carries two chloride ions**, so another <InlineMath math="2.08\text{ g}" /> holds <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of chloride, which needs <InlineMath math="0.0200\div0.100=0.200\text{ dm}^3" />, <InlineMath math="200\text{ cm}^3" /> of silver nitrate.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> gives one chloride ion per formula unit, but **<InlineMath math="\text{BaCl}_2" /> holds two**.
- <InlineMath math="2.73\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="235\text{ cm}^3" /> treat the <InlineMath math="2.44\text{ g}" /> of crystals as all barium chloride, forgetting the water in them.
- <InlineMath math="2.08\text{ g}" /> keeps the mass of the barium chloride for the precipitate, but barium sulfate is heavier per mole, <InlineMath math="M_r=233" />.
