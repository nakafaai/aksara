# The phosphorus runs out first, and each molecule it makes holds three shared pairs

The phosphorus is <InlineMath math="0.62\div31=0.0200\text{ mol}" /> and the chlorine <InlineMath math="840\div24\,000=0.0350\text{ mol}" />. **Two phosphorus take three chlorine**, so <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> needs only <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" /> and the phosphorus runs out. It makes <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of phosphorus trichloride, <InlineMath math="M_r=137.5" />, <InlineMath math="2.75\text{ g}" />, and leaves <InlineMath math="0.0050\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine, <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" />. **Each molecule has three <InlineMath math="\text{P–Cl}" /> bonds**, one shared pair each, so there are <InlineMath math="0.0600\times6.02\times10^{23}=3.61\times10^{22}" /> shared pairs.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="3.21\text{ g}" /> lets the chlorine decide, but <InlineMath math="0.0350\text{ mol}" /> of it needs more phosphorus than the <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> there.
- <InlineMath math="1.20\times10^{22}" /> counts one shared pair per molecule, but **each of the three chlorine atoms shares its own pair**.
- <InlineMath math="360\text{ cm}^3" /> uses one chlorine per phosphorus, but the equation takes three for every two.
