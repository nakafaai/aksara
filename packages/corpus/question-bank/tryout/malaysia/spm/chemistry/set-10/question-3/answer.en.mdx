# The relative atomic mass fixes the mix of isotopes, and the mix then sets both the neutrons and the mass of each isotope

With a fraction <InlineMath math="f" /> of <InlineMath math="^{10}\text{B}" />, <InlineMath math="10f+11(1-f)=10.8" />, so <InlineMath math="f=0.20" />, <InlineMath math="20\%" />. Boron has five protons, so **<InlineMath math="^{10}\text{B}" /> has five neutrons and <InlineMath math="^{11}\text{B}" /> six**, an average of <InlineMath math="0.20\times5+0.80\times6=5.8" />. The sample is <InlineMath math="2.16\div10.8=0.200\text{ mol}" /> of atoms, so it has <InlineMath math="0.200\times5.8=1.16\text{ mol}" /> of neutrons, <InlineMath math="6.98\times10^{23}" />. **The <InlineMath math="^{10}\text{B}" /> atoms are <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mol}" />**, each of mass <InlineMath math="10" />, so their mass is <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ g}" />, a smaller share of the mass than of the atoms.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="80\%" /> is the share of <InlineMath math="^{11}\text{B}" />. The mass <InlineMath math="10.8" /> sits closer to <InlineMath math="11" />, so <InlineMath math="^{11}\text{B}" /> is the common one.
- <InlineMath math="6.02\times10^{23}" /> gives every atom five neutrons, but **<InlineMath math="^{11}\text{B}" /> has six**.
- <InlineMath math="0.432\text{ g}" /> takes <InlineMath math="20\%" /> of the mass, but that <InlineMath math="20\%" /> is a share of atoms, and <InlineMath math="^{10}\text{B}" /> atoms are lighter.
