# The solid stage clocks the heater

Nothing tells you the heater's power, but the solid warming up does. **Below <InlineMath math="80^\circ\text{C}" /> every joule goes into raising the temperature**, <InlineMath math="20^\circ\text{C}" /> per minute, so the heater supplies <InlineMath math="12.8\times1.30\times20=332.8\text{ J}" /> each minute. The liquid climbs more slowly, <InlineMath math="15.3^\circ\text{C}" /> per minute, because liquid naphthalene stores heat differently. Feeding that slope into the solid's heat capacity undercounts the heater and ends at <InlineMath math="15.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />.

# Timing the flat part

You don't have to stretch either line, because the question tells you both ends. The first drop of liquid appears exactly at minute <InlineMath math="3" /> and the last crystal disappears exactly at minute <InlineMath math="9" />. So **the melting lasts six minutes, the gaps between readings, not the seven readings of <InlineMath math="80^\circ\text{C}" />**. Think of fence posts: seven posts have only six gaps between them. Counting the readings as minutes gives <InlineMath math="23.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />.

# From one sample to one mole

Six minutes at <InlineMath math="332.8\text{ J}" /> per minute is <InlineMath math="1997\text{ J}" /> for <InlineMath math="\tfrac{12.8}{128}=0.100\text{ mol}" />, which makes <InlineMath math="20.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
12.8\times1.30\times20&=332.8\text{ J}\\
\Delta H&=\tfrac{332.8\times(9-3)}{0.100}=19\,968\text{ J mol}^{-1}=20.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
\end{aligned}" />

**The temperature does not move while this energy goes in**, so the average kinetic energy of the molecules stays the same and the energy goes into overcoming the forces between them. Molecules speeding up would show as a rising temperature, which the flat line rules out.
