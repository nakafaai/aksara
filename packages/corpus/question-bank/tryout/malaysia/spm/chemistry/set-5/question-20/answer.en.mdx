# The cullet is already glass

Say you bake a big cake and stir in a slice of yesterday's cake. You only need fresh flour and eggs for the new part. Cullet is yesterday's cake. **Only the <InlineMath math="800\text{ g}" /> of new glass has to come from raw materials**, because the <InlineMath math="200\text{ g}" /> of cullet brings its own oxides. The new glass needs <InlineMath math="0.155\times800=124\text{ g}" /> of sodium oxide, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{124}{62}=2.00\text{ mol}" />. Each <InlineMath math="\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3" /> gives one <InlineMath math="\text{Na}_2\text{O}" />, so the batch takes <InlineMath math="2.00\times106=212\text{ g}" /> of sodium carbonate.

Run the numbers on the full <InlineMath math="1.00\text{ kg}" /> and the cullet gets charged for carbonate it never needed. So the sodium carbonate swells to <InlineMath math="265\text{ g}" /> and the gas to <InlineMath math="96.0\text{ dm}^3" />.

# Two carbonates, one gas

Heat a carbonate and it gives off carbon dioxide, a bit like a fizzy drink letting go of its bubbles. **Every carbonate that breaks down sends one carbon dioxide up the chimney**. The new glass also needs <InlineMath math="0.084\times800=67.2\text{ g}" /> of calcium oxide, <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ mol}" />, so <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ mol}" /> of limestone breaks down too.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{CO}_2)&=n(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3)+n(\text{CaCO}_3)=2.00+1.20=3.20\text{ mol}\\
V(\text{CO}_2)&=3.20\times24=76.8\text{ dm}^3
\end{aligned}" />
