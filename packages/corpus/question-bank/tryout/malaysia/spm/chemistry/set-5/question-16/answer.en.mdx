# Reading the tests one at a time

No gas with nitric acid rules out carbonate, a clear result with silver nitrate rules out chloride, and a clear result with barium chloride rules out sulfate. **The brown ring is the positive test for nitrate**, and a blue precipitate that dissolves in excess ammonia to a dark blue solution belongs to copper(II). So Y is some hydrate of copper(II) nitrate.

# The black residue weighs the formula

The black solid is copper(II) oxide, <InlineMath math="0.80\div80=0.0100\text{ mol}" />. **Every copper atom in it came from one formula unit of Y**, so <InlineMath math="2.42\text{ g}" /> is <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of Y and its formula mass is <InlineMath math="242" />. The anhydrous nitrate only accounts for <InlineMath math="188" />, and the missing <InlineMath math="54" /> is three waters. Writing the formula without water leaves <InlineMath math="0.54\text{ g}" /> of the sample unexplained.

# Which gases reach the measuring tube

Strong heating splits the nitrate, and the water of crystallisation leaves as steam. **The calcium chloride holds the water back**, so only <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of nitrogen dioxide and <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen are measured, <InlineMath math="600\text{ cm}^3" />. Letting the steam through as well gives <InlineMath math="1320\text{ cm}^3" />. Forgetting that one oxygen molecule comes off for every two copper atoms gives <InlineMath math="480\text{ cm}^3" />.
