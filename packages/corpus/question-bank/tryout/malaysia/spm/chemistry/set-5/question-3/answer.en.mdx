# Weigh the labelled formula, not the textbook one

The heavy isotopes change the mass of every formula unit. **With sulfur-34 and four oxygen-18 atoms, one <InlineMath math="\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4" /> weighs <InlineMath math="46+34+72=152" />**, not the usual <InlineMath math="142" />, so <InlineMath math="3.04\text{ g}" /> is exactly <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of sulfate ions.

# The precipitate keeps the heavy atoms

Barium ions simply collect each labelled sulfate ion, one to one, so <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of barium sulfate forms with <InlineMath math="M=137+34+72=243" />. **The label travels into the solid**, giving <InlineMath math="4.86\text{ g}" />. Using the ordinary <InlineMath math="233" /> for barium sulfate gives <InlineMath math="4.66\text{ g}" />, the mass the precipitate would have without the tracer.

Protons and electrons ignore isotopes. Sulfur brings <InlineMath math="16" /> electrons, four oxygens <InlineMath math="32" />, and **the <InlineMath math="2-" /> charge means two extra electrons**, so <InlineMath math="50" />. Forgetting the charge leaves <InlineMath math="48" />, the number of protons. Neutrons are where the label shows: <InlineMath math="34-16=18" /> in the sulfur and <InlineMath math="18-8=10" /> in each oxygen, <InlineMath math="58" /> per ion against <InlineMath math="48" /> in a natural one.
