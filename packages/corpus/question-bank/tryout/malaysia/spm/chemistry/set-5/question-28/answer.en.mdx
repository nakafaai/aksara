# Each chlorine molecule puts one chlorine atom on carbon and one in hydrogen chloride

In substitution, **a chlorine molecule swaps one hydrogen on the carbon for one chlorine atom**, and the other chlorine atom leaves with that hydrogen as hydrogen chloride. So the carbon gains <InlineMath math="0.25\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine atoms, shared between the two products, while every carbon atom ends up in one of them.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of trichloromethane would need <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine, more than there is.
- <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> gives two hydrogen chloride molecules for each chlorine molecule. **Half of each chlorine molecule stays on the carbon**.
- <InlineMath math="0.025" /> and <InlineMath math="0.075" /> need <InlineMath math="0.275\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine atoms on carbon, not <InlineMath math="0.25" />.
