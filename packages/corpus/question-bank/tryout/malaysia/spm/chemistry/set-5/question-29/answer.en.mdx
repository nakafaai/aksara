# Balance the electrode reaction first

Atoms and charge both have to balance. On the left there are eight hydrogen atoms, six in ethanol and two in water, and ethanoic acid keeps only four, so **four hydrogen ions leave and four electrons go with them**, <InlineMath math="x=4" />. An equation with <InlineMath math="x=2" /> loses two hydrogen atoms, halves the electrons per molecule and doubles every ethanol mass, which pushes all three drivers over.

# From the meter reading to micrograms

Driver Q's <InlineMath math="0.193\text{ C}" /> is <InlineMath math="2.00\times10^{-6}\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, so **a quarter of that, <InlineMath math="5.00\times10^{-7}\text{ mol}" />, is the ethanol**, <InlineMath math="23.0\ \mu\text{g}" />. That sat in <InlineMath math="50.0\text{ cm}^3" /> while the limit is written per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />, so Q's breath carries <InlineMath math="46.0\ \mu\text{g}" /> in every <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(e^-)&=\tfrac{0.193}{96\,500}=2.00\times10^{-6}\text{ mol},\quad n(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH})=\tfrac{2.00\times10^{-6}}{4}=5.00\times10^{-7}\text{ mol}\\
m&=2\times5.00\times10^{-7}\times46=4.60\times10^{-5}\text{ g}=46.0\ \mu\text{g}
\end{aligned}" />

Put <InlineMath math="23.0\ \mu\text{g}" /> straight next to the limit and you forget that the sample was only half the reference volume, so nobody looks guilty. Weighing the product, ethanoic acid at <InlineMath math="M_r=60" />, instead of the ethanol gives <InlineMath math="60.0\ \mu\text{g}" />.

# Three drivers against one line

The same steps turn P's charge into <InlineMath math="23.0\ \mu\text{g}" /> and R's into <InlineMath math="35.8\ \mu\text{g}" /> per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />. **R is over by less than a microgram**, which is exactly why the meter reports charge to three significant figures. Q and R are over the limit, and P is not.
