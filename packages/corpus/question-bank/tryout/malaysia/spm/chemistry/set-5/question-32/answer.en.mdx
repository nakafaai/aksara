# One chlorine molecule, one substitution

In ultraviolet light each chlorine molecule swaps one hydrogen atom on a carbon for a chlorine atom, and **the hydrogen it pulls off leaves as one hydrogen chloride**. So <InlineMath math="720\text{ cm}^3" />, or <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" />, of chlorine means exactly <InlineMath math="0.0300" /> substitutions and <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen chloride, whichever products form. Counting two hydrogen chlorides per chlorine molecule, because it has two chlorine atoms, forgets that the second atom ends up on the carbon.

# Sharing the methane between two products

All <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of methane reacted, so chloromethane and dichloromethane between them hold <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of carbon. Chloromethane cost one substitution and dichloromethane two, and **the substitutions add up to the chlorine used**. Those two facts fix the split at <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> each, so <InlineMath math="0.850\text{ g}" /> of dichloromethane forms. Sending every methane all the way to dichloromethane would give <InlineMath math="1.70\text{ g}" />, but that needs <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine, a third more than the flask held.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x+y&=0.0200,\quad x+2y=0.0300\ \Rightarrow\ y=0.0100\text{ mol}\\
m(\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2)&=0.0100\times85=0.850\text{ g},\quad [\text{H}^+]=\tfrac{0.0300}{0.300}=0.100\text{ mol dm}^{-3},\ \text{pH}=1.0
\end{aligned}" />

The <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen chloride in <InlineMath math="0.300\text{ dm}^3" /> of water gives <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> of hydrogen ions and a pH of <InlineMath math="1.0" />. Doubling the hydrogen chloride gives <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> and a pH of <InlineMath math="0.70" />.

The boiling points decide the last part. **Dichloromethane boils at <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />, so it is a liquid at room temperature**, while chloromethane at <InlineMath math="-24^\circ\text{C}" /> stays a gas. The water has already taken the hydrogen chloride, so only the <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of chloromethane bubbles out, <InlineMath math="240\text{ cm}^3" />. Treating both chloromethanes as gases doubles that to <InlineMath math="480\text{ cm}^3" />.
