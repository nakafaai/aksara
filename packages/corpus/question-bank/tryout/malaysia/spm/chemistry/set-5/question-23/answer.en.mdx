# What one cartridge can deliver

The cartridge is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6.00}{24}=0.250\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen, and the negative-terminal equation shows **every hydrogen molecule releases two electrons**. That is <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, a charge of <InlineMath math="48\,250\text{ C}" />. At <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ A}" /> the cell passes <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ C}" /> each second, so the hydrogen lasts <InlineMath math="193\,000\text{ s}" />, about <InlineMath math="53.6\text{ h}" />. With one electron per molecule the run would halve to <InlineMath math="26.8\text{ h}" />.

# Oxygen arrives diluted

The positive terminal must accept the same <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, and **an oxygen molecule takes four**, so the cell consumes <InlineMath math="0.125\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen, <InlineMath math="3.00\text{ dm}^3" />. Only a fifth of air is oxygen, so the cell draws in <InlineMath math="15.0\text{ dm}^3" /> of air. The <InlineMath math="3.00\text{ dm}^3" /> figure answers a different question, the volume of pure oxygen.

# Water made and water used

The negative terminal produces two water molecules per hydrogen, <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ mol}" /> or <InlineMath math="9.00\text{ g}" />. The positive terminal, though, **uses up two water molecules for every oxygen molecule it reduces**, <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol}" /> or <InlineMath math="4.50\text{ g}" />. What builds up is the difference, <InlineMath math="4.50\text{ g}" />.
