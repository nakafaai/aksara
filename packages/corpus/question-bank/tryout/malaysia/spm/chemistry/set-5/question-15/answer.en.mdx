# Two hydroxide ions in every packet

Think of each barium hydroxide as a little packet with two hydroxide sweets inside. Solution X holds <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1.71}{171}=0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of packets in <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ dm}^3" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. **Open the packets and you get twice as many hydroxide ions, <InlineMath math="[\text{OH}^-]=0.100\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />**. The pOH is <InlineMath math="1.0" />, so the pH is <InlineMath math="13.0" />. Take one sweet per packet instead and X comes out at <InlineMath math="12.7" />, which then drags the mixture down to <InlineMath math="12.4" />.

# A very weak partner

Solution Y is like one drop of cordial in a big jug. It holds <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0.0400}{40}=0.00100\text{ mol}" /> of sodium hydroxide in <InlineMath math="1.00\text{ dm}^3" />. **Its hydroxide ions are a hundred times scarcer than in X**, so its pOH is <InlineMath math="3.0" /> and its pH is <InlineMath math="11.0" />.

# Mixing adds ions, not pH values

Pour two cups of juice into one jug. To know how sweet the jug is, you add up the sugar from both cups and share it over the whole jug. You don't average the labels on the cups. Do the same with hydroxide ions.

So the mixture sits at pH <InlineMath math="14-1.3=12.7" />. **The pH scale jumps ten times for every step of one, so two pH values never average into the mixture's pH**. The middle of <InlineMath math="13.0" /> and <InlineMath math="11.0" /> is <InlineMath math="12.0" />, as if Y brought as many hydroxide ions as X. Adding the ions but keeping the volume at <InlineMath math="50.0\text{ cm}^3" /> is the other trap. The concentration stays near <InlineMath math="0.101\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> and the pH stays at <InlineMath math="13.0" />.
