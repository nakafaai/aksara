# Copper pays with two coins

Picture electrons as coins. Each copper atom pays two coins, but each iron(III) ion can only hold one. **So every copper atom needs two iron(III) ions to take its electrons**. The bath starts with <InlineMath math="2.00\times0.500=1.00\text{ mol}" /> of iron(III), and the copper is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{12.8}{64}=0.200\text{ mol}" />. That uses <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> of iron(III) and leaves <InlineMath math="0.600\text{ mol}" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.300\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

A one-for-one swap would leave <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> and halve every step after it, giving <InlineMath math="50.0\text{ cm}^3" /> and <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# One peroxide for two iron(II) ions

Hydrogen peroxide is the opposite of copper. It wants two coins, so it collects one from each of two iron(II) ions. **That means <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> of iron(II) needs only <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol}" /> of peroxide**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2)&=\tfrac{0.400}{2}=0.200\text{ mol}\\
V&=\tfrac{0.200}{2.00}=0.100\text{ dm}^3=100\text{ cm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

Give every iron(II) ion its own peroxide molecule and the volume doubles to <InlineMath math="200\text{ cm}^3" />.

# The acid gets used up too

The acid is not just watching. It is an ingredient, like the eggs in a cake, and it gets used. The equation spends two hydrogen ions for every two iron(II) ions. **That is one hydrogen ion per iron(II) ion, <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> in all**. Spread over <InlineMath math="2.00\text{ dm}^3" />, the hydrogen ion concentration falls by <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Reading <InlineMath math="2\text{H}^+" /> as two for each iron(II) ion counts <InlineMath math="0.800\text{ mol}" /> and a fall of <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.
