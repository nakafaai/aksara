# Half the gas is ammonia

Think of shoes. Forty shoes make twenty pairs, not forty. The gases pair up the same way, one ammonia with one boron trifluoride. The mixture is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0.960}{24}=0.0400\text{ mol}" /> of gas, so **there are only <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of each gas, and <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of solid forms**. Each joined molecule weighs <InlineMath math="M_r=17+68=85" />, so the solid comes to <InlineMath math="1.70\text{ g}" />. Call the whole <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mol}" /> solid and everything doubles, to <InlineMath math="3.40\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="4.33\times10^{23}" /> lone-pair electrons.

# The pair nitrogen lends

Nitrogen in ammonia carries a spare pair of electrons, like a spare umbrella. Boron in boron trifluoride has an empty spot, room for one more pair. When they meet, nitrogen holds its umbrella over both of them. **The spare pair is now shared, so it stops being a lone pair**. That is a dative bond.

So one molecule ends with seven bonding pairs, <InlineMath math="14" /> shared electrons, and nine lone pairs, all on the fluorine atoms, <InlineMath math="18" /> electrons. Leave that pair on nitrogen as a lone pair as well and you count <InlineMath math="20" /> lone-pair electrons and only <InlineMath math="12" /> shared ones. Count it that way and you land on <InlineMath math="2.41\times10^{23}" /> lone-pair electrons and only <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{7}" /> shared.

# Counting every electron

The question says all the electrons. That means the ones tucked deep inside each atom too, not only the outer ones. **Each molecule has <InlineMath math="1+1+1+7+5+9+9+9=42" /> electrons**. The inner shells of nitrogen, boron and the three fluorine atoms hold <InlineMath math="10" /> of them, and the other <InlineMath math="32" /> are valence electrons.

Dividing <InlineMath math="14" /> by <InlineMath math="32" /> gives <InlineMath math="\tfrac{7}{16}" />, but that is the share among the outer electrons only.
