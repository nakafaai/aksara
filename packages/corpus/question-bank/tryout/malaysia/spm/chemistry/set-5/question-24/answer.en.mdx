# Which reactant gives out first

The chlorine is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1.44}{24}=0.0600\text{ mol}" /> and the alkali <InlineMath math="0.100\times2.00=0.200\text{ mol}" />. The equation pairs **three chlorine molecules with six sodium hydroxide units, two for each**, so the chlorine needs only <InlineMath math="0.120\text{ mol}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.0800\text{ mol}" /> of sodium hydroxide survives, <InlineMath math="0.800\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> in the <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />. One sodium hydroxide per chlorine would leave <InlineMath math="0.140\text{ mol}" />, read as <InlineMath math="1.40\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Six chlorine atoms, two fates

Every chlorine atom starts at oxidation number zero. Five of the six end as chloride ions at <InlineMath math="-1" />, and one ends inside the chlorate(V) ion at <InlineMath math="+5" />, so **the same element is reduced and oxidised in one reaction**. The single atom loses five electrons and the five atoms gain one each, which is why the split has to be five to one.

Only <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{6}" /> of the chlorine atoms is oxidised. Five sixths is the share that is reduced, the bigger group but the opposite change.

# Chlorate from the chlorine

One chlorate(V) ion forms for every three chlorine molecules, so there is <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of **sodium chlorate(V), <InlineMath math="M_r=106.5" />**, which is <InlineMath math="2.13\text{ g}" />. Turning each chlorine molecule into its own chlorate gives <InlineMath math="6.39\text{ g}" />, three times too much, because it takes six chlorine atoms to make a single chlorate ion.

n(\text{NaOH})&=0.200-2\times0.0600=0.0800\text{ mol}\ \Rightarrow\ c=\tfrac{0.0800}{0.100}=0.800\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\\
m(\text{NaClO}_3)&=\tfrac{0.0600}{3}\times106.5=2.13\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />
