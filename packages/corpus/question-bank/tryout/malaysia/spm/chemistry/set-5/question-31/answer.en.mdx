# Splitting the fifteen millimoles

Pretend every molecule is propan-2-ol: the mixture would then use only <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of dichromate. The extra <InlineMath math="0.00300\text{ mol}" /> comes from propan-1-ol, and **each propan-1-ol molecule costs one third of a dichromate ion more than its isomer**, so there are <InlineMath math="0.00900\text{ mol}" /> of it.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{O})&=\tfrac{0.90}{60}=0.0150\text{ mol},\quad n(\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-})=0.0800\times0.100=0.00800\text{ mol}\\
\tfrac23x+\tfrac13(0.0150-x)&=0.00800\ \Rightarrow\ x=0.00900\text{ mol},\quad m=0.00900\times60=0.54\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />

Swap the two amounts and you get <InlineMath math="0.36\text{ g}" />, which is the propan-2-ol. Calling the whole <InlineMath math="0.90\text{ g}" /> propan-1-ol would need <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of dichromate, more than the burette delivered.

# Only one product is an acid

Propanoic acid releases hydrogen ions in water, so **only the product of propan-1-ol turns moist blue litmus red**. Propanone is a neutral ketone and leaves the paper blue, even though it also came out of an oxidation.
