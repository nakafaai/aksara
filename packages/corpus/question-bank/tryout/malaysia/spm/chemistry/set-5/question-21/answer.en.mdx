# The chloride runs out early

Twenty cubic centimetres of brine is not much, so start by counting what it holds: <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ dm}^3\times5.0=0.10\text{ mol}" /> of chloride ions. While chloride lasts it is discharged first at the anode, and **two electrons pass for every chlorine molecule**, so the first <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of electrons empties the solution of chloride and gives <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine, <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ dm}^3" />.

An anode reading of <InlineMath math="3.6\text{ dm}^3" /> pretends chlorine kept coming for all <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, from a solution that never had that much chloride.

# Then the anode switches to oxygen

The remaining <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ mol}" /> of electrons still has to leave the solution at the anode, and with the chloride gone **water itself is oxidised**, <InlineMath math="2\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow\text{O}_2+4\text{H}^++4e^-" />. Four electrons per oxygen molecule makes <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ mol}" />, another <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ dm}^3" />, so the anode collects <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ dm}^3" /> in total. The <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ dm}^3" /> row switches the current off in your head the moment the chloride is used up.

# The cathode, and what stays in the beaker

At the cathode nothing changes during the run. Water is reduced to hydrogen and hydroxide ions, **two electrons for every hydrogen molecule**, so <InlineMath math="0.15\text{ mol}" /> or <InlineMath math="3.6\text{ dm}^3" /> of hydrogen forms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{Cl}^-)&=0.0200\times5.0=0.10\text{ mol}\ \Rightarrow\ V(\text{Cl}_2)=\tfrac{0.10}{2}\times24=1.2\text{ dm}^3\\
V(\text{O}_2)&=\tfrac{0.30-0.10}{4}\times24=1.2\text{ dm}^3,\quad V(\text{H}_2)=\tfrac{0.30}{2}\times24=3.6\text{ dm}^3\\
n(\text{OH}^-)&=0.30-0.20=0.10\text{ mol}=n(\text{Na}^+)
\end{aligned}" />

The ion count settles the last part. The cathode made <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol}" /> of hydroxide ions and the oxygen step made <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen ions, which cancel the same amount of hydroxide. That leaves <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of hydroxide beside <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of sodium ions, a sodium hydroxide solution. An acidic answer counts the hydrogen ions from the oxygen step and forgets that the cathode made more hydroxide.
