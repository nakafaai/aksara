# Two readings, so twice the error

The cylinder never measures the volume of the metal directly. It measures two water levels, and **the volume is their difference, so the uncertainty of both readings adds**. In Measurement I that is <InlineMath math="6.0\pm1.0\text{ cm}^3" />, a <InlineMath math="16.7\%" /> uncertainty, while the <InlineMath math="\pm0.01\text{ g}" /> on the mass barely registers. The density <InlineMath math="9.05\text{ g cm}^{-3}" /> could really be anywhere from <InlineMath math="7.54" /> to <InlineMath math="10.56\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, wide enough for iron, nickel, copper and silver.

# Why the second piece is worth a cruder balance

Measurement II uses a rougher balance, yet it wins. The volume grows to <InlineMath math="15.0\pm1.0\text{ cm}^3" />, only <InlineMath math="6.7\%" />, and the <InlineMath math="\pm0.5\text{ g}" /> on <InlineMath math="135.8\text{ g}" /> adds just <InlineMath math="0.37\%" />. For a quotient such as mass over volume **the percentage uncertainties add**, giving <InlineMath math="7.0\%" />. Charging only one water reading, as if the starting level were exact, halves the volume part and lands on <InlineMath math="3.7\%" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\rho&=\tfrac{135.8}{65.0-50.0}=9.05\text{ g cm}^{-3},\quad \tfrac{1.0}{15.0}\times100+\tfrac{0.5}{135.8}\times100=6.67\%+0.37\%=7.0\%\\
\rho&=9.05\pm0.64\text{ g cm}^{-3}:\quad 8.42\le\rho\le9.69
\end{aligned}" />
