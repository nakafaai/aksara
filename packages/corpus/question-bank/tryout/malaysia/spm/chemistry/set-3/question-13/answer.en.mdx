# Two effects, one exception

Boiling a molecular substance separates molecules. It does **not break the covalent bonds inside them**, so the answer lies in the forces between molecules.

From <InlineMath math="\text{HCl}" /> to <InlineMath math="\text{HI}" /> the molecules get larger, the forces between them get stronger, and the boiling point rises from <InlineMath math="-85" /> to <InlineMath math="-35^\circ\text{C}" />. <InlineMath math="\text{HF}" /> breaks the trend at <InlineMath math="20^\circ\text{C}" />: fluorine is so electronegative and small that each hydrogen atom bonds to a fluorine atom of the next molecule. These **hydrogen bonds** are much stronger than the other forces.

# Why the other options are wrong

- The covalent bond in <InlineMath math="\text{HF}" /> stays **intact when it boils**, so its strength does not set the boiling point.
- <InlineMath math="\text{HF}" /> has the smallest molecules, yet it boils highest. Size alone cannot explain the pattern.
- <InlineMath math="\text{HI}" /> is a covalent molecule, and a gas at room temperature, not an ionic compound.
