# Balance the charges, then add the masses

Y has the arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8.2" />, so it is a metal that **loses two electrons** to form <InlineMath math="\text{Y}^{2+}" />. Z has the arrangement <InlineMath math="2.5" />, so it is a non-metal that **gains three electrons** to form <InlineMath math="\text{Z}^{3-}" />. A metal and a non-metal transfer electrons, so the bond is ionic.

The lowest common multiple of <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> is <InlineMath math="6" />, so three Y atoms give six electrons to two Z atoms. The formula is <InlineMath math="\text{Y}_3\text{Z}_2" />, and its relative formula mass is <InlineMath math="3(24) + 2(14) = 72 + 28 = 100" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{YZ}_2" /> treats the compound as if **electrons were shared**. A metal with two valence electrons does not share them with a non-metal.
- <InlineMath math="\text{Y}_2\text{Z}_3" /> swaps the charges: it gives a total of <InlineMath math="+4" /> against <InlineMath math="-9" />.
- The formula <InlineMath math="\text{Y}_3\text{Z}_2" /> and the mass are right, but the bond is ionic, not covalent.
