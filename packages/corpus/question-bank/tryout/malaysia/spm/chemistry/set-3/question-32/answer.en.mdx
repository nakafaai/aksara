# The salt names the ester and also counts it

Test the possible salts: sodium methanoate is <InlineMath math="68" />, sodium ethanoate, <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}" />, is <InlineMath math="15 + 44 + 23 = 82" />, and sodium propanoate is <InlineMath math="96" />. So the acid part is **ethanoate**, the other two carbons are the alcohol part, and the ester is ethyl ethanoate, <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5" />, <InlineMath math="M_r = 88" />.

The impurities hold no sodium, so the salt counts only the ester: <InlineMath math="3.69 \div 82 = 0.045" /> mol of salt from <InlineMath math="0.045" /> mol of ester.

Ester in the sample: <InlineMath math="0.045 \times 88 = 3.96" /> g, so the purity is <InlineMath math="3.96 \div 4.4 \times 100\% = 90\%" />. Each ester molecule gives one ethanol molecule: <InlineMath math="0.045 \times 46 = 2.07" /> g.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Methyl propanoate would give sodium propanoate, <InlineMath math="M_r = 96" />, **not** <InlineMath math="82" />.
- <InlineMath math="84\%" /> divides the salt's mass by the sample's, but the salt is a different compound with a different molar mass.
- <InlineMath math="2.30\text{ g}" /> treats the whole <InlineMath math="4.4" /> g as ester and ignores the impurities.
