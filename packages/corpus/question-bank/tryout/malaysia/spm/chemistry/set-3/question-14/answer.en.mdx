# Track the moles of hydrogen ions, not the pH

pH <InlineMath math="2.0" /> means <InlineMath math="[\text{H}^+] = 0.010\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, so <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> holds <InlineMath math="0.010 \times 0.100 = 0.0010" /> mol of hydrogen ions.

**Dilution**: pH <InlineMath math="3.0" /> needs <InlineMath math="0.0010\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, so the same <InlineMath math="0.0010" /> mol must fill <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ dm}^3" />. The sample is already <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />, so add <InlineMath math="900\text{ cm}^3" />.

**Alkali**: <InlineMath math="0.050 \times 0.010 = 0.00050" /> mol of hydroxide removes half the hydrogen ions, leaving <InlineMath math="0.00050" /> mol in <InlineMath math="150\text{ cm}^3" />: <InlineMath math="[\text{H}^+] = 0.00050 \div 0.150 = \tfrac{1}{300}" />. So <InlineMath math="\text{pH} = \log 300 = 2 + 0.48 = 2.48" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1000\text{ cm}^3" /> of water makes the total <InlineMath math="1100\text{ cm}^3" />, slightly **too much**, and pH <InlineMath math="2.30" /> forgets that the alkali adds volume.
- <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> of water only halves the concentration, which gives pH <InlineMath math="2.3" />, not <InlineMath math="3.0" />.
- pH <InlineMath math="7.0" /> needs equal moles of acid and alkali, but the alkali supplies only half.
