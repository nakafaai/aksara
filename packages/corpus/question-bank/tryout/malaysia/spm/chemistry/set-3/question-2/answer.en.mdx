# Find the new abundance first, then use the new formula mass

Let a fraction <InlineMath math="x" /> of the enriched atoms be lithium-6: <InlineMath math="6x + 7(1 - x) = 6.5" />, so <InlineMath math="x = 0.5" />. The natural <InlineMath math="7.5\%" /> **no longer applies**.

The enriched hydride has <InlineMath math="M_r = 6.5 + 1 = 7.5" />, so <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ g}" /> is <InlineMath math="3.0 \div 7.5 = 0.40" /> mol of <InlineMath math="\text{LiH}" />. Half of its lithium atoms are lithium-6: <InlineMath math="0.20" /> mol, or <InlineMath math="0.20 \times 6 = 1.20" /> g.

With water, **each formula unit gives one hydrogen molecule**: <InlineMath math="0.40" /> mol, or <InlineMath math="0.40 \times 24 = 9.6\text{ dm}^3" />. The hydrogen comes from the hydride ion and the water, so the isotope of lithium does not change it, but the molar mass of the hydride does.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.18\text{ g}" /> uses the **natural** abundance of <InlineMath math="7.5\%" /> for a sample that has been enriched.
- <InlineMath math="1.13\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="9.1\text{ dm}^3" /> both use the natural molar mass of the hydride, about <InlineMath math="7.9" />, instead of <InlineMath math="7.5" />.
- <InlineMath math="4.8\text{ dm}^3" /> makes one hydrogen molecule from two formula units, but the equation is one to one.
