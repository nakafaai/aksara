# One bromine molecule per molecule of X gives the molar mass

Bromine: <InlineMath math="M_r(\text{Br}_2) = 160" />, so <InlineMath math="2.0 \div 160 = 0.0125" /> mol. Each molecule of X takes **one bromine molecule**, so there were <InlineMath math="0.0125" /> mol of X, and <InlineMath math="M_r = 0.70 \div 0.0125 = 56" />.

One double bond makes X an alkene, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}" />: <InlineMath math="14n = 56" />, so <InlineMath math="n = 4" /> and X is <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_8" />.

Alkene structures of <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_8" />: the double bond at the end of a straight chain, **but-1-ene**, in the middle of it, **but-2-ene**, or on a branched chain, **2-methylpropene**. That makes three.

**Hydrogen**: it adds across the same single double bond as bromine, **one molecule per molecule of X**: <InlineMath math="0.0125" /> mol, or <InlineMath math="0.0125 \times 24\,000 = 300\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="150\text{ cm}^3" /> with <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_8" /> adds one hydrogen molecule to two molecules of X, but each double bond takes one.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_8\text{H}_{16}" /> comes from taking **two bromine molecules** per molecule of X, which doubles the molar mass and halves the hydrogen.
- Two isomers leave out the branched one, 2-methylpropene.
