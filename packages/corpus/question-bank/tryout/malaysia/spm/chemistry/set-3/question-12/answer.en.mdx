# Charges give the nitride, two equations give the masses

Lithium, <InlineMath math="2.1" />, loses one electron to form <InlineMath math="\text{Li}^+" />. Nitrogen, <InlineMath math="2.5" />, gains three to form <InlineMath math="\text{N}^{3-}" />. **Three lithium ions balance one nitride ion**, so the formula is <InlineMath math="\text{Li}_3\text{N}" />, with <InlineMath math="M_r = 35" />.

Let <InlineMath math="a" /> mol be <InlineMath math="\text{Li}_2\text{O}" /> (<InlineMath math="M_r = 30" />) and <InlineMath math="b" /> mol be <InlineMath math="\text{Li}_3\text{N}" />. Lithium: <InlineMath math="2a + 3b = 1.40 \div 7 = 0.20" />. Mass: <InlineMath math="30a + 35b = 2.80" />.

From the first, <InlineMath math="a = 0.10 - 1.5b" />. Then <InlineMath math="3.0 - 45b + 35b = 2.80" />, so <InlineMath math="b = 0.020" /> and <InlineMath math="a = 0.070" />. Lithium oxide: <InlineMath math="0.070 \times 30 = 2.10" /> g. Lithium nitride: <InlineMath math="0.020 \times 35 = 0.70" /> g.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Equal masses of <InlineMath math="1.40" /> g would use <InlineMath math="0.093 + 0.12 = 0.21" /> mol of lithium, **more than** the <InlineMath math="0.20" /> mol burnt.
- <InlineMath math="0.70" /> g and <InlineMath math="2.10" /> g swaps the two answers to the equations.
- <InlineMath math="\text{LiN}_3" /> would need each nitrogen to carry a charge of <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{1}{3}" />.
