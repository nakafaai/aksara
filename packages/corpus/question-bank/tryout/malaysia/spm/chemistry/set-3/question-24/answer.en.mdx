# The anode material decides the anode reaction

Both cathodes receive the same ions, and silver is low in the electrochemical series, so **silver is deposited at both cathodes**: <InlineMath math="\text{Ag}^+ + e^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}" />.

At the carbon anode in X, hydroxide ions discharge in place of nitrate ions: <InlineMath math="4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^-" />. Hydrogen ions are left behind, so the solution turns acidic. In Y, the silver anode itself **gives up electrons and dissolves**: <InlineMath math="\text{Ag} \rightarrow \text{Ag}^+ + e^-" />. Each silver ion that leaves at the cathode is replaced at the anode, so the concentration does not change.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Silver forms by **gaining electrons**, so it appears at the cathode, never at the anode. Y's anode dissolves rather than giving oxygen.
- The solution in X turns acidic, not alkaline, and the cathode in Y grows thicker rather than dissolving.
- Nitrate ions are not discharged in aqueous solution, and Y's anode wears away rather than growing.
