# The cathode sets the electrons, the anode decides what they do

In both cells, <InlineMath math="1.08 \div 108 = 0.010" /> mol of silver is deposited, and <InlineMath math="\text{Ag}^+ + e^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}" /> takes one electron each: <InlineMath math="0.010" /> mol of electrons.

**Cell X**: carbon is inert, and hydroxide ions or water are discharged in place of nitrate ions: <InlineMath math="2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-" />. Oxygen: <InlineMath math="0.010 \div 4 = 0.0025" /> mol, or <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" />. The same reaction leaves <InlineMath math="0.010" /> mol of hydrogen ions in <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ dm}^3" />: <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, pH <InlineMath math="1" />.

**Cell Y**: the silver anode itself dissolves, <InlineMath math="\text{Ag} \rightarrow \text{Ag}^+ + e^-" />, losing <InlineMath math="1.08" /> g. Every silver ion removed at the cathode is replaced at the anode, so the solution stays at <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> gives each oxygen molecule two electrons, but it takes **four**.
- Discharging water at the anode releases hydrogen ions, so the solution turns acidic, **not alkaline**.
- In Y the anode is where silver dissolves, so it gets lighter, and the silver ion concentration does not change.
