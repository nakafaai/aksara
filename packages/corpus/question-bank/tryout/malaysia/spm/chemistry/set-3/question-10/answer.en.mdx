# One formula unit of the oxide gives two hydroxide ions

The acid used: <InlineMath math="0.0200 \times 1.00 = 0.020" /> mol, so the solution held <InlineMath math="0.020" /> mol of hydroxide ions. The oxide reacts as <InlineMath math="\text{M}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{MOH}" />, so **each formula unit gives two** hydroxide ions: <InlineMath math="0.010" /> mol of oxide.

<InlineMath math="M_r = 0.62 \div 0.010 = 62" />, so <InlineMath math="2M + 16 = 62" /> and <InlineMath math="M = 23" />: sodium.

In <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ dm}^3" />, the hydroxide is <InlineMath math="0.020 \div 0.500 = 0.040\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. <InlineMath math="\text{pOH} = -\log 0.040 = 2 - 0.60 = 1.40" />, so <InlineMath math="\text{pH} = 14 - 1.40 = 12.6" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Lithium comes from taking one hydroxide ion per formula unit: <InlineMath math="0.020" /> mol of oxide gives <InlineMath math="M_r = 31" /> and <InlineMath math="M = 7.5" />, which **forgets the two M atoms** in <InlineMath math="\text{M}_2\text{O}" />.
- pH <InlineMath math="1.4" /> is the pOH, but a metal oxide gives an alkaline solution.
- Potassium oxide, <InlineMath math="M_r = 94" />, would need <InlineMath math="0.94" /> g for the same titre.
