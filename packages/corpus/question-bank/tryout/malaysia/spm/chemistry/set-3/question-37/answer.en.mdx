# Count whole chains, then feed for the losses

Each **chain** is one molecule of mass <InlineMath math="84\,000" />, so <InlineMath math="2100\text{ g}" /> is <InlineMath math="2100 \div 84\,000 = 0.025" /> mol of molecules: <InlineMath math="0.025 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.51 \times 10^{22}" />. Each chain holds <InlineMath math="84\,000 \div 42 = 2000" /> propene units.

Addition polymerisation **adds no atoms and loses none**, so <InlineMath math="2.1" /> kg of polymer came from <InlineMath math="2.1" /> kg of propene that reacted. That is only <InlineMath math="75\%" /> of the feed: <InlineMath math="2.1 \div 0.75 = 2.80" /> kg.

**Burning**: the polymer holds the same atoms as <InlineMath math="2100 \div 42 = 50" /> mol of propene units, <InlineMath math="(\text{C}_3\text{H}_6)_n" />, and **each unit has three carbon atoms**. So burning gives <InlineMath math="50 \times 3 = 150" /> mol of carbon dioxide, <InlineMath math="150 \times 24 = 3600\text{ dm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.58\text{ kg}" /> multiplies by <InlineMath math="0.75" />, which would feed in less propene than ends up in the polymer.
- <InlineMath math="1200\text{ dm}^3" /> gives one carbon dioxide per propene unit, but each unit carries **three** carbon atoms.
- <InlineMath math="3.01 \times 10^{25}" /> counts propene units, not whole molecules, and <InlineMath math="4800\text{ dm}^3" /> burns the propene fed in, though a quarter of it never became polymer.
