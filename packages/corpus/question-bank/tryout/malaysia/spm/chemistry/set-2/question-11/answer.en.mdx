# Find what runs out in each run, divide Experiment II's volume by its shortened time, and titrate a tenth of what it leaves

In Experiment I the magnesium and the acid are both <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" />, but **each magnesium takes two acid**, so the acid runs out, <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen, <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" />. In Experiment II **the magnesium runs out instead**, <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" />, again <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" />. Doubling the concentration and the extra <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> make it **four times as fast**, <InlineMath math="60\div4=15\text{ s}" />, so <InlineMath math="120\div15=8.00\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-1}" />. The magnesium uses <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of the <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of acid, a portion holds a tenth of the <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> left, <InlineMath math="0.00100\text{ mol}" />, and <InlineMath math="0.00100\div0.0200=0.0500\text{ dm}^3=50.0\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="240\text{ cm}^3" /> lets all the magnesium in Experiment I react, but <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of acid dissolves only half of it.
- <InlineMath math="4.00\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-1}" /> counts only the concentration, but **the higher temperature doubles the rate again**.
- <InlineMath math="75.0\text{ cm}^3" /> lets each magnesium use one acid, but the equation uses two.
