# Reading the two peaks

The peaks stand <InlineMath math="100" /> and <InlineMath math="33.3" /> high, so **three chlorine-35 atoms come with every chlorine-37 atom**. That is <InlineMath math="75\%" /> and <InlineMath math="25\%" />, and the weighted mean is <InlineMath math="35.5" />. Averaging the two masses without their abundances gives <InlineMath math="36.0" />, which treats the isotopes as equally common.

# Which gas is used up

Hydrogen is <InlineMath math="2.40\div24=0.100\text{ mol}" /> and chlorine <InlineMath math="0.150\text{ mol}" />, and they react one to one, so **the hydrogen runs out** and <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen chloride forms. Each molecule has one chlorine atom, and a quarter of those atoms are chlorine-37. Counting a quarter of the chlorine atoms in all <InlineMath math="0.150\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine gives <InlineMath math="4.52\times10^{22}" />, which pretends every chlorine molecule reacted.

# What the leftover chlorine frees

The <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine left frees the same amount of iodine molecules, and **an iodine molecule has <InlineMath math="M_r=254" />**. Using <InlineMath math="127" /> for one iodine atom halves the mass to <InlineMath math="6.35\text{ g}" />.
