# Alloying changes how layers slide, not the kind of bonding

A smaller dent means a harder metal: bronze averages <InlineMath math="2.1\text{ mm}" /> against <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ mm}" /> for copper. **Both are still held by metallic bonding**: positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.

- I is correct: tin atoms are larger than copper atoms, so they break up the regular layers and stop them sliding easily.
- II is wrong: copper is held by metallic bonding, not covalent bonds. It dents easily because its identical atoms form layers that slide.
- III is correct: in <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" />, copper is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{90}{64} = 1.41" /> mol and tin <InlineMath math="\dfrac{10}{119} = 0.084" /> mol, so tin is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{0.084}{1.49} = 0.056" />, about <InlineMath math="1" /> atom in <InlineMath math="18" />. So few foreign atoms are enough to jam the layers.
- IV is wrong: density does not decide hardness, and bronze is not denser than copper in any way that matters here.

So I and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only accepts II, **calling metallic bonding covalent**, and rejects the atom count.
- II and IV only rejects the real cause, the disrupted layers, and accepts two false reasons.
- I, III and IV only accepts IV, giving density the credit that belongs to the disrupted layers.
