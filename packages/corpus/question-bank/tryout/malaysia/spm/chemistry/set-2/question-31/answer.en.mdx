# More carbon for the same oxygen means more soot

Soot is **unburnt carbon**. A fuel that is richer in carbon needs more oxygen to burn completely, so in open air more of its carbon is left unburnt.

- Hexane, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}" />: <InlineMath math="\dfrac{72}{86} \times 100\% = 83.7\%" /> carbon.
- Hexene, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}" />: <InlineMath math="\dfrac{72}{84} \times 100\% = 85.7\%" /> carbon.

Hexene has two fewer hydrogen atoms for the same six carbons, so a larger share of its mass is carbon, and its flame is sootier.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Both molecules have **exactly six carbon atoms**.
- Boiling point does not decide how completely a fuel burns.
- Decolourising bromine water shows the double bond, but it does not explain the soot.
