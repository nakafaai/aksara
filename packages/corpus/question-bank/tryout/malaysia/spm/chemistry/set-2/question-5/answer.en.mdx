# The spark stops when the oxygen is gone

Sixty cubic centimetres of hydrogen sulfide would need <InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen, and only <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> is there. So **oxygen is the limiting gas**: it burns <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of hydrogen sulfide into <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of sulfur dioxide and leaves <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> unburnt. Equal volumes of gases hold equal amounts, so the volumes can be used like moles here.

# The leftover gas destroys some product

The unburnt hydrogen sulfide now reacts with the sulfur dioxide that the first reaction made. Two volumes of it use one volume of sulfur dioxide, so <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}^3" /> disappears and <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}^3" /> remains. Stopping after the first reaction leaves <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of gas, which forgets this second step.

The sulfur comes from both reactants: **two hydrogen sulfide and one sulfur dioxide give three sulfur atoms**. Taking one sulfur per hydrogen sulfide gives <InlineMath math="0.0267\text{ g}" /> instead.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
V(\text{H}_2\text{S})_{\text{burnt}}&=\tfrac{2}{3}\times60=40\text{ cm}^3\ \Rightarrow\ V(\text{SO}_2)=40\text{ cm}^3,\quad V(\text{H}_2\text{S})'=20\text{ cm}^3\\
V(\text{SO}_2)'&=40-\tfrac{20}{2}=30\text{ cm}^3,\quad m(\text{S})=\tfrac{3}{2}\times\tfrac{20}{24\,000}\times32=0.0400\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />
