# The oxygen runs out first and leaves hydrogen sulfide, which then eats into the sulfur dioxide it helped make

The hydrogen sulfide would need <InlineMath math="60\times\tfrac{3}{2}=90\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen, so **the oxygen runs out**, burning <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of it to <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of sulfur dioxide and leaving <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" />. In the second reaction **those <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> take <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}^3" /> of sulfur dioxide**, so <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}^3" /> of gas is left. The sulfur is three per two hydrogen sulfide, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3}{2}\times\tfrac{20}{24\,000}=1.25\times10^{-3}\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ g}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> lets all the hydrogen sulfide burn, but it would need <InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen and only <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> is there.
- <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> at the end forgets that the hydrogen sulfide left **uses up <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}^3" /> of the sulfur dioxide**.
- <InlineMath math="0.0267\text{ g}" /> gives one sulfur per hydrogen sulfide, but the equation gives three for every two.
