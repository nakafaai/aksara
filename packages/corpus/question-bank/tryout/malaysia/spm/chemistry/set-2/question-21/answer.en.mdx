# Put every metal on one scale against copper

Copper is the positive terminal in three cells, so P, Q and R sit below copper in the electrochemical series, by <InlineMath math="2.70" />, <InlineMath math="0.40" /> and <InlineMath math="1.10\text{ V}" />. In the last cell S is the positive terminal, so **S sits above copper**, by <InlineMath math="0.46\text{ V}" />. The highest voltage comes from the two metals that stand farthest apart on that scale.

On one scale with copper at zero, P is the lowest metal and S is the only one above copper.

P is lower in the series, so P gives up electrons and is the negative terminal. So P and S give <InlineMath math="3.16\text{ V}" />, with P as the negative terminal.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.24\text{ V}" /> subtracts <InlineMath math="0.46" /> from <InlineMath math="2.70" />, as if S were also below copper. **The terminal column puts S on the other side of copper**.
- <InlineMath math="3.16\text{ V}" /> with S as the negative terminal reverses the roles: the metal lower in the series, P, gives up the electrons.
- P and Q at <InlineMath math="2.30\text{ V}" /> reads the S cell as if copper were its positive terminal too, so Q looks farther from P than S does.
