# Empirical formula from combustion, molar mass from the vapour

The combustion data give the **ratio of atoms**, and the vapour volume gives **how many of those units make one molecule**. Oxygen in P comes by difference, because the burning also used oxygen from the air.

- Carbon: <InlineMath math="0.44 \times \dfrac{12}{44} = 0.12\text{ g}" />. Hydrogen: <InlineMath math="0.18 \times \dfrac{2}{18} = 0.02\text{ g}" />. Oxygen: <InlineMath math="0.30 - 0.12 - 0.02 = 0.16\text{ g}" />.

- Moles: C <InlineMath math="0.01" />, H <InlineMath math="0.02" />, O <InlineMath math="0.01" />, so the empirical formula is <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2\text{O}" />, mass <InlineMath math="30" />.
- Vapour: <InlineMath math="\dfrac{0.120}{24} = 0.0050\text{ mol}" />, so <InlineMath math="M_r = \dfrac{0.30}{0.0050} = 60" />.
- <InlineMath math="\dfrac{60}{30} = 2" />, so the molecular formula is <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2\text{O}" /> is the empirical formula: it **stops before using the vapour volume**.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2" /> counts one hydrogen atom per water molecule instead of two.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_3" /> takes three empirical units without checking the molar mass: its <InlineMath math="M_r" /> would be <InlineMath math="90" />, but the vapour gives <InlineMath math="60" />.
