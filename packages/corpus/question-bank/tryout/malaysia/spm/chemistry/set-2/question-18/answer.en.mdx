# Follow the trend of the three heavier hydrides, then see how far water breaks it

Boiling a simple molecular substance overcomes the **forces between molecules**, not the covalent bonds inside them. Larger molecules attract each other more strongly, so the boiling point rises with molecular size.

From <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{S}" /> to <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{Se}" /> to <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{Te}" />, the boiling point rises from <InlineMath math="-60" /> to <InlineMath math="-41" /> to <InlineMath math="-2^\circ\text{C}" />. Water is the smallest molecule, so on that trend alone it would boil below <InlineMath math="-60^\circ\text{C}" />. It boils at <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> because oxygen is small and very electronegative, so water molecules form hydrogen bonds that the other three cannot.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Boiling does not break covalent bonds. <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{Te}" /> boils highest because **its molecules are the largest**.
- Water is the lightest of the four molecules, with <InlineMath math="M_r = 18" />.
- The <InlineMath math="\text{O-H}" /> bonds stay intact when water boils: steam is still made of water molecules.
