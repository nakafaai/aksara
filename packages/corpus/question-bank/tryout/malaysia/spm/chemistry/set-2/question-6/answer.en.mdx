# Two oxides, two opposite solutions

Sodium oxide is basic and sulfur trioxide acidic, so the two solutions neutralise each other before anything else. Sodium oxide, <InlineMath math="M_r=62" />, is <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" />, and **its two sodium ions make two sodium hydroxide**, <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" />. With only one per oxide the acid would cancel the alkali exactly, which is where pH <InlineMath math="7.0" /> comes from. Sulfur trioxide, <InlineMath math="M_r=80" />, gives <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of sulfuric acid.

# What the acid leaves behind

Sulfuric acid is dibasic, so <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of it removes <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of hydroxide ions and leaves <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> in <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" />. The chart follows that fall: **without the acid the pH would be <InlineMath math="12.6" />**, and letting each acid take one hydroxide stops it near <InlineMath math="12.5" />.
