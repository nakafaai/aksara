Treat the acid as giving one hydrogen ion per molecule and it becomes the smaller side: <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol}" /> of water, <InlineMath math="2.86\text{ kJ}" />, <InlineMath math="6.81^\circ\text{C}" />, and nothing left for the magnesium.

# Heat spread through the whole cup

Each mole of water releases <InlineMath math="57.2\text{ kJ}" />, so <InlineMath math="0.0750\text{ mol}" /> gives <InlineMath math="4.29\text{ kJ}" />. **That heat warms both solutions together**, <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> or <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" />, so the rise is <InlineMath math="10.2^\circ\text{C}" />. Heating only the <InlineMath math="50\text{ g}" /> of acid doubles it to <InlineMath math="20.4^\circ\text{C}" />.

# What the magnesium finds

The <InlineMath math="0.0250\text{ mol}" /> of spare hydrogen ions attack the ribbon, and **each magnesium atom needs two of them** to become <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" /> and release one hydrogen molecule. So <InlineMath math="0.0125\text{ mol}" /> of magnesium dissolves. Letting a single hydrogen ion dissolve a whole magnesium atom gives <InlineMath math="0.600\text{ g}" /> and leaves the charges unbalanced.

m(\text{Mg})&=\tfrac{0.100-0.0750}{2}\times24=0.300\text{ g}
