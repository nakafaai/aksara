# Carbon atoms tell the two fuels apart

After cooling, the water is liquid, so the alkali only meets carbon dioxide and leftover oxygen. For gases at the same conditions, volumes count molecules, and **a methane molecule carries one carbon atom while a propane molecule carries three**.

The <InlineMath math="2.88\text{ dm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide from <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ dm}^3" /> of fuel means <InlineMath math="2.40" /> carbon atoms per fuel molecule on average. That sits <InlineMath math="70\%" /> of the way from one to three, so <InlineMath math="70\%" /> of the fuel is propane, <InlineMath math="0.840\text{ dm}^3" />. The <InlineMath math="0.360\text{ dm}^3" /> choice is the methane's share.

# Oxygen the flame leaves behind

Propane is the greedier fuel, **five oxygen per molecule against two for methane**. Together they burn <InlineMath math="4.92\text{ dm}^3" /> and leave <InlineMath math="1.08\text{ dm}^3" />. Taking <InlineMath math="2.88\text{ dm}^3" /> off the oxygen gives <InlineMath math="3.12\text{ dm}^3" />, but the carbon dioxide volume says nothing directly about the oxygen used.

# Magnesium mops up what is left

Each oxygen molecule turns two magnesium atoms into oxide, so **<InlineMath math="0.0450\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen makes <InlineMath math="0.0900\text{ mol}" /> of magnesium oxide**. One oxide per oxygen molecule gives half the mass, <InlineMath math="1.80\text{ g}" />, and the <InlineMath math="10.4\text{ g}" /> choice carries the <InlineMath math="3.12\text{ dm}^3" /> slip into this step.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1+2p&=\tfrac{2.88}{1.20}=2.40\ \Rightarrow\ p=0.700,\quad V(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8)=0.700\times1.20=0.840\text{ dm}^3\\
V(\text{O}_2)'&=6.00-(2\times0.360+5\times0.840)=1.08\text{ dm}^3\\
m(\text{MgO})&=2\times\tfrac{1.08}{24}\times40=3.60\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />

The magnesium never touches the fuel, so the burning matters here only through the oxygen it leaves.
