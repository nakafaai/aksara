# Find which solution runs out, then carry the precipitate through the heating, where it loses carbon dioxide

The copper(II) ions are <InlineMath math="0.0500\times0.200=0.0100\text{ mol}" /> and the carbonate <InlineMath math="0.0400\times0.300=0.0120\text{ mol}" />, so **the copper runs out**, giving <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) carbonate, <InlineMath math="M_r=123.5" />, <InlineMath math="1.24\text{ g}" />. On heating **each carbonate leaves one oxide and one carbon dioxide**, <InlineMath math="0.0100\times79.5=0.795\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.0100\times24\,000=240\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.48\text{ g}" /> lets the carbonate decide, but only <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) ions is there to take it.
- <InlineMath math="480\text{ cm}^3" /> gives two carbon dioxide per carbonate, but <InlineMath math="\text{CuCO}_3" /> holds one carbonate ion.
- <InlineMath math="0.635\text{ g}" /> weighs the residue as copper metal, but **heating leaves copper(II) oxide**.
