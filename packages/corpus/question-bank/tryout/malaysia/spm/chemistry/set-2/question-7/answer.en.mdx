# Doubly charged ions pull on each other much harder

Melting an ionic solid means **overcoming the electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions**. The larger the charges, the stronger those forces.

Sodium chloride is built from <InlineMath math="\text{Na}^+" /> and <InlineMath math="\text{Cl}^-" />, each with a charge of <InlineMath math="1" />. Magnesium oxide is built from <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" /> and <InlineMath math="\text{O}^{2-}" />, each with a charge of <InlineMath math="2" />, and the ions are also smaller and closer together. Much more heat is needed to pull them apart, so it melts at <InlineMath math="2852^\circ\text{C}" /> instead of <InlineMath math="801^\circ\text{C}" />. The chart shows the same pattern for the other pair: calcium oxide melts far above potassium chloride.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Magnesium oxide is ionic, not covalent: **a metal and a non-metal transfer electrons**.
- Delocalised electrons belong to metallic bonding, not to an ionic lattice.
- The reactivity of the metal does not set the strength of the forces in its compound.
