# Same cation, so look at the anion

Both compounds contain <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" />, so the difference must come from the anion. The attraction between ions grows with **the charges of both ions and how close they can get**.

- The oxide ion, <InlineMath math="\text{O}^{2-}" />, has a charge of <InlineMath math="2" />, while the chloride ion, <InlineMath math="\text{Cl}^-" />, has a charge of <InlineMath math="1" />.
- The oxide ion is also smaller, with two filled shells against three for chloride, so it sits closer to each magnesium ion.

Both effects make the electrostatic forces in magnesium oxide much stronger, so far more heat is needed to break up its lattice: <InlineMath math="2852^\circ\text{C}" /> against <InlineMath math="714^\circ\text{C}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Magnesium oxide is ionic: a metal and a non-metal **transfer electrons**, they do not share them.
- Magnesium chloride, <InlineMath math="\text{MgCl}_2" />, has three ions per formula unit and magnesium oxide only two. A count of ions does not decide the melting point.
- The magnesium ion is <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" /> in both compounds, so its charge cannot explain the difference.
