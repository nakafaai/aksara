The lab runs that charge through pure copper. There the anode loses what the cathode gains and the blue colour stays put. An impure anode breaks both, and the rest of this question is about how much.

# Zinc takes a share of the electrons

Zinc is more reactive than copper, so it dissolves alongside the copper. Its <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> supplies <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, and **copper supplies only the other <InlineMath math="0.180\text{ mol}" />**, which is <InlineMath math="0.0900\text{ mol}" /> of atoms or <InlineMath math="5.76\text{ g}" />. Writing <InlineMath math="6.40\text{ g}" /> for the copper dissolved pretends the anode were pure.

# What the anode's lost mass is made of

The <InlineMath math="7.00\text{ g}" /> left the anode three ways: copper into the solution, zinc into the solution, and silver to the bottom of the cell. **Whatever is not copper or zinc is sludge**, so <InlineMath math="0.590\text{ g}" />. Calling <InlineMath math="1.24\text{ g}" /> the sludge piles the zinc in with the silver, yet zinc ions are dissolved, not lying under the anode.

# Why the blue fades a little

The anode put <InlineMath math="0.0900\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) ions into the solution while the cathode pulled <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" /> out. **The missing <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> is the zinc's share**, and zinc ions now fill those places. In <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ dm}^3" /> the copper(II) ions drop from <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol}" /> to <InlineMath math="0.240\text{ mol}" />.
