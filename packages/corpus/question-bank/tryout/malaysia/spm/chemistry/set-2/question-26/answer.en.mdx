# The same electrons, two different anodes

In series, both cells pass the same <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of electrons. The cathodes behave alike, but the anodes do not: a copper anode dissolves and replaces every copper(II) ion it loses, while **a carbon anode only discharges hydroxide ions**.

Run both cells in the lab and compare the two solutions.

The <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ mol}" /> of copper deposited in Q is every copper(II) ion it held, and nothing replaces them, so the blue colour disappears. So the anode of P loses <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ g}" />, and the solution in Q turns colourless as oxygen forms at its anode.

# Why the other options are wrong

- The blue fading in P swaps the cells: in P the copper anode keeps the copper(II) ions at the same level.
- <InlineMath math="6.4\text{ g}" /> takes the <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of electrons as moles of copper. **Each copper(II) ion needs two electrons**.
- Q staying blue treats the carbon anode like the copper one, though nothing in Q replaces the copper(II) ions that leave the solution.
