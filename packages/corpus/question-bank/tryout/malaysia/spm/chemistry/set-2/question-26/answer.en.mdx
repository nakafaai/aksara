# A copper anode dissolves as fast as the cathode grows

With copper electrodes, the anode is not inert: **copper atoms at the anode give up electrons and enter the solution**, while copper(II) ions are discharged at the cathode.

- Anode: <InlineMath math="\text{Cu} \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^-" />, so the anode gets thinner.
- Cathode: <InlineMath math="\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}" />. <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of electrons deposit <InlineMath math="0.05" /> mol of Cu: <InlineMath math="0.05 \times 64 = 3.2\text{ g}" />.

Each copper(II) ion removed at the cathode is replaced by one from the anode, so the concentration, and the blue colour, stay the same.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="6.4\text{ g}" /> takes one copper atom per electron. The fading blue belongs to **carbon electrodes, where nothing replaces the copper(II) ions**.
- With carbon electrodes the cathode does gain <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ g}" /> and the colour fades, but here the copper anode refills the solution.
- The anode dissolves and loses mass. Only the cathode gains.
