# What the bubbles can and cannot tell you

Two things in the piece react with water, but they do it differently. A sodium atom still has its outer electron to hand to water, so **only the metal makes hydrogen**. In sodium oxide the sodium is already an ion, so the oxide just dissolves. Both give hydroxide ions, and both turn the indicator pink.

# Doubling the gas to find the metal

The hydrogen is <InlineMath math="360\div24\,000=0.0150\text{ mol}" />. **Two sodium atoms make one hydrogen molecule**, so the metal is <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.690\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="81.7\%" /> of the piece. Treating the gas amount as the sodium amount leaves you at <InlineMath math="40.8\%" />.

# Every sodium ends as hydroxide

The rest of the piece, <InlineMath math="0.155\text{ g}" />, is oxide, <InlineMath math="M_r=62" />, so <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" />. **Each formula unit brings two sodium ions into the solution**, so the oxide adds <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> of sodium hydroxide to the metal's <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" />. Without the oxide you would get <InlineMath math="0.120\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> and <InlineMath math="1.76\text{ g}" />. With one sodium per oxide you would get <InlineMath math="0.130\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> and <InlineMath math="1.90\text{ g}" />.

The salt mass is a quick check, because every sodium atom from the piece ends up in exactly one sodium chloride.
