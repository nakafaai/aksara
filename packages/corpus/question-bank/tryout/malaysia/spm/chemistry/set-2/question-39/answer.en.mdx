# Each calcium ion takes two soap anions

Calcium stearate is <InlineMath math="(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}" />, so **every calcium ion pulls two stearate ions out of the water**. Count the calcium ions, double them, and that is the soap lost before any lather forms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{Ca}^{2+})&=5.0\times0.0020=0.010\text{ mol}\\
\text{Ca}^{2+}+2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO}^-&\rightarrow(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}\\
m(\text{soap})&=2\times0.010\times306=6.12\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />

The foam test shows the other half of the answer. The detergent still foams in hard water, <InlineMath math="5.8\text{ cm}" /> against <InlineMath math="6.2\text{ cm}" />, because the calcium salt of its anion dissolves instead of settling out as scum.

So the water turns <InlineMath math="6.12\text{ g}" /> of soap into scum, and the detergent forms none.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="3.06\text{ g}" /> pairs one stearate ion with each calcium ion. The scum's formula needs two.
- The detergent keeps its foam in hard water, which it could not do if it lost the same mass as scum.
- <InlineMath math="6.06\text{ g}" /> is the mass of the scum, calcium stearate, not of the sodium stearate used up. **The question asks for the soap**.
