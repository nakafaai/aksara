# One bridge, two sulfur atoms, two linked units

Turn the sulfur into bridges first, then count the units the bridges touch. **Every bridge uses two sulfur atoms but links two units**, one on each chain, so the number of linked units equals the number of sulfur atoms.

So <InlineMath math="10\%" /> of the units carry a cross-link. The bridges hold the chains in place, so the stretched rubber pulls back to its shape and is harder and stronger than natural rubber.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="5\%" /> counts one unit per bridge and forgets that each bridge has two ends, one on each chain.
- <InlineMath math="20\%" /> treats every sulfur atom as a bridge with two ends. **A bridge needs a pair of sulfur atoms**, so there are only half as many.
- The cross-links make the rubber harder, not softer, and most of its double bonds are still there. The percentage is right, but the effect is reversed.
