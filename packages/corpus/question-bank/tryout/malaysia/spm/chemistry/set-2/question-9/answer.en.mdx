# Reading the trial titration

The trial uses <InlineMath math="0.0250\text{ mol}" /> of sodium hydroxide, and **each sulfuric acid neutralises two hydroxide ions**, so <InlineMath math="20.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of acid holds <InlineMath math="0.0125\text{ mol}" />. That makes the acid <InlineMath math="0.625\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Pairing the acid with the alkali one to one doubles it to <InlineMath math="1.25\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Mixing without indicator

Doubling both volumes keeps the trial's ratio, so <InlineMath math="50.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of alkali and <InlineMath math="40.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of acid neutralise each other exactly and **nothing is left over to spoil the crystals**. That gives <InlineMath math="0.0250\text{ mol}" /> of sodium sulfate, and the crystals include ten waters each, <InlineMath math="M_r=322" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
c(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4)&=\tfrac{\tfrac12\times0.0250}{0.0200}=0.625\text{ mol dm}^{-3}\\
n(\text{NaOH})&=0.0500=2\times n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4)=2\times0.0250\ \Rightarrow\ m_{\max}=0.0250\times322=8.05\text{ g},\quad \tfrac{6.44}{8.05}\times100=80.0\%
\end{aligned}" />

A yield cannot exceed <InlineMath math="100\%" />, so <InlineMath math="125\%" /> has divided the wrong way. Giving each sodium hydroxide its own sodium sulfate doubles the maximum to <InlineMath math="16.1\text{ g}" /> and halves the yield.
