# The steep jump marks the end point

At the end point, **the moles of hydroxide ions added equal the moles of hydrogen ions in the acid**. On a pH curve, that is the middle of the steep jump.

The jump happens at <InlineMath math="20.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of alkali. The equation is <InlineMath math="\text{HCl} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}" />, a <InlineMath math="1 : 1" /> ratio.

- Moles of HCl: <InlineMath math="0.100 \times \dfrac{25.0}{1000} = 0.00250\text{ mol}" />.
- Moles of NaOH at the end point: <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" />.
- Concentration of NaOH: <InlineMath math="\dfrac{0.00250}{20.0/1000} = 0.125\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.080\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> swaps the volumes: **it divides by the acid's volume and multiplies by the alkali's**.
- <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> treats the acid as if it gave two hydrogen ions per formula, like sulfuric acid.
- <InlineMath math="0.0625\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> halves the answer, as if two hydroxide ions were needed for each hydrogen ion.
