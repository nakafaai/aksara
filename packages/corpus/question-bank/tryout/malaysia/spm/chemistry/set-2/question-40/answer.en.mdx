# Yield and atom economy measure two different losses

The yield tells you how much of the possible ethanol the batch really made, so it decides how much glucose you need. The atom economy comes from the equation alone. **Even at a perfect yield, the carbon dioxide carries off part of every glucose molecule**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH})&=\frac{46\,000}{46}=1000\text{ mol}\ \Rightarrow\ n(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6)=500\text{ mol}\\
m(\text{glucose})&=\frac{500\times180}{0.80}=112\,500\text{ g}=112.5\text{ kg}\\
\text{atom economy}&=\frac{2(46)}{180}\times100\%=\frac{92}{180}\times100\%=51.1\%
\end{aligned}" />

So the batch uses <InlineMath math="112.5\text{ kg}" /> of glucose, and the atom economy is <InlineMath math="51.1\%" />, whatever the yield.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="90\text{ kg}" /> is the glucose for a full yield. At <InlineMath math="80\%" /> the factory needs more, <InlineMath math="90\div0.80" />.
- <InlineMath math="225\text{ kg}" /> uses one glucose per ethanol and misses the <InlineMath math="2" /> in front of <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}" />. One glucose gives two ethanol molecules.
- <InlineMath math="100\%" /> counts the carbon dioxide as a desired product. **Only the ethanol counts**, so almost half the mass is lost by design.
