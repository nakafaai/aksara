# One chlorine molecule oxidises two iron(II) ions, and the alkali then precipitates the iron as iron(III) hydroxide

The iron(II) ions are <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" />. **One chlorine molecule takes two electrons, so it oxidises two iron(II) ions**: <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine, <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" />. The pale green solution turns yellowish brown. **The iron is now iron(III)**, so the alkali precipitates <InlineMath math="\text{Fe(OH)}_3" />, <InlineMath math="M_r=107" />: <InlineMath math="0.00500\times107=0.535\text{ g}" /> of brown solid.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> takes one chlorine molecule per iron(II) ion. **Each chlorine molecule oxidises two**.
- <InlineMath math="0.450\text{ g}" /> precipitates iron(II) hydroxide, but the chlorine has already oxidised the iron to iron(III).
- <InlineMath math="1.07\text{ g}" /> doubles the iron, but each iron(II) ion becomes just one iron(III) ion.
