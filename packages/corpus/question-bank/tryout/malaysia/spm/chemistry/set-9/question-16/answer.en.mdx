# Subtract the acid left over from the acid added, then let every two acid molecules count one carbonate

The acid added is <InlineMath math="0.0500\times0.200=0.0100\text{ mol}" />, and the sodium hydroxide shows <InlineMath math="0.00300\text{ mol}" /> was left over. **The tablet used the difference**, <InlineMath math="0.00700\text{ mol}" />. <InlineMath math="\text{CaCO}_3+2\text{HCl}\rightarrow\text{CaCl}_2+\text{CO}_2+\text{H}_2\text{O}" /> takes two acid per carbonate, so the tablet held <InlineMath math="0.00350\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="0.350\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="70.0\%" /> of it. **Each carbonate gives one carbon dioxide**, <InlineMath math="0.00350\times24\,000=84.0\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="30.0\%" /> takes the acid left over as the acid that reacted. **The tablet used the difference, <InlineMath math="0.00700\text{ mol}" />**.
- <InlineMath math="60.0\%" /> lets each sodium hydroxide neutralise two hydrogen ions, but it neutralises one.
- <InlineMath math="168\text{ cm}^3" /> gives one carbon dioxide per acid molecule, but two acid molecules give one.
