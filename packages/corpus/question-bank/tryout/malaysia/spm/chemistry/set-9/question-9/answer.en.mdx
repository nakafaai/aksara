# Each halogen molecule takes two iodide ions, so the chlorine uses half the iodide and the bromine the other half

The iodide is <InlineMath math="0.0500\times0.200=0.0100\text{ mol}" /> and the chlorine <InlineMath math="60\div24\,000=0.00250\text{ mol}" />. **Each chlorine molecule takes two iodide ions**, <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" />, and frees <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" /> of iodine molecules, <InlineMath math="0.00250\times254=0.635\text{ g}" />. The iodide left is <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> in <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ dm}^3" />, <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Bromine is more reactive than iodine, so it oxidises this iodide too, **again two iodide ions per molecule**, <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" /> of bromine, <InlineMath math="0.00250\times160=0.400\text{ g}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.27\text{ g}" /> lets each chlorine take one iodide ion, so all the iodide reacts and none is left for the bromine. **Each chlorine takes two**.
- <InlineMath math="0.150\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> takes away only <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" /> of iodide, one per chlorine, which then also gives <InlineMath math="0.600\text{ g}" /> of bromine.
- <InlineMath math="0.318\text{ g}" /> weighs the iodine as atoms, <InlineMath math="A_r=127" />, but it forms as <InlineMath math="\text{I}_2" />, <InlineMath math="M_r=254" />.
