# One bromine molecule adds across each double bond, so the bromine counts the alkene, and burning turns every carbon atom into carbon dioxide

The bromine is <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" />. **An alkene has one double bond, and each takes one bromine molecule**, so the alkene is <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> and <InlineMath math="M_r=0.700\div0.0100=70" />. <InlineMath math="14n=70" /> gives <InlineMath math="n=5" />: <InlineMath math="\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}" />. Both bromine atoms join the molecule, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}\text{Br}_2" />, <InlineMath math="0.0100\times230=2.30\text{ g}" />. **Each molecule has five carbon atoms**, so burning gives <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide, <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ dm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}" /> is an alkane, which does not decolourise bromine. **The alkene is <InlineMath math="\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}" />**.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_{20}" /> lets two alkene molecules share one bromine molecule, but each double bond takes a whole one.
- <InlineMath math="0.240\text{ dm}^3" /> gives one carbon dioxide per alkene molecule, but each has five carbon atoms.
