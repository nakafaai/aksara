The carbon dioxide holds <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" /> of carbon, <InlineMath math="0.36\text{ g}" />, and the water <InlineMath math="0.0600\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen, <InlineMath math="0.06\text{ g}" />. **The rest of the <InlineMath math="0.900\text{ g}" /> is oxygen**, <InlineMath math="0.48\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" />, so the empirical formula is <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2\text{O}" />, mass <InlineMath math="30" />. The vapour is <InlineMath math="153\div30\,600=5.00\times10^{-3}\text{ mol}" />, so <InlineMath math="M_r=0.450\div0.00500=90" />, three empirical units: <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_3" />. <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_3+3\text{O}_2\rightarrow3\text{CO}_2+3\text{H}_2\text{O}" />, **so the compound brings half the oxygen itself**, and <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> uses <InlineMath math="0.0300\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen, <InlineMath math="720\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2\text{O}" /> is only the empirical formula. **The vapour gives <InlineMath math="M_r=90" />, three times its mass**.
- <InlineMath math="240\text{ cm}^3" /> takes one oxygen molecule per molecule of compound, but the balanced equation needs three.
- <InlineMath math="1080\text{ cm}^3" /> counts all the oxygen in the products as coming from the air, but the compound supplies some itself.
