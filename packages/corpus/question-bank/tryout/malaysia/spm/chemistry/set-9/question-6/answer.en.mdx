# The alkali separates the carbon dioxide from the spare oxygen, and that spare oxygen then decides how much hydrogen burns

**The alkali removes only carbon dioxide**, so <InlineMath math="110-50=60\text{ cm}^3" /> is carbon dioxide and the <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> left is unused oxygen. Equal volumes hold equal moles, so <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> of hydrocarbon gives <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide and <InlineMath math="x=3" />. The oxygen used is <InlineMath math="150-50=100\text{ cm}^3" />, five volumes per volume of hydrocarbon, and <InlineMath math="x+\tfrac{y}{4}=5" /> gives <InlineMath math="y=8" />, propane. **The spare oxygen now meets the hydrogen**, <InlineMath math="2\text{H}_2+\text{O}_2\rightarrow2\text{H}_2\text{O}" />, so <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen burns <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> of hydrogen, and <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> of hydrogen is left once the water condenses.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" /> burns hydrogen and oxygen one to one. **Each oxygen molecule takes two hydrogen molecules**, so <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen uses <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_4" /> finds the hydrogen from <InlineMath math="x+\tfrac{y}{2}=5" />, but four hydrogen atoms take one oxygen molecule, so the term is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{y}{4}" />.
- <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> counts the water from the spark as a gas, but at room conditions it is liquid.
