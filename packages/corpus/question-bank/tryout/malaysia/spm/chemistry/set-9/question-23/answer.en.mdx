# The charge fixes the copper deposited, but at the anode it is shared between copper and iron

The charge is <InlineMath math="2.00\times4825=9650\text{ C}" />, <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" /> of electrons, and **only copper takes them at the cathode**, <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="3.20\text{ g}" />. At the anode the same <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" /> of electrons comes from copper and iron together, both giving two each, so <InlineMath math="n_{\text{Cu}}+n_{\text{Fe}}=0.0500" />. The metal dissolved is <InlineMath math="3.26-0.100=3.16\text{ g}" />, so <InlineMath math="64n_{\text{Cu}}+56n_{\text{Fe}}=3.16" />, which gives <InlineMath math="n_{\text{Fe}}=0.00500" /> and <InlineMath math="n_{\text{Cu}}=0.0450" />, <InlineMath math="2.88\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="88.3\%" /> of the anode's loss. **The cathode takes <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol}" /> of copper ions out but the anode puts back only <InlineMath math="0.0450\text{ mol}" />**, so the solution loses <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" /> in <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ dm}^3" />, a fall of <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="98.2\%" /> treats all the copper on the cathode as coming from the anode, but part of it came from the solution.
- No change holds for a pure copper anode. **Here iron takes some of the electrons at the anode**, so less copper goes into solution than leaves it.
- <InlineMath math="2.88\text{ g}" /> puts only the anode's copper on the cathode, but the cathode takes copper ions for all <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" /> of electrons.
