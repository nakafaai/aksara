# The bromine water takes only the alkene, so the gas that passes through is the methane that then burns

**Bromine adds across the double bond of ethene but leaves methane alone**, so the <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> that comes out is methane and the ethene was <InlineMath math="100-60=40\text{ cm}^3" />. That is <InlineMath math="40\div24\,000=1.67\times10^{-3}\text{ mol}" />, which gives as much 1,2-dibromoethane, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{Br}_2" /> with <InlineMath math="M_r=188" />, <InlineMath math="1.67\times10^{-3}\times188=0.313\text{ g}" />. The methane burns by <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_4+2\text{O}_2\rightarrow\text{CO}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}" />, so <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> uses <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen and makes <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide. **The water condenses**, so the gas left is <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide and <InlineMath math="80\text{ cm}^3" /> of spare oxygen, <InlineMath math="140\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> takes the gas that comes out as ethene, but bromine water keeps the alkene and lets methane through.
- <InlineMath math="260\text{ cm}^3" /> counts the <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> of steam, but **at room conditions the water is liquid**.
- <InlineMath math="0.267\text{ g}" /> uses the bromine taken up, <InlineMath math="M_r=160" />, not the product with <InlineMath math="M_r=188" />.
