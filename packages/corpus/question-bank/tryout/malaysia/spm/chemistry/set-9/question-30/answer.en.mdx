# The carbon dioxide counts carbon atoms, which splits the mixture, each gas takes its own oxygen, and both carbon dioxide and spare oxygen remain

Methane gives one carbon dioxide per molecule and ethane two. **If the mixture holds <InlineMath math="x" /> of ethane, the carbon dioxide is <InlineMath math="100+x=160" />**, so <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> of ethane and <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of methane. Methane takes two oxygen per molecule and ethane three and a half, <InlineMath math="2(40)+3.5(60)=290\text{ cm}^3" />. **What is left is the unused oxygen plus all the carbon dioxide**, <InlineMath math="110+160=270\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of ethane swaps the two gases. **The extra <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide comes from ethane's second carbon atom**.
- <InlineMath math="110\text{ cm}^3" /> counts only the unused oxygen, but the carbon dioxide is also still a gas.
- <InlineMath math="160\text{ cm}^3" /> of oxygen takes one oxygen per carbon dioxide, but the hydrogen also burns, to water.
