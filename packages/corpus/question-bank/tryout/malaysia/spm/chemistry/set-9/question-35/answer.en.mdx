# Break one <InlineMath math="\text{H–H}" /> and one <InlineMath math="\text{Cl–Cl}" />, form two <InlineMath math="\text{H–Cl}" />, halve the result for one mole of product, then work back to the hydrogen

Breaking one <InlineMath math="\text{H–H}" /> and one <InlineMath math="\text{Cl–Cl}" /> takes <InlineMath math="436+242=678\text{ kJ}" />, and forming two <InlineMath math="\text{H–Cl}" /> releases <InlineMath math="2\times431=862\text{ kJ}" />, so the equation as written releases <InlineMath math="184\text{ kJ}" />. **That is for two moles of hydrogen chloride**, so each mole gives <InlineMath math="-92\text{ kJ}" />. Releasing <InlineMath math="46.0\text{ kJ}" /> needs <InlineMath math="0.500\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen chloride, **which comes from half as much hydrogen**, <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="6.00\text{ dm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="-184\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" /> is for the equation as written, which forms two moles. **Each mole of hydrogen chloride gives half**.
- <InlineMath math="+92\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" /> reverses the sign: forming the bonds releases more than breaking them takes.
- <InlineMath math="12.0\text{ dm}^3" /> takes the hydrogen chloride's moles as hydrogen, but one hydrogen molecule gives two.
