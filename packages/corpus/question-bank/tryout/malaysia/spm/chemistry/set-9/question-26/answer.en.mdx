# Turn the charge into electrons, discharge two per gas molecule, and let the hydrogen leave hydroxide ions behind

The charge is <InlineMath math="0.500\times1930=965\text{ C}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of electrons. **The solution is concentrated, so chloride ions, not hydroxide ions, are discharged at the anode**: <InlineMath math="2\text{Cl}^-\rightarrow\text{Cl}_2+2e^-" />. At the cathode water is reduced, <InlineMath math="2\text{H}_2\text{O}+2e^-\rightarrow\text{H}_2+2\text{OH}^-" />. Each gas takes two electrons, <InlineMath math="0.00500\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" /> each, **and every electron leaves one hydroxide ion**, <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> in <InlineMath math="200\text{ cm}^3" />, <InlineMath math="0.0500\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="240\text{ cm}^3" /> of each gas gives one molecule per electron. **Each gas molecule takes two electrons**.
- Oxygen at the anode would come from dilute brine. **This brine is concentrated, so chlorine forms**, and the cathode still leaves hydroxide ions.
- <InlineMath math="0.0250\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> gives one hydroxide ion per two electrons, but the cathode equation gives one per electron.
