# The titration counts the carbonate in the whole flask, which fixes the water in the crystals and so the mass after they lose water

The acid is <InlineMath math="0.0250\times0.200=0.00500\text{ mol}" />, and **each carbonate takes two acid**, so the portion holds <InlineMath math="0.00250\text{ mol}" />. The flask holds ten portions, <InlineMath math="0.0250\text{ mol}" />, so the crystals have <InlineMath math="M_r=7.15\div0.0250=286" />. Taking away <InlineMath math="106" /> for the carbonate leaves <InlineMath math="180" />, ten waters, so <InlineMath math="x=10" />. **In dry air each formula unit keeps one water**, so the solid left is <InlineMath math="0.0250\times124=3.10\text{ g}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="x=2" /> reads the titration as one acid per carbonate, which doubles the carbonate and halves its <InlineMath math="M_r" />.
- <InlineMath math="2.65\text{ g}" /> is the anhydrous carbonate. **In dry air the crystals keep one water each**, so the solid is the monohydrate.
- <InlineMath math="0.310\text{ g}" /> uses the carbonate in one portion, not in the whole sample of <InlineMath math="7.15\text{ g}" />.
