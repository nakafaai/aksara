# The oxygen runs out, so some sodium stays as metal, yet every sodium atom still ends up as hydroxide

X is sodium, <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" />, and Y is oxygen, <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" />. In <InlineMath math="4\text{Na}+\text{O}_2\rightarrow2\text{Na}_2\text{O}" /> the sodium would need <InlineMath math="0.025\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen, **so the oxygen runs out**, giving <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mol}" /> of <InlineMath math="\text{Na}_2\text{O}" /> and leaving <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of sodium. In water both give sodium hydroxide, so all <InlineMath math="0.100\text{ mol}" /> of sodium ends as hydroxide ions. Only the leftover metal releases hydrogen, <InlineMath math="2\text{Na}+2\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow2\text{NaOH}+\text{H}_2" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.16\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> counts only the oxide's hydroxide and assumes no metal is left. **The oxygen runs out, and the leftover sodium also makes hydroxide**.
- The hydroxide is right, but the <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of leftover sodium also releases hydrogen in the water.
- <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> gives one hydroxide ion per formula unit of oxide, but each gives two.
