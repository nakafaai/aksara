# Split the mixture with the mass and the gas, then count each salt's own electron transfer

Each salt gives one molecule of gas, so the <InlineMath math="0.150\text{ mol}" /> of gas means <InlineMath math="x+y=0.150" />, and the mass gives <InlineMath math="80x+64y=11.2" />: <InlineMath math="x=0.100" /> of the nitrate and <InlineMath math="y=0.050" /> of the nitrite. **The two salts transfer different numbers of electrons**: in the nitrate nitrogen goes from <InlineMath math="-3" /> to <InlineMath math="+1" />, four electrons, and in the nitrite from <InlineMath math="-3" /> to <InlineMath math="0" />, three electrons.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.60\text{ mol}" /> gives the nitrite four electrons per unit too. **Its nitrogen changes by only three**.
- <InlineMath math="0.45\text{ mol}" /> gives the nitrate only three electrons per unit, but its nitrogen changes by four.
- <InlineMath math="1.10\text{ mol}" /> counts every electron twice, once as lost and once as gained.
