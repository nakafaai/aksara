# Weigh the litre to find the salt, then let each chloride ion take one silver ion

**One cubic decimetre of seawater weighs <InlineMath math="1030\text{ g}" />, not <InlineMath math="1000\text{ g}" />**, so it holds <InlineMath math="0.035\times1030=36.1\text{ g}" /> of salt, <InlineMath math="0.616\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. The <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> portion holds <InlineMath math="0.0154\text{ mol}" /> of chloride ions. <InlineMath math="\text{Ag}^++\text{Cl}^-\rightarrow\text{AgCl}" /> is one to one, so it needs <InlineMath math="0.0154\text{ mol}" /> of silver ions, <InlineMath math="30.8\text{ cm}^3" /> of the solution, and forms <InlineMath math="0.0154\times143.5=2.21\text{ g}" /> of silver chloride. The sodium ions stay in solution.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="29.9\text{ cm}^3" /> takes a litre of seawater as <InlineMath math="1000\text{ g}" />. **Its density is <InlineMath math="1.03\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />**.
- <InlineMath math="61.6\text{ cm}^3" /> counts the sodium ions too, but only the chloride ions react with silver ions.
- <InlineMath math="0.901\text{ g}" /> is the mass of sodium chloride in the portion, not of the silver chloride that precipitates.
