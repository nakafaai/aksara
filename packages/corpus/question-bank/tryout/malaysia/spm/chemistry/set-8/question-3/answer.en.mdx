# Find which gases are present at each temperature, then use the molar volume for that temperature

Propane is <InlineMath math="2.20\div44=0.0500\text{ mol}" /> and oxygen <InlineMath math="7.20\div24=0.300\text{ mol}" />. <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_8+5\text{O}_2\rightarrow3\text{CO}_2+4\text{H}_2\text{O}" /> uses <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol}" /> of oxygen, so <InlineMath math="0.050\text{ mol}" /> is left, with <InlineMath math="0.150\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide and <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol}" /> of steam. **At <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> all <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> are gas, and one mole fills <InlineMath math="30.6\text{ dm}^3" />**, so the gas is <InlineMath math="12.2\text{ dm}^3" />. **At room conditions the steam condenses**, leaving <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide and oxygen, <InlineMath math="4.80\text{ dm}^3" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="9.60\text{ dm}^3" /> uses <InlineMath math="24\text{ dm}^3" />, the molar volume at room conditions, for the hot gases. **At <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> one mole fills <InlineMath math="30.6\text{ dm}^3" />**.
- <InlineMath math="9.60\text{ dm}^3" /> after cooling still counts the water as a gas, but it condenses to a liquid at room conditions.
- <InlineMath math="10.7\text{ dm}^3" /> and <InlineMath math="3.60\text{ dm}^3" /> forget the leftover oxygen. <InlineMath math="0.300\text{ mol}" /> is supplied but only <InlineMath math="0.250\text{ mol}" /> reacts.
