# Find how much pure acid there is, let it use up part of the hydrogen carbonate, then decompose the rest two at a time

The sodium hydrogen carbonate is <InlineMath math="8.40\div84=0.100\text{ mol}" />. **The cream of tartar is only <InlineMath math="94\%" /> pure**, <InlineMath math="0.94\times12.0=11.28\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="11.28\div188=0.0600\text{ mol}" />, so the acid runs out after giving <InlineMath math="0.0600\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide and leaves <InlineMath math="0.0400\text{ mol}" /> of hydrogen carbonate. **That decomposes two at a time**, giving <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide and <InlineMath math="0.0200\text{ mol}" /> of sodium carbonate, <InlineMath math="2.12\text{ g}" />. The carbon dioxide totals <InlineMath math="0.0800\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0800\times37.2=2.98\text{ dm}^3" /> in the oven.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="2.23\text{ dm}^3" /> counts only the gas from the acid. **The leftover hydrogen carbonate decomposes in the oven and adds more**.
- <InlineMath math="3.72\text{ dm}^3" /> and <InlineMath math="4.24\text{ g}" /> let each leftover hydrogen carbonate give its own carbon dioxide, but two give one.
- <InlineMath math="1.92\text{ dm}^3" /> uses <InlineMath math="24\text{ dm}^3" />, the molar volume at room conditions, for gas in a hot oven.
