# Each sucrose gives two glucose, each glucose two ethanol and two carbon dioxide, and each carbon dioxide one carbonate

The sucrose is <InlineMath math="34.2\div342=0.100\text{ mol}" />. **The split doubles it to <InlineMath math="0.200\text{ mol}" /> of glucose**, and fermentation doubles it again: <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> of ethanol, <InlineMath math="18.4\text{ g}" />, and <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> of carbon dioxide, <InlineMath math="9.60\text{ dm}^3" />. In excess limewater, <InlineMath math="\text{Ca(OH)}_2+\text{CO}_2\rightarrow\text{CaCO}_3+\text{H}_2\text{O}" /> gives <InlineMath math="0.400\text{ mol}" /> of calcium carbonate, <InlineMath math="40.0\text{ g}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="9.2\text{ g}" /> ferments each sucrose as if it were one glucose. **The enzyme first splits it into two**.
- <InlineMath math="4.80\text{ dm}^3" /> gives one carbon dioxide per glucose, but the equation gives two.
- <InlineMath math="36.8\text{ g}" /> doubles one time too many, as if each glucose gave four ethanol.
