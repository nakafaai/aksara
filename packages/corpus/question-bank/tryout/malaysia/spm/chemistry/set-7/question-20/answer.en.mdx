# Weigh a hundred cubic centimetres of composite with its air, then scale up to the hull

Take <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> of composite: <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}^3" /> of glass is <InlineMath math="102\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="55\text{ cm}^3" /> of polyester is <InlineMath math="66\text{ g}" />, and the <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}^3" /> of air adds nothing. **The air still takes up volume**, so the density is <InlineMath math="168\div100=1.68\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, and glass is <InlineMath math="102\div168=60.7\%" /> of the mass. The hull fills <InlineMath math="84\,000\div1.68=50\,000\text{ cm}^3" />, and <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of that, <InlineMath math="2.50\text{ dm}^3" />, is air.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.77\text{ g cm}^{-3}" /> spreads the mass over only the solid <InlineMath math="95\text{ cm}^3" />. **The air bubbles take up <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of the volume**.
- <InlineMath math="40.0\%" /> is the share of the volume, but glass is denser than polyester, so its share of the mass is larger.
- <InlineMath math="4.20\text{ dm}^3" /> takes <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of the hull's mass, but the air is <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of its volume and has no mass.
