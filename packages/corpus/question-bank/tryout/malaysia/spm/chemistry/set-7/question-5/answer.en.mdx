# Weigh the crystals with their water, let the acid count the hydroxide two at a time, then scale the portion up to the flask

The acid is <InlineMath math="0.250\times0.0500=0.0125\text{ mol}" />. **The crystals carry two water molecules**, <InlineMath math="M_r=126" />, so <InlineMath math="0.0125\times126=1.58\text{ g}" /> is weighed. The <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> portion holds <InlineMath math="1.25\times10^{-3}\text{ mol}" /> of acid. **Each acid molecule neutralises two hydroxide ions**, so the <InlineMath math="26.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of alkali held <InlineMath math="2.50\times10^{-3}\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0962\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. The <InlineMath math="250\text{ cm}^3" /> flask then held <InlineMath math="0.0240\text{ mol}" />, <InlineMath math="0.962\text{ g}" /> of sodium hydroxide, <InlineMath math="96.2\%" /> of the <InlineMath math="1.00\text{ g}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.13\text{ g}" /> weighs the anhydrous acid, <InlineMath math="M_r=90" />. **The crystals are the dihydrate**, <InlineMath math="M_r=126" />.
- <InlineMath math="48.1\%" /> lets each acid molecule neutralise one hydroxide ion, but both of its hydrogen atoms react.
- <InlineMath math="1.13\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="48.1\%" /> make both mistakes: the anhydrous formula and one hydroxide per acid.
