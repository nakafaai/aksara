# Let the zero error cancel in the differences, count the carbon dioxide lost, then carry the copper into the sulfate

**The zero error adds <InlineMath math="0.04\text{ g}" /> to every reading, so it cancels in each difference**: the sample is <InlineMath math="22.72-20.24=2.48\text{ g}" /> and the carbon dioxide lost is <InlineMath math="22.72-22.06=0.66\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="0.0150\text{ mol}" />. <InlineMath math="\text{CuCO}_3\rightarrow\text{CuO}+\text{CO}_2" />, so the sample held <InlineMath math="0.0150\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) carbonate, <InlineMath math="1.86\text{ g}" />, <InlineMath math="75.0\%" /> of it. That leaves <InlineMath math="0.0150\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) oxide, which the acid turns into <InlineMath math="0.0150\text{ mol}" /> of copper(II) sulfate, <InlineMath math="0.0150\times160=2.40\text{ g}" />. The impurity does not react.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="76.2\%" /> subtracts the zero error from the sample's mass only. **Both readings carry it, so it cancels**.
- <InlineMath math="26.6\%" /> is the share of the mass lost as carbon dioxide, not the share of copper(II) carbonate.
- <InlineMath math="3.64\text{ g}" /> treats the whole residue as copper(II) oxide, but part of it is the impurity.
