# Atoms from the combustion products

All the carbon ends up in <InlineMath math="\text{CO}_2" /> and all the hydrogen ends up in <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{O}" />. Remember that **one water molecule carries two hydrogen atoms**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{C})&=n(\text{CO}_2)=\frac{2.20}{44}=0.050\text{ mol}\\
n(\text{H})&=2\,n(\text{H}_2\text{O})=2\times\frac{0.90}{18}=0.10\text{ mol}
\end{aligned}" />

The chart shows the atoms each product carries. The hydrogen bar is twice the carbon bar, so the ratio is <InlineMath math="\text{C}:\text{H}=1:2" />.

Check the mass: <InlineMath math="0.050\times12+0.10\times1=0.70\text{ g}" />, exactly the mass of the hydrocarbon, so no other element is present. The ratio <InlineMath math="\text{C}:\text{H}=1:2" /> gives the empirical formula <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2" />, of mass <InlineMath math="14" />.

<BlockMath math="n=\frac{56}{14}=4\ \Rightarrow\ (\text{CH}_2)_4=\text{C}_4\text{H}_8" />

So the molecular formula is <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_8" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_2" /> is the empirical formula, but the question asks for **the molecular formula** with molar mass <InlineMath math="56" />.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_4" /> forgets that each water molecule carries two hydrogen atoms.
- <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_4" /> comes from <InlineMath math="56/28" />, dividing the molar mass by the mass of <InlineMath math="\text{C}_2\text{H}_4" /> instead of the empirical formula mass.
