# The same electrons pass through both electrodes

At the cathode <InlineMath math="\text{Cu}^{2+}" /> ions are reduced, and at the inert carbon anode <InlineMath math="\text{OH}^-" /> ions are discharged as oxygen. **The same number of electrons passes at both electrodes**, so link the copper and the oxygen through moles of electrons.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{Cu})&=\frac{0.64}{64}=0.010\text{ mol}\ \Rightarrow\ n(e^-)=0.020\text{ mol}\\
4\text{OH}^-&\rightarrow\text{O}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}+4e^-\ \Rightarrow\ n(\text{O}_2)=\frac{0.020}{4}=0.0050\text{ mol}\\
V&=0.0050\times24=0.12\text{ dm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

<InlineMath math="\text{OH}^-" /> ions from water keep being used up, so <InlineMath math="\text{H}^+" /> ions build up and the solution becomes more acidic. So <InlineMath math="0.12\text{ dm}^3" /> of oxygen is released at the anode, and the pH decreases.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.24\text{ dm}^3" /> sets the moles of oxygen equal to the moles of copper, but **one mole of oxygen needs four moles of electrons**, while one mole of copper needs only two.
- A rising pH reverses the effect, because discharging <InlineMath math="\text{OH}^-" /> ions makes the solution more acidic.
- No hydrogen forms at the anode, because oxidation happens there, not the reduction of hydrogen ions.
