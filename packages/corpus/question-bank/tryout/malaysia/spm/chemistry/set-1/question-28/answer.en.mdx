# Oxygen per mole of each hydrocarbon

First balance both combustion equations to find **the oxygen needed per mole** of each gas. Propane gives <InlineMath math="3\text{CO}_2+4\text{H}_2\text{O}" />, while propene gives <InlineMath math="3\text{CO}_2+3\text{H}_2\text{O}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{C}_3\text{H}_8+5\text{O}_2&\rightarrow3\text{CO}_2+4\text{H}_2\text{O}\\
\text{C}_3\text{H}_6+\tfrac92\text{O}_2&\rightarrow3\text{CO}_2+3\text{H}_2\text{O}\\
5(0.10-x)+4.5x&=0.48\ \Rightarrow\ 0.50-0.5x=0.48\ \Rightarrow\ x=0.040
\end{aligned}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="0.060" /> mol of propane uses <InlineMath math="0.30" /> mol of oxygen and <InlineMath math="0.040" /> mol of propene uses <InlineMath math="0.18" /> mol, a total of <InlineMath math="0.48" /> mol. So the mixture contains <InlineMath math="0.040" /> mol of propene.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.060\text{ mol}" /> is the amount of propane, because **the two substances were swapped** when setting up the equation.
- <InlineMath math="0.020\text{ mol}" /> is the oxygen shortfall against pure propane, <InlineMath math="0.50-0.48" />, without dividing by the difference of <InlineMath math="0.5" /> per mole.
- <InlineMath math="0.080\text{ mol}" /> uses <InlineMath math="4\text{O}_2" /> for propene, making the difference per mole <InlineMath math="1" />.
