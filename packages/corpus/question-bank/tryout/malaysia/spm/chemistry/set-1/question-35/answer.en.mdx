# Moles of precipitate, then heat, then temperature

This time the calculation runs backwards: from <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> to the temperature rise. First find the moles of precipitate formed. Both ions are present in equal amounts, so **the moles of precipitate equal the moles of either ion**, not their sum.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{AgCl})&=0.50\times\frac{50}{1000}=0.025\text{ mol}\\
Q&=0.025\times65{,}500=1637.5\text{ J}\\
\theta&=\frac{Q}{mc}=\frac{1637.5}{(100)(4.2)}\approx3.9^\circ\text{C}
\end{aligned}" />

The energy level diagram shows the heat for each mole of silver chloride. Only <InlineMath math="0.025\text{ mol}" /> forms here, so only that share of <InlineMath math="65.5\text{ kJ}" /> warms the mixture.

The mass heated is the whole mixture, <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" />. So the temperature of the mixture rises by about <InlineMath math="3.9^\circ\text{C}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="7.8^\circ\text{C}" /> uses a mass of <InlineMath math="50\text{ g}" />, but **the heat warms all <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" /> of mixture**.
- <InlineMath math="1.9^\circ\text{C}" /> uses <InlineMath math="0.0125\text{ mol}" />, as if only half the ions precipitated.
- <InlineMath math="15.6^\circ\text{C}" /> combines two errors: a mass of <InlineMath math="50\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.050" /> mol, the sum of both ions.
