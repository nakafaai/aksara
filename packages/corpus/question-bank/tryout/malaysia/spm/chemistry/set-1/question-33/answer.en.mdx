# Heat and mass change together

The temperature rise is <InlineMath math="\theta=\dfrac{Q}{mc}" />. The heat <InlineMath math="Q" /> is proportional to the moles of water formed, while <InlineMath math="m" /> is the mass of the mixture. So **what decides the temperature rise is moles per unit mass**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{II: }&n\times2,\ m\times2\ \Rightarrow\ \theta=6.5^\circ\text{C}\\
\text{III: }&n\times2,\ m\ \text{unchanged}\ \Rightarrow\ \theta=2\times6.5=13.0^\circ\text{C}
\end{aligned}" />

The chart compares each experiment with experiment I. The temperature rise follows the heat divided by the mass it has to warm.

Experiment III: heat <InlineMath math="\times2" />, mass <InlineMath math="\times1" />, so the temperature rise is <InlineMath math="\times2" />, that is <InlineMath math="13.0^\circ\text{C}" />.</>}

In experiment II the heat doubles, but it has to warm twice as much solution. In experiment III the heat doubles with the same mass. So the temperature rise is <InlineMath math="6.5^\circ\text{C}" /> in experiment II and <InlineMath math="13.0^\circ\text{C}" /> in experiment III.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="13.0^\circ\text{C}" /> for experiment II looks only at the doubled heat, but **the mass being heated also doubles**.
- The option that swaps the two values mixes up the effect of volume with the effect of concentration.
- <InlineMath math="6.5^\circ\text{C}" /> for experiment III forgets that double the concentration makes twice the water in the same mass.
