# Judging each statement

Statement I is correct. In a pure metal the atoms sit in neat layers that slide easily, and carbon atoms of a different size **disrupt that arrangement**. Statement II is correct, because borosilicate glass hardly expands, so it does not crack when the temperature changes suddenly. Statement III is wrong, because duralumin is used in aircraft for being strong yet still light.

Statement IV needs a calculation, because the percentages are by mass, not by number of atoms. Take <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" /> of bronze.

<BlockMath math="\frac{n(\text{Cu})}{n(\text{Sn})}=\frac{90/64}{10/119}=\frac{1.406}{0.0840}\approx16.7\approx17" />

The chart shows why mass percentages mislead here. Tin is <InlineMath math="10\%" /> of the mass, but each tin atom is heavy, so its bar of moles is tiny.

So statement IV is correct, and I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects statement IV, usually by reading the <InlineMath math="9:1" /> mass ratio as an atom ratio, but **a tin atom is much heavier** than a copper atom.
- I, II and III accepts a reason for duralumin that is the wrong way round, since aircraft need light materials.
- II, III and IV rejects the correct statement I and accepts the wrong statement III.
