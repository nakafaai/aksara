# One hydrogen molecule for each double bond

In hydrogenation, **one hydrogen molecule adds across one carbon-carbon double bond**. So the number of double bonds per oil molecule equals the ratio of moles of hydrogen to moles of oil. The oil gains the mass of the hydrogen that joins its molecules.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{H}_2)&=\frac{1.44}{24}=0.060\text{ mol}\\
\text{double bonds per molecule}&=\frac{0.060}{0.010}=6\\
\Delta m&=0.060\times2=0.12\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="0.06\text{ g}" /> uses a molar mass of <InlineMath math="1" /> for hydrogen, but **hydrogen exists as <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2" /> molecules** with a molar mass of <InlineMath math="2" />.
- <InlineMath math="12" /> counts hydrogen atoms, but each double bond takes up one <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2" /> molecule.
- <InlineMath math="3" /> assumes each fatty acid chain has only one double bond, but the calculation gives six per molecule.
