# Carbon fixes n, hydrogen fixes the water

All the carbon in the alcohol ends up as carbon dioxide, so <InlineMath math="n" /> is the ratio of moles of carbon dioxide to moles of alcohol. Once <InlineMath math="n" /> is known, count the hydrogen: the formula <InlineMath math="\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{OH}" /> has **<InlineMath math="2n+2" /> hydrogen atoms**, including the one in the <InlineMath math="-\text{OH}" /> group.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n(\text{CO}_2)&=\frac{13.2}{44}=0.30\text{ mol}\ \Rightarrow\ n=\frac{0.30}{0.10}=3\ \Rightarrow\ \text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{OH}\\
\text{H per molecule}&=2(3)+2=8\ \Rightarrow\ 4\ \text{H}_2\text{O per alcohol molecule}\\
m(\text{H}_2\text{O})&=0.10\times4\times18=7.2\text{ g}
\end{aligned}" />

The chart shows the products from <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of alcohol. The carbon dioxide bar is three times the alcohol bar and the water bar is four times, which fits <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{OH}" />.

So the alcohol is <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{OH}" /> and <InlineMath math="7.2\text{ g}" /> of water forms.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="5.4\text{ g}" /> with propanol assumes the moles of water equal the moles of carbon dioxide, but **propanol has eight hydrogen atoms**.
- Ethanol has only two carbon atoms, so <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of it gives <InlineMath math="8.8\text{ g}" /> of carbon dioxide, not <InlineMath math="13.2\text{ g}" />.
- Butanol has four carbon atoms, so it gives <InlineMath math="17.6\text{ g}" /> of carbon dioxide.
