# Split each precipitate into the part that dissolves and the part that stays

A precipitate that only **partly** dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide holds two hydroxides: one amphoteric (aluminium, zinc or lead(II)) that dissolves, and one that does not. Ammonia then sorts the amphoteric one, because only zinc hydroxide dissolves in excess ammonia, as zinc ions form a complex with it. The part left in both alkalis is a white hydroxide that dissolves in neither, such as magnesium hydroxide.

The anions come from the acidified tests. Silver nitrate after nitric acid gives white silver chloride, so chloride is present. Barium chloride after hydrochloric acid gives white barium sulfate, so sulfate is present too. Zinc sulfate with magnesium chloride supplies <InlineMath math="\text{Zn}^{2+}" />, <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" />, <InlineMath math="\text{Cl}^-" /> and <InlineMath math="\text{SO}_4^{2-}" />, matching all four results.

# Why the other options are wrong

- With aluminium chloride and magnesium sulfate, ammonia would dissolve **none** of the precipitate, because aluminium hydroxide stays solid in excess ammonia.
- Zinc chloride and calcium nitrate give no sulfate for barium chloride, and calcium ions give no precipitate with ammonia, so all of that precipitate would dissolve.
- Lead(II) nitrate and zinc sulfate cannot share one clear solution, since lead(II) sulfate is insoluble, and they hold no chloride for silver nitrate.
