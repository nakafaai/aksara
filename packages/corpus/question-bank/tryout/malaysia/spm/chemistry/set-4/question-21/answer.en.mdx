# Electrons gained per manganese give the drop in oxidation number

Each iron(II) ion gives up **one electron**: <InlineMath math="\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + e^-" />. So the manganate(VII) ions gain as many electrons as there are iron(II) ions.

- In acid: <InlineMath math="0.010 \div 0.0020 = 5" /> electrons per manganese, so <InlineMath math="+7 - 5 = +2" />: <InlineMath math="\text{Mn}^{2+}" />.
- In neutral solution: <InlineMath math="0.0060 \div 0.0020 = 3" /> electrons per manganese, so <InlineMath math="+7 - 3 = +4" />: <InlineMath math="\text{MnO}_2" />, a brown solid named manganese(IV) oxide.

The same oxidising agent does different amounts of work depending on its surroundings: in acid, hydrogen ions take up the oxygen as water and let manganese fall further.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Swapping the products would need three electrons in acid and five in neutral solution, the opposite of **the measured ratios**.
- Manganate(VI), <InlineMath math="+6" />, gains only one electron per manganese, which would oxidise <InlineMath math="0.0020" /> mol of iron(II), not <InlineMath math="0.0060" /> mol.
- <InlineMath math="\text{Mn}^{3+}" /> would need four electrons per manganese, <InlineMath math="0.0080" /> mol of iron(II), not <InlineMath math="0.010" /> mol.
