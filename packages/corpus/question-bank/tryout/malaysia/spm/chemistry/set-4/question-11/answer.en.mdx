# Each bond is a pair, so count electrons, not lines

Draw each molecule so every atom reaches a stable arrangement, then count **two electrons for every shared pair**:

- <InlineMath math="\text{N}_2" />: one triple bond, <InlineMath math="6" /> shared electrons, one lone pair on each N.
- <InlineMath math="\text{NH}_3" />: three single bonds, <InlineMath math="6" /> shared electrons, one lone pair on N.
- <InlineMath math="\text{CO}_2" />: two double bonds, <InlineMath math="8" /> shared electrons, two lone pairs on each O.
- <InlineMath math="\text{H}_2\text{O}" />: two single bonds, <InlineMath math="4" /> shared electrons, two lone pairs on O.

Carbon dioxide shares the most: <InlineMath math="8" /> electrons, with <InlineMath math="4" /> lone pairs on its two oxygen atoms.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\text{N}_2" /> shares <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons in its triple bond, fewer than the <InlineMath math="8" /> in carbon dioxide.
- <InlineMath math="\text{NH}_3" /> also shares only <InlineMath math="6" />.
- <InlineMath math="4" /> counts the **bonds** in carbon dioxide, two double bonds as four lines, instead of the <InlineMath math="8" /> electrons they hold.
