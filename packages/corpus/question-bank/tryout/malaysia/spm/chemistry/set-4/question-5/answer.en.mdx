# One amount, two equations

<InlineMath math="\text{CaCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2" />: <InlineMath math="10.0 \div 100 = 0.10" /> mol of calcium carbonate gives <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of carbon dioxide, which is <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 24 = 2.4\text{ dm}^3" />.

  `}
/>

<InlineMath math="2\text{NaOH} + \text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}" /> needs **two hydroxide ions for each carbon dioxide molecule**: <InlineMath math="0.20" /> mol of sodium hydroxide, or <InlineMath math="0.20 \times 40 = 8.0" /> g. It gives <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of sodium carbonate: <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 106 = 10.6" /> g.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ g}" /> treats the absorption as one to one and misses the second hydroxide ion.
- <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ dm}^3" /> halves the gas, though each calcium carbonate unit gives **one** carbon dioxide molecule.
- <InlineMath math="16.0\text{ g}" /> doubles the sodium hydroxide twice over.
