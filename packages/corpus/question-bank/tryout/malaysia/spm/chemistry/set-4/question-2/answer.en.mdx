# Rebuild the atom from its ion

An ion of charge <InlineMath math="3+" /> has **lost three electrons**, so the atom had <InlineMath math="10 + 3 = 13" /> electrons and therefore <InlineMath math="13" /> protons: aluminium. Its nucleon number is <InlineMath math="13 + 14 = 27" />.

Aluminium forms <InlineMath math="\text{Al}^{3+}" /> and oxygen forms <InlineMath math="\text{O}^{2-}" />, so the oxide is <InlineMath math="\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3" />, with <InlineMath math="M_r = 2(27) + 3(16) = 102" />. In <InlineMath math="10.2" /> g there are <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of oxide, holding <InlineMath math="0.20" /> mol of aluminium: <InlineMath math="0.20 \times 27 = 5.4" /> g.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Sodium has <InlineMath math="11" /> protons, and it forms ions of charge <InlineMath math="1+" />, not <InlineMath math="3+" />.
- <InlineMath math="2.7\text{ g}" /> counts **one aluminium atom** per formula unit, but <InlineMath math="\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3" /> holds two.
- Magnesium has <InlineMath math="12" /> protons and forms <InlineMath math="\text{Mg}^{2+}" />.
