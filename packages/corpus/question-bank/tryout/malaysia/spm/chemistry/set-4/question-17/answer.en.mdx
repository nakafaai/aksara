# The titration counts all the acid, the pH counts only the ions

Each flask holds <InlineMath math="0.010 \times 0.0250 = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}" /> mol of a monoprotic acid. As the alkali removes hydrogen ions, ethanoic acid **keeps ionising until every molecule has reacted**, so both need <InlineMath math="2.5 \times 10^{-4} \div 0.010 = 0.0250\text{ dm}^3" />, which is <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" />.

The pH tells how much is ionised before any alkali is added. At <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, ethanoic acid has pH <InlineMath math="3.4" />: <InlineMath math="[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-3.4} = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}" />, so <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{0.010} \times 100\% = 4.0\%" />. At <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, pH <InlineMath math="2.9" /> gives <InlineMath math="1.3 \times 10^{-3}" />, only <InlineMath math="1.3\%" />.

Diluting a weak acid raises the share of its molecules that ionise, even though the hydrogen ion concentration falls.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ cm}^3" /> neutralises only the ions present at the start, but more molecules ionise as soon as **those are removed**.
- <InlineMath math="0.40\%" /> divides by <InlineMath math="0.10" /> instead of <InlineMath math="0.010" />.
- Each molecule gives one hydrogen ion however it ionises, so the alkali needed matches the acid's moles, not more.
