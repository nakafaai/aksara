# Equal gas volumes hold equal numbers of molecules

At the same temperature and pressure, gas volumes are in the same ratio as moles. So <InlineMath math="1" /> volume of hydrocarbon needs **<InlineMath math="5" /> volumes of oxygen**. For <InlineMath math="\text{C}_x\text{H}_y" />, the oxygen needed per molecule is <InlineMath math="x + \tfrac{y}{4}" />.

Propane fits: <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}" />, since <InlineMath math="3 + \tfrac{8}{4} = 5" />. Then <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}^3" /> of propane gives <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}^3" /> of carbon dioxide, and the water condenses to a liquid.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Propene, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_3\text{H}_6" />, needs <InlineMath math="3 + \tfrac{6}{4} = 4.5" /> volumes of oxygen, not <InlineMath math="5" />.
- Butane, <InlineMath math="\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}" />, needs <InlineMath math="6.5" /> volumes of oxygen.
- <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" /> counts the water as a gas, but at room temperature it is a **liquid** and takes no gas volume.
