# Equal moles do not mean equal shares

Aluminium: <InlineMath math="2.7 \div 27 = 0.10" /> mol. Chlorine: <InlineMath math="7.1 \div 71 = 0.10" /> mol. The equation needs **three chlorine molecules for every two aluminium atoms**, so <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of aluminium would need <InlineMath math="0.15" /> mol of chlorine. Only <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol is there, so chlorine runs out first.

<InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of chlorine makes <InlineMath math="0.10 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 0.0667" /> mol of <InlineMath math="\text{AlCl}_3" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.0667 \times 133.5 = 8.9" /> g. It uses <InlineMath math="0.0667" /> mol of aluminium, leaving <InlineMath math="0.0333 \times 27 = 0.90" /> g.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Aluminium is in excess: chlorine runs out first, so aluminium is **not the limiting reactant**.
- <InlineMath math="13.4\text{ g}" /> assumes all <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol of aluminium reacts, which needs more chlorine than there is.
- <InlineMath math="1.8\text{ g}" /> is the mass of aluminium that reacts, <InlineMath math="0.0667 \times 27" />, not the mass left over.
