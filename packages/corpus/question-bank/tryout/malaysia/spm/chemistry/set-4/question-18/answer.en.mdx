# The time halves for every <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" />, so the rate doubles

From <InlineMath math="30" /> to <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" /> the time falls from <InlineMath math="80" /> s to <InlineMath math="40" /> s, and from <InlineMath math="25" /> to <InlineMath math="35^\circ\text{C}" /> from <InlineMath math="113" /> s to <InlineMath math="57" /> s. Each <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> rise **halves the time**, so <InlineMath math="50^\circ\text{C}" /> should take about <InlineMath math="40 \div 2 = 20" /> s.

The rate is proportional to <InlineMath math="1/t" />, so it doubles. At a higher temperature the particles move faster and collide more often, and far more of those collisions carry at least the activation energy, so a larger share of collisions succeeds.

# Why the other options are wrong

- The time does not fall by a fixed number of seconds: it falls by <InlineMath math="33" /> s from <InlineMath math="25" /> to <InlineMath math="30^\circ\text{C}" /> but by only <InlineMath math="17" /> s from <InlineMath math="35" /> to <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />. It **halves**, so it never reaches zero.
- Heating makes particles move faster. Their size does not change.
- The activation energy is a property of the reaction and stays the same. Only the share of particles that reach it grows.
