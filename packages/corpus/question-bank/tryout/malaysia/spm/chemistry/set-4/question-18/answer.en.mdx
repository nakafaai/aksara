# Two changes, two factors of two

**Temperature.** From <InlineMath math="30" /> to <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" /> the time falls from <InlineMath math="80" /> s to <InlineMath math="40" /> s, and from <InlineMath math="25" /> to <InlineMath math="35^\circ\text{C}" /> from <InlineMath math="113" /> s to <InlineMath math="57" /> s. Each <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> rise halves the time, so the original mixture would take about <InlineMath math="40 \div 2 = 20" /> s at <InlineMath math="50^\circ\text{C}" />.

**Concentration.** Diluting <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}^3" /> to <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> halves the thiosulfate concentration while the total volume stays the same. The rate is proportional to that concentration, so the same mass of sulfur takes **twice as long** to form: <InlineMath math="20 \times 2 = 40" /> s.

The heating raises the share of collisions with at least the activation energy, and the dilution halves how often thiosulfate ions meet acid. Each change alone is a factor of two, in opposite directions.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="20" /> s ignores the dilution. The same mass of sulfur still hides the cross, but half the concentration makes it form **half as fast**.
- <InlineMath math="80" /> s squares the effect of concentration. The collision frequency between thiosulfate and acid halves, it does not fall to a quarter.
- The second <InlineMath math="40" /> s gives the right number for a wrong reason: water does not slow the particles, which move at the speed set by the temperature.
