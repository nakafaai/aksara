# One chlorine atom of each molecule gains an electron and the other loses one

In <InlineMath math="\text{Cl}_2" /> chlorine is <InlineMath math="0" />. It becomes <InlineMath math="-1" /> in sodium chloride and <InlineMath math="+1" /> in sodium chlorate(I), <InlineMath math="\text{NaOCl}" />. **So one atom of each molecule is reduced and the other is oxidised**, and the electron simply passes from one to the other: one electron per molecule. The <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of chlorine moves <InlineMath math="0.0100\text{ mol}" /> of electrons.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="1.20\times10^{22}" /> treats both atoms as reduced, as when chlorine becomes chloride ions. **Half of the chlorine is oxidised instead**.
- Sodium hydroxide keeps every oxidation number: sodium stays <InlineMath math="+1" />, oxygen <InlineMath math="-2" /> and hydrogen <InlineMath math="+1" />.
- <InlineMath math="1.20\times10^{22}" /> counts the electron twice, once as lost and once as gained.
