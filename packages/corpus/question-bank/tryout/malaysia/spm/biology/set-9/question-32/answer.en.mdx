# Follow the gene into the yeast, then follow the protein into the doses

The yeast carries only **one viral gene**, so it makes one viral protein and never a whole virus. To count the doses, keep every quantity in the same unit: <InlineMath math="20\text{ µg}" /> is <InlineMath math="0.02\text{ mg}" />.

- I: a virus needs its full set of genes to copy itself, and the vaccine holds only one protein. That protein trains the immune system without any risk of infection. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="1000\text{ L}" /> at <InlineMath math="20\text{ mg}" /> per litre is <InlineMath math="20\,000\text{ mg}" />, half of that is <InlineMath math="10\,000\text{ mg}" />, and dividing by <InlineMath math="0.02\text{ mg}" /> per dose gives <InlineMath math="500\,000" /> doses. Statement II is correct.
- III: a yeast cell copies its plasmids before it buds, so each bud inherits the gene. A single changed cell can grow into a fermenter full of protein makers. Statement III is correct.
- IV: antibodies and memory cells are made in the vaccinated person's body. They do not change the genes in eggs or sperm, so immunity from a vaccine is not inherited. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, **how one changed yeast cell fills a whole fermenter**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and **lets an acquired immunity pass through genes**.
- I, III and IV only accepts IV and rejects II, **although <InlineMath math="10\text{ g}" /> divided by <InlineMath math="20\text{ µg}" /> gives <InlineMath math="500\,000" /> doses**.
