# A higher rate in a small group can still mean fewer cases

The rate per <InlineMath math="1000" /> births says how likely one baby is to be affected. The **number of cases** in a group is that rate times the number of births, so a common low-risk group can outnumber a rare high-risk one.

<BlockMath math="\frac{10}{1.1}\approx9.1,\qquad 2000\times\frac{0.8}{1000}=1.6,\qquad 100\times\frac{10}{1000}=1.0" />

- I: in meiosis a pair of chromosome <InlineMath math="21" /> can fail to separate, so one gamete gets two copies. After fertilisation the baby has three copies, <InlineMath math="46+1=47" /> chromosomes. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="10" /> per <InlineMath math="1000" /> against <InlineMath math="1.1" /> per <InlineMath math="1000" /> is about <InlineMath math="9" /> times. Statement II is correct.
- III: the younger mothers are expected to have <InlineMath math="1.6" /> affected babies and the older mothers <InlineMath math="1.0" />, so more are born to the younger mothers despite their lower risk. Statement III is correct.
- IV: Down syndrome comes from a whole extra chromosome, a change in chromosome number that usually arises new in one gamete. It is not a recessive allele carried by the parents. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, **forgetting that a large low-risk group can produce more cases**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and **treats a chromosome mutation as a recessive allele**.
- I, III and IV only accepts IV and rejects II, **although <InlineMath math="10" /> is about <InlineMath math="9" /> times <InlineMath math="1.1" />**.
