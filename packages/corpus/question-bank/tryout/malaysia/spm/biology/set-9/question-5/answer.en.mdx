# Compare treatments that differ in one ion at a time

Sodium chloride and sodium nitrate share sodium, so any difference between them is due to **chloride**. Dialysis removes free ions, so activity that does not return after dialysis must come from something **bound to the enzyme**.

- I: without chloride the enzyme works at only <InlineMath math="10\%" />, and with chloride back it works at <InlineMath math="100\%" />. A non-protein ion the enzyme needs is a cofactor. Statement I is correct.
- II: sodium nitrate brings the same sodium but no chloride and gives only <InlineMath math="15\%" />, so chloride is what matters. Statement II is correct.
- III: dialysis would remove free mercury ions, yet activity stays at <InlineMath math="5\%" /> even with chloride added. The mercury must stay bound, changing the enzyme for good. Statement III is correct.
- IV: chloride switches the enzyme on and can be removed and replaced. Mercury switches it off permanently. They act in opposite ways. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, **although dialysis cannot restore the mercury-treated enzyme**.
- II and IV only rejects I and **treats a poison as a cofactor**.
- I, III and IV only accepts IV and rejects II, **missing the nitrate control that isolates chloride**.
