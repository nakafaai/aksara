# Count doublings, then turn them into time

Each doubling multiplies the cells by <InlineMath math="2" />, so ten doublings multiply them by about <InlineMath math="1000" />. A tumour grows **unseen for most of its life**, because the early doublings add very few cells.

<BlockMath math="2^{30}\approx10^9,\qquad 30\times60=1800\text{ days}\approx4.9\text{ years},\qquad 2^{10}\approx1000" />

- I: <InlineMath math="2^{30}\approx10^9" />, the number of cells in a tumour that can just be found. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="30" /> doublings of <InlineMath math="60" /> days each take <InlineMath math="1800" /> days, about <InlineMath math="5" /> years. Statement II is correct.
- III: ten doublings multiply the cells by <InlineMath math="2^{10}\approx1000" />, not by ten. A tumour found at <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}^3" /> would reach about <InlineMath math="1" /> litre. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: normal cells divide only when signalled and stop when told to. Mutations in genes that control the cell cycle remove these brakes, so mitosis carries on unchecked. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, **the loss of cell-cycle control behind every tumour**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and accepts III, **multiplying by <InlineMath math="10" /> instead of <InlineMath math="2" /> to the power <InlineMath math="10" />**.
- I, III and IV only accepts III and rejects II, although <InlineMath math="30\times60" /> days **is about <InlineMath math="5" /> years**.
