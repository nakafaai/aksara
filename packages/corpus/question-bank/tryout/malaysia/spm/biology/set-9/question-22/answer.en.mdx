# Turn light passing through into light absorbed, then compare the two leaves

Light that does not pass through the leaf has been absorbed, so **absorbed = <InlineMath math="100\%" /> minus transmitted**. The size of the drop must be judged against what the leaf absorbed before, not against <InlineMath math="100\%" />.

<BlockMath math="100-20=80\%,\qquad 100-32=68\%,\qquad \frac{80-68}{80}=\frac{12}{80}=0.15" />

- I: absorption falls from <InlineMath math="80\%" /> to <InlineMath math="68\%" />, a drop of <InlineMath math="12" /> out of <InlineMath math="80" />, which is <InlineMath math="15\%" /> of what the leaf absorbed. Statement I is correct.
- II: the normal leaf, which moves its chloroplasts to the side walls, loses only <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of its rate, while the mutant that cannot move them is bleached. Chloroplasts in a line shade one another and escape the damage. Statement II is correct.
- III: in weak light both leaves spread their chloroplasts across the walls facing the light and absorb the most, <InlineMath math="80\%" />. Statement III is correct.
- IV: in weak light the mutant lets through <InlineMath math="20\%" />, the same as the normal leaf, so it absorbs the same light and holds as much chlorophyll. Its later damage comes from being unable to move its chloroplasts. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects II, **the protective reason the mutant lacks**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and accepts IV, **missing that both leaves absorbed <InlineMath math="80\%" /> at first**.
- I, II and IV only rejects III, **although spreading out catches the most light when light is scarce**.
