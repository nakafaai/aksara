# Given

A double-stranded DNA molecule has <InlineMath math="1000" /> base pairs (<InlineMath math="2000" /> total bases), of which **<InlineMath math="30\%" /> are adenine (A)**. Each A and thymine (T) pair forms <InlineMath math="2" /> hydrogen bonds, while each guanine (G) and cytosine (C) pair forms <InlineMath math="3" /> hydrogen bonds.

# Asked

How many cytosine bases and how many total hydrogen bonds exist between the two DNA strands?

# Solution

Picture a DNA ladder with <InlineMath math="1000" /> rungs (<InlineMath math="2000" /> half-rungs). Since every A pairs with T using <InlineMath math="2" /> glue dots, A and T claim <InlineMath math="60\%" /> of all bases, leaving **the remaining <InlineMath math="40\%" /> split evenly between G (<InlineMath math="20\%" />) and C (<InlineMath math="20\%" />)** with <InlineMath math="3" /> glue dots per rung.

> In double-stranded DNA, adenine always equals thymine in share, and the remaining percentage divides equally between guanine and cytosine.

Slide the adenine share <InlineMath math="a" /> below to see both the cytosine count and total hydrogen bonds drop linearly as A and T pairs replace G and C pairs.

Every extra <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of adenine replaces <InlineMath math="100" /> triple-bonded G and C pairs with double-bonded pairs, dropping total bonds by <InlineMath math="100" />. At <InlineMath math="a = 30\%" />, the readouts give <InlineMath math="400" /> and <InlineMath math="2400" />.

## Base pairing and bond count

Apply Chargaff's rule to find **the cytosine count**, then weight A and T pairs by <InlineMath math="2" /> and G and C pairs by <InlineMath math="3" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A = T = 30\% &\implies G + C = 40\% \implies C = 20\% \\
\text{number of } C &= 0.20 \times 2000 = 400\text{ bases} \\
A\text{ and }T\text{ pairs} &= 0.30 \times 2000 = 600, \quad G\text{ and }C\text{ pairs} = 400 \\
\text{hydrogen bonds} &= 600(2) + 400(3) = 1200 + 1200 = 2400
\end{aligned}" />

Quick check: <InlineMath math="600" /> plus <InlineMath math="400" /> recovers all <InlineMath math="1000" /> base pairs cleanly.

## Why other options fail

- Choosing <InlineMath math="400" /> and <InlineMath math="2600" /> swaps the bond counts, forgetting that **G and C pairs carry three hydrogen bonds**.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="600" /> and <InlineMath math="2600" /> equates cytosine with adenine, even though cytosine pairs only with guanine.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="800" /> and <InlineMath math="2800" /> assigns the entire leftover <InlineMath math="40\%" /> to cytosine without sharing equally with guanine.

# Answer

There are **<InlineMath math="400" />** cytosine bases and **<InlineMath math="2400" />** hydrogen bonds in total.
