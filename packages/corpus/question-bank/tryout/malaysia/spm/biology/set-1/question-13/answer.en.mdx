# Given

Before training, an athlete's resting heart rate is <InlineMath math="72\text{ beats min}^{-1}" /> with a stroke volume of <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" />. After training, resting heart rate drops to <InlineMath math="56\text{ beats min}^{-1}" /> while **resting cardiac output stays unchanged**.

During maximum exercise after training, heart rate reaches <InlineMath math="180\text{ beats min}^{-1}" /> with a stroke volume of <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Asked

What is the resting stroke volume after training, and how many times larger is cardiac output during maximum exercise than at rest?

# Solution

Picture the heart as a water pump: total volume pumped per minute equals volume per squeeze times squeezes per minute. Because training strengthens the heart muscle, **each beat pushes more blood so the heart needs fewer beats** to keep pumping <InlineMath math="5040\text{ cm}^3" /> per minute.

> When resting cardiac output stays constant, a lower resting heart rate always requires an inversely larger stroke volume.

Slide resting heart rate <InlineMath math="r" /> along the constant <InlineMath math="5040\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" /> curve below from <InlineMath math="72" /> down to <InlineMath math="56" /> to watch stroke volume climb to <InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" />.

Moving left along the hyperbola as resting heart rate falls lifts the green marker directly to <InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" />.

## Stroke volume and output ratio

Compute resting cardiac output first, solve for the **new resting stroke volume**, then divide maximum exercise output by resting output.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{resting output} &= 72 \times 70 = 5040\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1} \\
\text{new stroke volume} &= \frac{5040}{56} = 90\text{ cm}^3 \\
\text{maximum output} &= 180 \times 120 = 21{,}600\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1} \\
\text{ratio} &= \frac{21{,}600}{5040} = \frac{30}{7} \approx 4.3\text{ times}
\end{aligned}" />

Quick check: multiplying <InlineMath math="56" /> beats by <InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" /> restores <InlineMath math="5040\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" /> exactly.

## Why other options fail

- Choosing <InlineMath math="54\text{ cm}^3" /> lowers stroke volume with heart rate, even though **stroke volume must rise** to hold output steady.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="3.2" /> times divides only heart rates (<InlineMath math="180" /> by <InlineMath math="56" />) and ignores the larger exercise stroke volume.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="2.5" /> times reuses the old <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" /> stroke volume instead of the exercise value of <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}^3" />.

# Answer

The resting stroke volume after training is **<InlineMath math="90\text{ cm}^3" />** and maximum cardiac output is about **<InlineMath math="4.3" />** times the resting output.
