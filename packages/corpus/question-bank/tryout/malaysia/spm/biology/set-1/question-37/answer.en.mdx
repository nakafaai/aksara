# Given

During spermatogenesis, a primary spermatocyte (<InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes) completes **meiosis I normally**, forming two secondary spermatocytes with <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes each.

In **only one** secondary spermatocyte, the two sister chromatids of chromosome <InlineMath math="21" /> fail to separate during meiosis II, while the other cell divides normally. Each sperm fertilises a normal egg (<InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes).

# Asked

Find what fraction of the four sperm would produce a child with Down syndrome, and how many chromosomes that child would have.

# Solution

Imagine sorting two full packs of <InlineMath math="23" /> cards into four envelopes. The first pack deals cleanly (<InlineMath math="23" /> and <InlineMath math="23" />), while the second slips both copies of card <InlineMath math="21" /> into one envelope (<InlineMath math="24" /> and <InlineMath math="22" />), so only <InlineMath math="1" /> of <InlineMath math="4" /> envelopes carries an extra chromosome <InlineMath math="21" />.

> Non-disjunction in one cell during meiosis II affects only two of the four gametes, producing one gamete with an extra chromosome and one missing a chromosome.

Track **all four sperm** and add the <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes contributed by the normal egg.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{sperm chromosomes} &: 23,\ 23,\ 24\ (+21),\ 22\ (-21) \\
\text{zygote chromosomes} &: 46,\ 46,\ 47\ (\text{trisomy } 21),\ 45\ (\text{monosomy } 21)
\end{aligned}" />

Follow the cell-division tree below to confirm that exactly one of the four sperm leads to trisomy <InlineMath math="21" /> (Down syndrome).

    B --> S3["Sperm 24 (+21) + egg 23 -> 47 Down syndrome"]
    B --> S4["Sperm 22 (-21) + egg 23 -> 45 monosomy"]`}
/>

Only the sperm with <InlineMath math="24" /> chromosomes produces **three copies of chromosome <InlineMath math="21" />** (<InlineMath math="24 + 23 = 47" />), giving a fraction of <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" />.

## Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="47" /> matches non-disjunction in meiosis I, where two sperm get <InlineMath math="24" />, yet **here meiosis I is normal** and only one cell fails.
- <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" /> and <InlineMath math="45" /> follows the <InlineMath math="22" />-chromosome sperm, which gives monosomy (<InlineMath math="45" />), not Down syndrome (<InlineMath math="47" />).
- <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="45" /> combines both mistakes at once.

# Answer

A fraction of **<InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" />** of the four sperm would produce a child with Down syndrome, and that child would have **<InlineMath math="47" />** chromosomes.
