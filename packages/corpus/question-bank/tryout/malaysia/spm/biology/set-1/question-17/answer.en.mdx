# Given

An earthworm body segment behaves like a cylinder filled with incompressible coelomic fluid, so **its volume <InlineMath math="\pi r^2 L" /> stays constant**. During movement, the segment length doubles to <InlineMath math="2L" />.

# Asked

By what percentage does the segment radius decrease, and which muscles contract to produce this shape change?

# Solution

Picture squeezing the waist of a water balloon with a ring of fingers. Because water cannot be compressed, the balloon shoots out longer and thinner, and **the earthworm's circular muscles act just like that squeezing ring**.

> Because cylinder volume depends on the square of the radius, doubling the length halves the squared radius so the radius drops to one over root two, not one half.

At <InlineMath math="k = 2" />, the green curve sits well above the orange trap: the new radius is about <InlineMath math="70.7\%" />, giving a <InlineMath math="29.3\%" /> decrease (about <InlineMath math="29\%" />).

## Radius change and muscle action

Equate cylinder volumes before and after stretching, then identify **the antagonistic muscle pair** that makes the segment thin and long.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\pi r^2 L &= \pi r'^2 (2L) \implies r'^2 = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \\
r' &= \frac{r}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 r \\
\text{decrease} &\approx (1 - 0.707) \times 100\% \approx 29\%
\end{aligned}" />

A long, thin segment forms when **the circular muscles contract** while the longitudinal muscles relax.

## Why other options fail

- Choosing <InlineMath math="50\%" /> treats radius as inversely proportional to length, forgetting that **length varies inversely with <InlineMath math="r^2" />**.
- Choosing longitudinal muscles produces the opposite shape, making the segment short and thick.
- Choosing <InlineMath math="75\%" /> assumes cross-sectional area drops to a quarter when it only halves.

# Answer

The radius decreases by about **<InlineMath math="29\%" />**, caused by contraction of the circular muscles.
