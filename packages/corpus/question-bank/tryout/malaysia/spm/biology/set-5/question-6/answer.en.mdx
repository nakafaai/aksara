# Follow one cell through a division without a spindle, then through normal ones

The spindle only **pulls** separated chromatids to two poles. Without it, the chromatids still part at the centromere, but all of them stay in one cell, which therefore doubles its chromosome number.

- I: the <InlineMath math="16" /> chromosomes split into <InlineMath math="32" /> separate chromatids, now counted as <InlineMath math="32" /> chromosomes, and no cell division follows. Statement I is correct.
- II: normal mitosis copies the chromosome number exactly, so a <InlineMath math="32" />-chromosome cell gives two <InlineMath math="32" />-chromosome cells. Statement II is correct.
- III: before the next division, S phase copies each of the <InlineMath math="32" /> chromosomes, so at metaphase each has two chromatids, <InlineMath math="32\times2=64" />. Statement III is correct.
- IV: replication happens once, as usual. The doubling comes from the **failure to share** the chromatids between two cells. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, forgetting that **S phase doubles the chromatids** again before metaphase.
- II and III only rejects I, **the step that explains where the <InlineMath math="32" /> chromosomes come from**.
- I, II and IV only blames an extra round of replication, **although the spindle, not replication, is what colchicine stops**.
