# Count survivors first, then count alleles, not people

The share of **carriers** is a share of people. The **allele frequency** counts copies: each carrier holds one <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^S" />, each <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^S\text{Hb}^S" /> person two, out of two alleles per person.

- I: <InlineMath math="304" /> of <InlineMath math="820" /> adults are carriers, about <InlineMath math="37\%" />, against <InlineMath math="320" /> of <InlineMath math="1000" /> newborns, <InlineMath math="32\%" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="37\%" /> is the share of carrier **people**. Counting alleles gives about <InlineMath math="19\%" />, close to the <InlineMath math="20\%" /> at birth, since most <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^S\text{Hb}^S" /> children die. Statement II is wrong.
- III: sickle-cell anaemia comes from a **gene mutation**, a change of one base that alters one amino acid in haemoglobin. No part of a chromosome is lost. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: without malaria, carriers survive no better than <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^A\text{Hb}^A" /> people, while <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^S\text{Hb}^S" /> children still die young, so <InlineMath math="\text{Hb}^S" /> is steadily removed. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and IV only takes the **share of carriers as the allele frequency**.
- II and III only rejects both correct statements and accepts a **chromosome mutation**.
- I, III and IV only calls sickle-cell anaemia a chromosome mutation, **although one base change causes it**.
