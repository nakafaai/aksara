# Measure how much two species share, then see what changes when one is removed

Species that share a habitat compete less if they **use it in different ways**. The overlap of two species is the share they have in common in each zone, which is **the smaller of their two shares**, added over the zones. Removing a species is a test: if another moves into the space it leaves, the second was **held back** from part of what it could use.

- I: A and B have <InlineMath math="10\%" /> in common at the top, <InlineMath math="20\%" /> in the middle and <InlineMath math="10\%" /> at the bottom, <InlineMath math="40\%" /> in all, so most of their feeding does not overlap. Statement I is correct.
- II: C spends <InlineMath math="15\%" /> of its feeding time in the middle zone and A spends <InlineMath math="20\%" />, so C spends less, not more. Statement II is wrong.
- III: without B, A's share in the middle zone rises from <InlineMath math="20\%" /> to <InlineMath math="50\%" />. B's presence must normally push A out of that zone. Statement III is correct.
- IV: A can clearly feed in the middle zone, as it does when B is gone, so with B present it uses less of the tree than it could. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, III and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects IV, **although A's use of the tree widens once B is gone**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and accepts II, **misreading <InlineMath math="15\%" /> as more than <InlineMath math="20\%" />**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts II, **although C feeds less in the middle zone than A**.
