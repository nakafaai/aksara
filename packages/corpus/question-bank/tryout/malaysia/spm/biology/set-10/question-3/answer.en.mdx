# Ask which way water moves between each bacterium and its broth

Water moves by osmosis from **higher water potential to lower**. Salt lowers the water potential of the broth. Once the broth is saltier than the cytoplasm, water leaves the bacteria, and a cell short of water cannot grow or divide.

- I: in <InlineMath math="20\%" /> salt the broth has a far lower water potential than the cytoplasm, so the bacteria lose water and plasmolyse. The count falls from <InlineMath math="10^3" /> to <InlineMath math="10^2" />, a tenfold drop. Statement I is correct.
- II: the count rises from <InlineMath math="10^3" /> to <InlineMath math="10^8" />, a factor of <InlineMath math="10^5" />. Since <InlineMath math="10^5=100\times1000\approx2^{6.6}\times2^{10}" />, that is about <InlineMath math="17" /> doublings in <InlineMath math="24" /> hours, one every <InlineMath math="1.4" /> hours or so. Statement II is correct.
- III: salt works by drawing water out of the bacteria by osmosis. The same effect comes from sugar in jam, which is not a poison. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: water enters a cell only from a region of higher water potential. To draw water from brine, a halophile must keep its own water potential even lower, by holding more solutes. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects II and IV, **the doubling time hidden in the rise to <InlineMath math="10^8" /> and how halophiles still take in water**.
- II and IV only rejects I, **the tenfold fall in <InlineMath math="20\%" /> salt**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts III, **treating salt as a poison instead of a cause of water loss**.
