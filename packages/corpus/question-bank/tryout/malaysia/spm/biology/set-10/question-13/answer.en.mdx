# See which store is spent so that blood glucose can stay steady

Blood glucose is held in a narrow range by **negative feedback**. When it starts to fall, the pancreas releases more **glucagon**, and the liver first breaks down glycogen, then makes new glucose from amino acids and other substances. Fat becomes the main fuel, and ketone bodies rise.

- I: glycogen falls from <InlineMath math="100" /> to <InlineMath math="10\text{ g}" />, a <InlineMath math="90\%" /> fall, while glucose falls from <InlineMath math="5.0" /> to <InlineMath math="4.5\text{ mmol}" /> per litre, only <InlineMath math="10\%" />. The store is spent so that the blood stays steady. Statement I is correct.
- II: from <InlineMath math="24" /> to <InlineMath math="72" /> hours glycogen hardly changes, yet glucose drops only from <InlineMath math="4.5" /> to <InlineMath math="4.0" />. The liver must be making glucose from non-carbohydrate sources while fat, shown by the ketones, supplies most of the energy. Statement II is correct.
- III: a fall in blood glucose makes the pancreas secrete more glucagon, not less, so that the liver releases glucose. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the liver uses <InlineMath math="90\text{ g}" /> of glycogen in the first day, which releases <InlineMath math="90\times17=1530\text{ kJ}" />, about <InlineMath math="19\%" /> of the <InlineMath math="8000\text{ kJ}" /> the body uses. That is less than a fifth, so fat and other stores must supply the rest. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, **although <InlineMath math="1530\text{ kJ}" /> is under a fifth of a day's <InlineMath math="8000\text{ kJ}" />**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and **runs the glucagon response backwards**.
- I, III and IV only accepts III and rejects II, **missing how glucose stays steady once glycogen is gone**.
