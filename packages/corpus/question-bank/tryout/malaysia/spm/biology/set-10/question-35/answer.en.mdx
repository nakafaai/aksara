# Give each kitten one X from its mother, and an X or a Y from its father

A son gets his only X from his **mother**, so his colour is hers to give. A daughter gets one X from each parent. A female with <InlineMath math="X^O" /> and <InlineMath math="X^B" /> shows both colours in patches, because each patch of skin comes from cells in which a different X is switched on.

<BlockMath math="X^OX^B\times X^BY\to\tfrac14\,X^OX^B,\ \tfrac14\,X^BX^B,\ \tfrac14\,X^OY,\ \tfrac14\,X^BY" />

- I: a male has one X, so he carries only one colour allele and is orange or black. Only a female, with two X chromosomes, can carry both. Statement I is correct.
- II: each daughter gets the father's <InlineMath math="X^B" /> and either <InlineMath math="X^O" /> or <InlineMath math="X^B" /> from her mother, so half are <InlineMath math="X^OX^B" />, tortoiseshell. Statement II is correct.
- III: half the kittens are male, and half of the males get the mother's <InlineMath math="X^O" />, so <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac14" /> are orange males. Statement III is correct.
- IV: the sons get their only X from the black mother, <InlineMath math="X^B" />, and a Y from the father, so every son is black, whatever the father's colour. Statement IV is correct.

So all four statements are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only rejects IV, **although a son's colour comes only from his mother**.
- I and III only rejects II and IV, **the two crosses that follow the X from mother to child**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I, **although a male has only one X and one colour allele**.
