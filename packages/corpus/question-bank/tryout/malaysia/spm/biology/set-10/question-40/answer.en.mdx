# Read each ten-year drop, and find where the curve crosses the limit

Germination is still above <InlineMath math="85\%" /> at year <InlineMath math="20" /> but below it at year <InlineMath math="30" />, so the limit is crossed **between** those tests. The drops between tests are not equal: the seeds lose viability **faster as they age**. A seed is alive and slowly using its store, so cold and dryness, which slow its chemistry, buy time.

- I: from <InlineMath math="90\%" /> at year <InlineMath math="20" /> to <InlineMath math="80\%" /> at year <InlineMath math="30" /> is a fall of <InlineMath math="1\%" /> a year, so <InlineMath math="85\%" /> is reached <InlineMath math="5" /> years after year <InlineMath math="20" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: the drops are <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="10" /> and <InlineMath math="20" /> percentage points, roughly doubling each decade, not the same each time. Statement II is wrong.
- III: a dry, frozen seed respires very slowly and its enzymes barely work, so its food store and cells last for decades, while seeds at room temperature fall to <InlineMath math="50\%" /> in <InlineMath math="5" /> years. Statement III is correct.
- IV: old varieties and wild relatives carry alleles, such as for disease resistance or tolerance of heat, that breeders may need later. Keeping them keeps that genetic variety. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, III and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects IV, **the reason the seeds are kept at all**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and accepts II, **although the drops grow from <InlineMath math="3" /> to <InlineMath math="20" />**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts II, **reading a speeding decline as a steady one**.
