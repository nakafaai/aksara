# Compare each drink with the meal eaten alone, then balance the day and the month

Only part of the iron in food is absorbed. **Vitamin C** helps iron dissolve in a form the intestine can take up, while substances in **tea** bind iron so it passes out unabsorbed. A day's intake is the sum over the meals, and any shortfall each day is taken from the store.

- I: orange juice raises the iron absorbed from <InlineMath math="0.5" /> to <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ mg}" />, which triples it, not doubles it. Statement I is wrong.
- II: tea lowers it from <InlineMath math="0.5" /> to <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ mg}" />, a drop of <InlineMath math="0.3" /> out of <InlineMath math="0.5" />, which is <InlineMath math="60\%" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: three meals with tea give <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ mg}" /> a day, <InlineMath math="0.9\text{ mg}" /> short of the <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ mg}" /> she needs. Over <InlineMath math="30" /> days the store falls by <InlineMath math="27\text{ mg}" />. Statement III is correct.
- IV: one meal with orange juice gives <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ mg}" /> and two meals with tea give <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ mg}" /> each, <InlineMath math="1.9\text{ mg}" /> in all, which covers her need. Statement IV is correct.

So statements II, III and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only accepts I and rejects IV, **reading a threefold rise as twofold**.
- II and IV only rejects III, **although a daily shortfall of <InlineMath math="0.9\text{ mg}" /> adds up to <InlineMath math="27\text{ mg}" /> in a month**.
- I, III and IV only accepts I and rejects II, **although tea removes <InlineMath math="60\%" /> of the absorption**.
