# Multiply the chances from each parent, then ask about at least one sibling

A sibling matches fully only if they got the **same cluster from the mother and the same from the father**. Each is a <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" /> chance, so both together are <InlineMath math="\tfrac14" />. For "at least one", find the chance that **none** match and take it from <InlineMath math="1" />.

<BlockMath math="\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac14,\qquad 1-\left(\tfrac34\right)^3=1-\tfrac{27}{64}=\tfrac{37}{64}\approx0.58" />

- I: matching the cluster from one parent is a <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" /> chance, but a full match needs the clusters from both parents, so the chance is <InlineMath math="\tfrac14" />. Statement I is wrong.
- II: each sibling fails to match with chance <InlineMath math="\tfrac34" />, so all three fail with chance <InlineMath math="\tfrac{27}{64}" />, and at least one matches with chance <InlineMath math="\tfrac{37}{64}" />, about <InlineMath math="58\%" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: an identical twin has the same genes, so the surface proteins on the kidney are the same. The patient's lymphocytes see them as self and do not attack, and <InlineMath math="95\%" /> of these grafts still work. Statement III is correct.
- IV: immunosuppressant drugs weaken the lymphocytes so they do not reject the kidney, but this also weakens the defence against pathogens, so infections become more likely. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only accepts I, **counting the match from only one parent**.
- III and IV only rejects II and **lets drugs that weaken immunity protect against infection**.
- I, II and IV only rejects III and accepts I and IV, **missing why a twin's kidney is not attacked**.
