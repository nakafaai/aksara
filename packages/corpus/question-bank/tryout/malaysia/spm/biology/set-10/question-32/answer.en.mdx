# Count the chromosomes in each gamete and check whether each one has a partner

A gamete carries **half the diploid number**, so each parent gives <InlineMath math="9" />. Meiosis needs every chromosome to **pair with a matching partner**. Nine radish and nine cabbage chromosomes do not match, but doubling gives every chromosome an identical copy to pair with.

<BlockMath math="\frac{18}{2}=9,\qquad 9+9=18,\qquad 2\times18=36,\qquad 18+9=27" />

- I: a gamete has half of <InlineMath math="18" />, which is <InlineMath math="9" />, so the hybrid has <InlineMath math="9+9=18" /> chromosomes, not <InlineMath math="36" />. Statement I is wrong.
- II: the <InlineMath math="9" /> radish chromosomes and <InlineMath math="9" /> cabbage chromosomes are all different, so none can find a partner in meiosis, and normal gametes cannot form. Statement II is correct.
- III: doubling gives <InlineMath math="36" /> chromosomes in <InlineMath math="18" /> matched pairs, so meiosis works and each gamete gets <InlineMath math="18" />. Statement III is correct.
- IV: a gamete of <InlineMath math="18" /> from the new plant and a radish gamete of <InlineMath math="9" /> give <InlineMath math="27" /> chromosomes. The <InlineMath math="9" /> cabbage chromosomes have no partners, so the offspring are sterile. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only accepts I, **forgetting that a gamete carries half the diploid number**.
- II, III and IV only accepts IV, **although a cross back to radish leaves cabbage chromosomes unpaired**.
- I and IV only rejects the two pairing statements and **accepts both counting errors**.
