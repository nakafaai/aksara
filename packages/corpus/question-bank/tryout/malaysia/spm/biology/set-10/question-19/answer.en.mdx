# Separate the treatments that remove something for a while from those that destroy the enzyme

Browning needs the **enzyme**, its substrates and **oxygen**. Nitrogen and cold only hold the reaction back while they last, so browning starts again once air or warmth returns. Boiling **denatures** the enzyme for good, and acid changes the active site while the pH stays low.

- I: the slices in nitrogen did not brown only because oxygen was missing, and their enzyme is unharmed, so in air they brown. The blanched slices have a denatured enzyme and stay pale even in air. Statement I is correct.
- II: the cells are already cut open, so killing them would not stop the enzyme. The acid lowers the pH far below the enzyme's optimum, which changes its active site and slows it. Statement II is wrong.
- III: at <InlineMath math="4" /> °C the score halves to <InlineMath math="4" />, so the enzyme still works slowly. Once warm again it speeds up. Statement III is correct.
- IV: boiling water denatures the enzyme permanently, so the score stays at <InlineMath math="0" /> even after the slices cool. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, III and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects IV, **the permanent change that heat makes to an enzyme**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and **treats a missing gas as if it had destroyed the enzyme**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts II, **although the enzyme is already free of the cells when they are cut**.
