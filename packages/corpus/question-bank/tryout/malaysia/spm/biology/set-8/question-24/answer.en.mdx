# Root hairs take in water only while the soil water is more dilute than their sap, and the soil water gets stronger as it is used up

Water enters a root hair by osmosis, from the more dilute soil water into the more concentrated cell sap. So compare the **concentration of dissolved particles** in the soil water with the <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol per dm}^3" /> in the sap. Count every particle in the soil water, the <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ mol}" /> already there as well as the fertiliser.

## Just after the fertiliser dissolves

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{P: } \frac{0.010}{0.80} &= 0.0125\text{ mol per dm}^3\\
\text{Q: } \frac{0.010+5\times0.020}{0.80} = \frac{0.110}{0.80} &= 0.1375\text{ mol per dm}^3\\
\text{R: } \frac{0.010+12\times0.020}{0.80} = \frac{0.250}{0.80} &= 0.3125\text{ mol per dm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

In pot R the soil water is **more concentrated than the cell sap**, so water leaves the root hairs although the soil is wet. The cells lose water, and the plant wilts.

## As the plant uses the soil water

The plant takes in water but leaves the particles behind, so the same particles end up in less water. Water stops entering when the soil water reaches <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol per dm}^3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{Q: volume left} &= \frac{0.110\text{ mol}}{0.30\text{ mol per dm}^3} \approx 0.367\text{ dm}^3 = 367\text{ cm}^3, & \text{absorbed} &= 800-367 \approx 430\text{ cm}^3\\
\text{P: volume left} &= \frac{0.010}{0.30} \approx 0.033\text{ dm}^3 = 33\text{ cm}^3, & \text{absorbed} &= 800-33 \approx 770\text{ cm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

Inside the root, water crosses the cortex into the xylem. In the xylem, **cohesion** between water molecules holds the water as an unbroken column, and the **transpiration pull** of the leaves draws that column up the stem.

- I: <InlineMath math="0.3125" /> is above <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol per dm}^3" />, so water leaves the root hairs in pot R. Statement I is correct.
- II: the plant in pot Q can absorb about <InlineMath math="430\text{ cm}^3" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: pot Q does start below half the sap concentration, but its soil water gets stronger as it is used and reaches the sap's concentration after about <InlineMath math="430\text{ cm}^3" />, against about <InlineMath math="770\text{ cm}^3" /> in pot P. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: water crosses the cortex into the xylem and rises as a continuous column drawn up by transpiration pull. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, III and IV only treats the starting concentration of pot Q as fixed, **forgetting that the soil water gets more concentrated as the plant uses it**, and so rejects II.
- II and IV only leaves out the <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ mol}" /> already in the soil water, gets <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0.240}{0.80}=0.30" /> for pot R, **equal to the sap**, and so expects no net movement.
- I and II only rejects IV, as if the water in the xylem rose as separate drops pushed only by the root, **although cohesion keeps it as one column that transpiration pull draws up**.
