# The beaker holds nine times the tube's volume, so every gram that leaves counts nine times more inside the tube

The table only gives you the beaker. To judge the tube, follow the **glucose mass**: whatever leaves the tube turns up in the beaker. The beaker holds <InlineMath math="180\text{ cm}^3" /> and the tube only <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" />, so a small rise in the beaker means a big fall inside the tube.

Start with the tube at minute <InlineMath math="0" />: <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" /> in <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> is <InlineMath math="10\text{ g per }100\text{ cm}^3" />. If the beaker reads <InlineMath math="c" /> grams per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />, then

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{glucose in the beaker} &= c\times\frac{180}{100} = 1.8c\text{ g}\\
\text{glucose left in the tube} &= 2.0-1.8c\text{ g}\\
\text{tube concentration} &= \frac{2.0-1.8c}{20}\times100 = 10-9c\text{ g per }100\text{ cm}^3
\end{aligned}" />

- I: at minute <InlineMath math="20" />, <InlineMath math="10-9\times0.64 = 10-5.76 = 4.24\text{ g per }100\text{ cm}^3" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: by minute <InlineMath math="10" /> the beaker holds <InlineMath math="1.8\times0.40 = 0.72\text{ g}" />, so <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0.72}{10} = 0.072\text{ g}" /> leaves each minute. You need the **beaker's volume** here, because the table gives a concentration, not a mass. Statement II is correct.
- III: <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" /> spread through <InlineMath math="200\text{ cm}^3" /> does give <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ g per }100\text{ cm}^3" /> on both sides. But the molecules keep moving and keep crossing in both directions. Only the **net movement** stops, because equal numbers cross each way. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the gradient is the tube concentration minus the beaker concentration.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{gradient at minute 0} &= 10-0 = 10\\
\text{gradient at minute 10} &= (10-9\times0.40)-0.40 = 6.4-0.40 = 6.0 = 60\%\text{ of }10\\
\text{rate from minute 10 to 20} &= \frac{1.8\times(0.64-0.40)}{10} = \frac{0.432}{10} = 0.0432\text{ g per minute}\\
\frac{0.0432}{0.072} &= 0.60 = 60\%
\end{aligned}" />

The rate falls in step with the gradient, which is exactly what diffusion predicts. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- II only treats the tube and the beaker as if they had the same volume, so it takes the tube to <InlineMath math="10-0.64 = 9.36" /> at minute <InlineMath math="20" /> and the gradient at minute <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="9.2" />. **The tube is nine times smaller**, so it loses concentration nine times faster.
- I, II, III and IV believes that no glucose molecules cross the tubing at equilibrium, **when only the net movement stops**.
- I and IV only reads the beaker's rise of <InlineMath math="0.40" /> as <InlineMath math="0.04\text{ g}" /> per minute, **forgetting to multiply the concentration by the beaker's volume**.
