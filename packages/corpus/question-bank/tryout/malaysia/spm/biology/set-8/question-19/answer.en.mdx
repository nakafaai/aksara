# Read each drug from what the pupil and the lens fail to do, and compare the light with the right normal pupil

Without drops, both responses work. In bright light the pupil is small, for the near object it **narrows a little more**, and the lens thickens from <InlineMath math="3.6" /> to <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ mm}" />. Each drug stops some of these changes, and each change that is missing points to one muscle that can no longer contract.

## Statement I

Light follows the pupil's area, and area grows with the **square** of the diameter. With drug X the pupil is <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ mm}" /> at both distances, but the normal pupil it must be compared with is different at each distance:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}

\text{looking 25 cm away:}\quad \left(\frac{2.4}{7.5}\right)^2 &= 0.32^2 \approx 0.102 \approx 10\%
\end{aligned}" />

The near reflex normally narrows the pupil to <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ mm}" />, and drug X stops that too, so the near object needs the darker glasses. Both limits are right. Statement I is correct.

## Statements II and III

- The pupil narrows when the **circular muscles of the iris** contract. With drugs X and Z it stays at <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ mm}" /> in bright light, so those drugs block the circular muscles.
- The lens thickens for a near object when the **ciliary muscles** contract and the suspensory ligaments slacken. With drugs X and Y it stays at <InlineMath math="3.6\text{ mm}" />, so those drugs block the ciliary muscles. With drug Z it still reaches <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ mm}" />.

Statement II matches all three drugs, so it is correct. The radial muscles widen the pupil when they contract. If drug Z or drug X blocked them, the pupil would be small, not <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ mm}" />. Statement III is wrong.

## Statement IV

With drug Z the lens still thickens from <InlineMath math="3.6" /> to <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ mm}" /> for the near object, exactly as with no drops, so the student can focus on it. The iris and the ciliary body work separately, and a **wide pupil does not stop the lens changing shape**. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- II only compares drug X with the **far-object pupil at both distances**, so it thinks the same <InlineMath math="16\%" /> glasses work for the near object, and rejects I. With no drops the pupil is smaller for the near object, so the limit there is about <InlineMath math="10\%" />.
- I and III only swaps the jobs of the iris muscles, **as if the radial muscles narrowed the pupil**, when they widen it.
- I, II and IV only links the **wide pupil to the lens**, but drug Z's own row shows the lens still thickening to <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ mm}" />.
