# The more juice it takes to clear the DCPIP, the less vitamin C the juice holds

Everything starts from the standard. It took <InlineMath math="0.60\text{ cm}^3" /> of a solution with <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mg}" /> per <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^3" /> to clear the DCPIP, so <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of DCPIP always needs a fixed mass of vitamin C:

<BlockMath math="0.60\times1.0 = 0.60\text{ mg of vitamin C}" />

Every other liquid delivered that same <InlineMath math="0.60\text{ mg}" /> in the volume it took. So its concentration is <InlineMath math="0.60\text{ mg}" /> **divided by** its volume, and a larger volume means a weaker juice:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{fresh orange} &= \frac{0.60}{1.20} = 0.50\text{ mg cm}^{-3}\\
\text{boiled orange} &= \frac{0.60}{2.00} = 0.30\text{ mg cm}^{-3}\\
\text{carton drink} &= \frac{0.60}{1.80} \approx 0.333\text{ mg cm}^{-3}\\
\text{diluted guava} &= \frac{0.60}{1.50} = 0.40\text{ mg cm}^{-3}
\end{aligned}" />

The guava was diluted <InlineMath math="5" /> times before the test, so the juice itself is <InlineMath math="5" /> times stronger than the tube showed:

<BlockMath math="0.40\times5 = 2.0\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />

Now put each one per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />, the unit on the label.

- I: boiling took the orange juice from <InlineMath math="0.50" /> to <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />. The loss as a share of what was there **before** boiling is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0.50-0.30}{0.50}\times100\% = 40\%" />. Vitamin C is destroyed by heat. Statement I is correct.
- II: the carton holds <InlineMath math="0.333\times100 \approx 33.3\text{ mg}" /> per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />, less than the <InlineMath math="40\text{ mg}" /> on its label. Statement II is wrong.
- III: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{65}{0.50} = 130\text{ cm}^3" /> of fresh juice, and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{65}{0.30} \approx 217\text{ cm}^3" /> of boiled juice. Statement III is correct.
- IV: the tube showed <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" /> for guava, but that was the diluted juice. Fresh guava juice holds <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />, **four times** the <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" /> of fresh orange juice. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, III and IV only reads the guava tube as the guava juice itself, **forgetting the fivefold dilution**, so <InlineMath math="0.40" /> looks smaller than orange's <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />.
- I, II and III only **multiplies by the volume instead of dividing**, <InlineMath math="0.60\times1.80 = 1.08\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />, which makes the carton look like <InlineMath math="108\text{ mg}" /> per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />.
- III only works out the boiling loss on the **wrong base**, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2.00-1.20}{1.20}\approx67\%" />, by comparing the volumes against the fresh juice's volume instead of comparing the concentrations.
