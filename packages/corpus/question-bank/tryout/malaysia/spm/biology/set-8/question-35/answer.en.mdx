# Track the number of gene-carrying cells, because the percentage hides changes in the total

The table gives the total T lymphocytes and the **percentage** that carry the normal gene. What matters for the child is the **number** of gene-carrying cells per <InlineMath math="\mu\text{L}" />, which is the total times the percentage. Work that out first for every month.

## T lymphocytes that carry the gene in each microlitre

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{X:}\quad & 400\times0.32=128,\quad 320\times0.20=64,\quad 200\times0.08=16,\quad 100\times0.04=4\\
\text{Y:}\quad & 300\times0.15=45,\quad 900\times0.40=360,\quad 1500\times0.60=900,\quad 1600\times0.62=992
\end{aligned}" />

## Statement I

Check the halving in each interval of child X:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{month 6 to 12 (one 6-month step):}\quad & 128\to64 = \tfrac{1}{2}\\
\text{month 12 to 24 (two steps):}\quad & 64\to16 = \tfrac{1}{4} = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\\
\text{month 24 to 36 (two steps):}\quad & 16\to4 = \tfrac{1}{4} = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}
\end{aligned}" />

The number **halves every <InlineMath math="6" /> months**. Two more halvings take it from <InlineMath math="4" /> at month <InlineMath math="36" /> to <InlineMath math="2" /> at month <InlineMath math="42" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> at month <InlineMath math="48" />. Statement I is correct.

## Statement II

The percentage in child Y rises <InlineMath math="\tfrac{60}{15}=4" /> times, but the total also rises <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1500}{300}=5" /> times, so **the number rises <InlineMath math="4\times5=20" /> times**: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{900}{45}=20" />. Statement II is wrong.

## Statements III and IV

The virus puts the normal gene **into a chromosome**, so it is copied with the chromosome and passed to both daughter cells at every division. It is not lost. Child X's treated cells are mature T lymphocytes, which live for a limited time and cannot make new cells of their own kind for ever. As they die, the gene-carrying cells halve every <InlineMath math="6" /> months. Statement III is wrong.

Child Y's treated cells are stem cells in the bone marrow. They keep dividing and keep making new T lymphocytes that carry the gene, so the number rises to about <InlineMath math="1000" /> per <InlineMath math="\mu\text{L}" /> and stays there after a single infusion. Child X has no such source and would need repeated infusions. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- II and IV only **reads the percentage as if it were the number of cells**. In child X the percentage falls <InlineMath math="8" /> times in <InlineMath math="30" /> months, which looks slower than halving every <InlineMath math="6" /> months, and in child Y it rises only <InlineMath math="4" /> times. Multiplying by the changing total gives halving every <InlineMath math="6" /> months in X and a <InlineMath math="20" />-fold rise in Y.
- I, III and IV only thinks **an inserted gene is lost when a cell divides**. A gene inside a chromosome is replicated with it, so the fall in child X comes from treated cells dying, not from cells losing the gene.
- I only misses that **stem cells renew themselves**, and expects child Y to need more infusions too. The level of about <InlineMath math="1000" /> per <InlineMath math="\mu\text{L}" /> from month <InlineMath math="24" /> to month <InlineMath math="36" /> shows a lasting source of new gene-carrying cells.
