# Each polar body marks a finished meiotic division, and the second one needs a sperm

An oocyte pauses twice in meiosis. A **primary oocyte** waits in meiosis I until the LH surge. It then finishes meiosis I, giving off the first polar body, and becomes a **secondary oocyte**, which pauses again in meiosis II. Meiosis II finishes, with the second polar body, **only when a sperm enters**. So the polar bodies are a record of how far each egg has gone.

First the timing. From day <InlineMath math="12" /> at 9:00 p.m. to day <InlineMath math="14" /> at 9:00 a.m. is one full day plus half a day, <InlineMath math="24+12 = 36" /> hours. Ovulation follows the LH surge after roughly <InlineMath math="36" /> to <InlineMath math="40" /> hours, so the eggs are collected **just before the follicles burst**.

Now sort the <InlineMath math="12" /> eggs on day <InlineMath math="15" />. The <InlineMath math="2" /> eggs without a polar body never finished meiosis I, so they are still primary oocytes. The <InlineMath math="10" /> with a first polar body are secondary oocytes. Of those, the <InlineMath math="8" /> with two pronuclei were entered by a sperm and gave off a second polar body, while the other <InlineMath math="2" /> are still paused in meiosis II.

\text{second polar bodies} &= 8\\
\text{total} &= 10+8 = 18
\end{aligned}" />

Next, the chromatids. A secondary oocyte has finished only meiosis I, which separates the pairs of chromosomes, not the sister chromatids. So each of its <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes **still has two chromatids**:

<BlockMath math="23\times2 = 46\text{ chromatids per oocyte}, \qquad 46\times10 = 460\text{ chromatids}" />

Last, the second cycle. Work out the proportion at each step of the first cycle, counting only the eggs that could take that step. Only the <InlineMath math="10" /> secondary oocytes, the eggs with a first polar body, were mature enough to be fertilised, and only the <InlineMath math="8" /> zygotes could go on dividing:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{mature eggs fertilised} &= \tfrac{8}{10} = 0.8\\
\text{zygotes reaching 8 cells} &= \tfrac{6}{8} = 0.75\\
\text{second cycle}: \quad 18\times0.8 &= 14.4\text{ zygotes}, \quad 14.4\times0.75 = 10.8\text{ embryos}
\end{aligned}" />

- I: hCG acts on the follicles like LH, and the eggs are taken <InlineMath math="36" /> hours later, before ovulation. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="10" /> first polar bodies and <InlineMath math="8" /> second ones make <InlineMath math="18" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: the <InlineMath math="10" /> secondary oocytes hold <InlineMath math="460" /> chromatids, not <InlineMath math="230" />. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: about <InlineMath math="10.8" /> embryos are expected, which is more than <InlineMath math="10" />. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II, III and IV counts **one chromatid per chromosome** in a secondary oocyte, <InlineMath math="23\times10 = 230" />, forgetting that meiosis II, which splits the sister chromatids, has not happened yet.
- I and IV only gives **all <InlineMath math="10" /> secondary oocytes a second polar body**, <InlineMath math="10\times2 = 20" />, as if meiosis II finished without a sperm.
- I and II only takes **all <InlineMath math="12" /> collected eggs as the base**, as if the <InlineMath math="2" /> primary oocytes could have been fertilised. Then <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6}{12}" /> of the <InlineMath math="20" /> eggs gives only <InlineMath math="10" /> embryos, which is not more than <InlineMath math="10" />.
