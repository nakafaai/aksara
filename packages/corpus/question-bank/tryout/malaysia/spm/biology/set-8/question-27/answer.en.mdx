# The water that fills the air spaces measures them, and the soaked piece shows what really floats

When the air is drawn out, water moves into exactly the space the air took up. So the extra mass of piece B is the mass of water that fills the **air spaces**, and since <InlineMath math="1\text{ g}" /> of water takes up <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}^3" />, it also gives their volume.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\text{Volume of air spaces} &= \frac{2.06-0.80\text{ g}}{1.00\text{ g cm}^{-3}} = 1.26\text{ cm}^3\\ \text{Share of the stalk} &= \tfrac{1.26}{2.0}\times100\% = 63\%\\ \text{Volume of cell material} &= 2.0-1.26 = 0.74\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}" />

Now compare three densities with water:

<BlockMath math="\text{Piece A: } \tfrac{0.80}{2.0}=0.40,\qquad \text{Piece B: } \tfrac{2.06}{2.0}=1.03,\qquad \text{Cell material: } \tfrac{0.80}{0.74}\approx1.08\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />

The cell material itself is **denser than water**. The stalk floats only because <InlineMath math="63\%" /> of it is air held in **aerenchyma**, a tissue of large air spaces. Fill those spaces with water and the piece sinks.

How much flooding can the stalk take? Piece A, <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}^3" />, floats as long as its mass stays at or below the mass of <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of water, <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" />. So the water that can enter is the **spare mass compared with the air volume**, not with the whole stalk:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\text{spare mass} &= 2.0-0.80 = 1.20\text{ g, which is }1.20\text{ cm}^3\text{ of water}\\ \text{share of the air spaces} &= \tfrac{1.20}{1.26}\times100\% \approx 95\%\end{aligned}" />

*Hydrilla* sp. uses its aerenchyma in more than one way:

- I: the soaked piece sinks at <InlineMath math="1.03\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, and the cell material is about <InlineMath math="1.08\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, so the cells are denser than water. Statement I is wrong.
- II: <InlineMath math="1.20" /> of the <InlineMath math="1.26\text{ cm}^3" /> of air spaces, about <InlineMath math="95\%" />, can fill with water before the piece reaches the density of water. Statement II is correct.
- III: <InlineMath math="1.26" /> of the <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ cm}^3" /> is air, which is <InlineMath math="63\%" />. Statement III is correct.
- IV: under water there is no air for stomata to open into, and a **thin cuticle lets gases and ions diffuse** straight into the leaf cells. Statement IV is correct.

So statements II, III and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II, III and IV reads the density of the untreated piece, <InlineMath math="0.40" />, as the density of its cells, **ignoring that the soaked piece sinks**.
- III and IV only divides the spare <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ cm}^3" /> by the whole stalk, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1.20}{2.0}=60\%" />, **instead of by the air spaces**.
- II and III only expects a submerged leaf to need stomata and a thick cuticle, **as if it lived in air**.
