# The water pumped out each minute is discharges times volume times two vacuoles, and at constant volume it equals the water coming in

A vacuole that discharges more often doesn't have to expel more water: in the sucrose solutions each discharge is also smaller. So the water expelled per minute is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{water expelled per minute} = 2\times(\text{discharges per minute})\times(\text{volume per discharge})" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{pond water}: \quad 2\times10\times1000 &= 20\,000\,\mu\text{m}^3\text{ per minute}\\
40\text{ mM sucrose}: \quad 2\times4\times750 &= 6000\,\mu\text{m}^3\text{ per minute}\\
80\text{ mM sucrose}: \quad 2\times1\times500 &= 1000\,\mu\text{m}^3\text{ per minute}
\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The cell's volume stays constant in every liquid. That only happens if the water entering by osmosis **equals** the water the vacuoles expel, so these three numbers are also the rates at which water enters.

- I: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{400\,000}{20\,000} = 20" /> minutes. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6000}{20\,000} = 30\%" />, so the output falls by <InlineMath math="70\%" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: with the vacuoles stopped, the cell keeps the water that enters. To burst it must gain half its volume:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{extra volume} &= 0.5\times400\,000 = 200\,000\,\mu\text{m}^3\\
\text{time in }40\text{ mM sucrose} &= \frac{200\,000}{6000} \approx 33\text{ minutes}
\end{aligned}" />

That is longer than <InlineMath math="20" /> minutes. Statement III is wrong.

- IV: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{20\,000}{1000} = 20" />. The more dilute pond water gives a steeper concentration gradient into the cytoplasm, so water enters <InlineMath math="20" /> times as fast. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only compares only the discharges in IV, <InlineMath math="10" /> against <InlineMath math="1" />, and gets <InlineMath math="10" /> times. **Each discharge in <InlineMath math="80\text{ mM}" /> sucrose is also only half as big**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts III by using the pond water rate, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{200\,000}{20\,000} = 10" /> minutes, **when the cell sits in <InlineMath math="40\text{ mM}" /> sucrose**, where water enters at only <InlineMath math="6000\,\mu\text{m}^3" /> per minute.
- I and IV only compares only the discharges in II, <InlineMath math="10" /> against <InlineMath math="4" />, a fall of <InlineMath math="60\%" />, **forgetting that each discharge also shrinks from <InlineMath math="1000" /> to <InlineMath math="750\,\mu\text{m}^3" />**.
