# Keep the volume fixed and let the length absorb the squeeze

The fluid cannot be compressed, so <InlineMath math="\pi r^2 L" /> stays the same. Narrowing the segment must lengthen it by the **square** of the factor by which the radius shrinks.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\pi\times3^2\times10=\pi\times2^2\times L\ \Rightarrow\ L=10\times\frac{9}{4}=22.5\text{ mm}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{half the radius}: L\times2^2=4L" />
</MathContainer>

- I: the length grows by <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^2=2.25" />, from <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="22.5\text{ mm}" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: when circular muscles contract, the fluid pushes the segment longer and stretches the longitudinal muscles. When those contract, the segment shortens and widens, stretching the circular muscles. They work as an antagonistic pair through the fluid. Statement II is correct.
- III: length depends on the square of the radius, so half the radius gives **four** times the length. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: chaetae grip the soil in the short, wide segments, giving the long, thin segments something to push against as they extend forwards. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, **the anchoring that turns stretching into forward movement**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and **scales length with the radius instead of its square**.
- I, III and IV only accepts III and rejects II, **although the fluid links the two muscle layers**.
