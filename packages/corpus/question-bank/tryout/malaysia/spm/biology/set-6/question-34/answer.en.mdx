# Separate what the genes allow from what the temperature permits

Every cell of the cat has the same genes, including this heat-sensitive enzyme. Where the skin is cool, the enzyme works and makes dark pigment. Where it is warm, it does not. The **phenotype** follows temperature, but the **genotype** is unchanged.

- I: the ears, at about <InlineMath math="28\,^\circ\text{C}" />, and the iced back, at <InlineMath math="25\,^\circ\text{C}" />, both grow dark fur, while warmer skin grows pale fur. Statement I is correct.
- II: one genotype gives dark fur on cool skin and pale fur on warm skin, so the environment helps decide the phenotype. Statement II is correct.
- III: the ice pack changed only the skin cells of the back, not the genes in the cat's eggs. Its kittens inherit the usual pattern. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: above <InlineMath math="33\,^\circ\text{C}" /> this form of the enzyme loses the shape of its active site and cannot make pigment, so warm parts stay pale. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, **the enzyme effect that explains the whole pattern**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I and accepts III, **as if a change in skin cells were inherited**.
- I, III and IV only accepts III and rejects II, **although one genotype gives two colours**.
