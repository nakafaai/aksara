# Count woman-years, then scale to a hundred

A study of many women for a few years and a study of fewer women for longer can only be compared **per unit of use**. Multiply women by years, then find the pregnancies per <InlineMath math="100" /> of those woman-years.

<BlockMath math="X: \frac{6}{1200\times1}\times100=0.5,\qquad Y: \frac{24}{400\times2}\times100=3,\qquad Z: \frac{90}{500\times2}\times100=9" />

- I: <InlineMath math="6" /> pregnancies in <InlineMath math="1200" /> woman-years is <InlineMath math="0.5" /> per <InlineMath math="100" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: dividing <InlineMath math="24" /> by <InlineMath math="400" /> women alone gives <InlineMath math="6" />, but each woman used it for <InlineMath math="2" /> years, so the <InlineMath math="24" /> pregnancies came from <InlineMath math="800" /> woman-years, giving <InlineMath math="3" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: <InlineMath math="9" /> against <InlineMath math="3" /> per <InlineMath math="100" /> woman-years is three times. Statement III is correct.
- IV: the length of a study does not make a method more reliable. Per woman-year, Y fails <InlineMath math="3" /> times against X's <InlineMath math="0.5" />, six times as often. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, often after **dividing Z by women alone**, which gives <InlineMath math="18" />.
- II and IV only rejects I and III and **ranks a method by the length of its study**.
- I, III and IV only rejects II, **the woman-years step that the whole comparison rests on**.
