# Use the uniform groups to measure the environment, then subtract it

Pure lines are homozygous, and a cross between two pure lines gives a first generation in which every plant has the **same heterozygous genotype**. So any spread in these groups can only come from the environment. The second generation has the same environment but mixed genes.

<BlockMath math="\text{environment}=4,\qquad \text{genes}=20-4=16,\qquad \frac{16}{20}=80\%" />

- I: P1, P2 and the first generation are each genetically uniform, and all show the same variance of <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}^2" />. That spread comes from the field: soil, water and light. Statement I is correct.
- II: the second generation shares the same field, so <InlineMath math="4" /> of its <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^2" /> comes from the environment and the other <InlineMath math="16" /> from genes, <InlineMath math="80\%" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: height is controlled by several genes and spreads smoothly around a mean, which is **continuous variation**. Different parents do not make it discontinuous. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the first-generation plants are all alike because they are all **heterozygous** in the same way, not because they are homozygous. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only calls the variation discontinuous, **although several genes give a smooth spread of heights**.
- I and IV only rejects the <InlineMath math="80\%" /> that follows from subtracting the environment and makes the first generation homozygous.
- II and IV only rejects I, **although uniform genes leave only the environment to cause the spread**.
