# Work out the index, then ask what it measures beyond the number of species

Both habitats hold four species, so counting species cannot tell them apart. Simpson's index also weighs **how evenly** the individuals are spread among the species.

In habitat Y one species makes up <InlineMath math="17" /> of the <InlineMath math="20" /> animals, so two animals picked at random are very likely to be the same species. That is low diversity, even with four species present.

So X is about <InlineMath math="0.79" /> and Y about <InlineMath math="0.28" />, and X is more diverse even though both have four species.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Equally diverse looks only at the **number of species** and ignores how evenly the animals are spread.
- <InlineMath math="0.21" /> and <InlineMath math="0.72" /> stops at the fraction and **forgets to subtract it from one**, which reverses the conclusion.
- Y more diverse reads one thriving species as variety, **but dominance by one species lowers diversity**.
