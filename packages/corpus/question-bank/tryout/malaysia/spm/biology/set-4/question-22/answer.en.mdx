# Link potassium to water and water to width, then use the straight-line rule

A guard cell opens the stoma by **taking in water**, and water follows potassium by osmosis. So the potassium level sets the width, and anything that removes potassium closes the stoma.

- I: potassium climbs from <InlineMath math="100" /> to <InlineMath math="400\text{ mM}" /> after sunrise, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become **turgid** and the width rises to <InlineMath math="10\,\mu\text{m}" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: between <InlineMath math="150\text{ mM}" /> with <InlineMath math="3\,\mu\text{m}" /> and <InlineMath math="300\text{ mM}" /> with <InlineMath math="8\,\mu\text{m}" />, the value <InlineMath math="250\text{ mM}" /> lies two thirds of the way, giving about <InlineMath math="6\,\mu\text{m}" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: the second leaf has light but no potassium uptake, and its stomata stay at <InlineMath math="2\,\mu\text{m}" />. Light alone is not enough. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: in a drought, abscisic acid makes guard cells lose potassium. Water follows it out, the cells become flaccid, and the stomata close to save water, even in bright light. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, missing that a plant can **override the light** to save water in a drought.
- I, II and III only ignores the second leaf, **whose stomata stay shut in full light without potassium uptake**.
- II, III and IV only rejects the potassium and osmosis mechanism that the whole day's data follow.
