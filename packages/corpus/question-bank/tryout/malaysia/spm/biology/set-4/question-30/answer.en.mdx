# Remove one species and see where the other spreads

If a species spreads into new ground once a neighbour is removed, the neighbour was **keeping it out**. If nothing changes, something else sets the limit, such as drying out at high levels.

- I: with Y present, X is absent below <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m}" />. With Y removed, X covers <InlineMath math="50" /> to <InlineMath math="60\%" /> there. Y was crowding X out of the lower shore. Statement I is correct.
- II: Y stops at <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ m}" /> whether or not X is removed. X is not what keeps Y off the upper shore, so a physical factor such as drying out must. Statement II is correct.
- III: X can grow from <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ m}" /> to <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ m}" />, but with Y present it is found only from <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ m}" /> up. Its possible range is wider than its actual range. Statement III is correct.
- IV: removing Y lets X spread down the shore, which shows that the two **do compete** for space. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III only rejects II, **although Y's upper limit does not move when X is removed**.
- II, III and IV only reads the removal as proof of no competition, **when X spreads only once Y is gone**.
- I, II and IV only rejects III, missing that X **grows lower down** once released.
