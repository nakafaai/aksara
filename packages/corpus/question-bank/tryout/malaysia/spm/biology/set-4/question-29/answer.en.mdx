# Remove one group of decomposers at a time and see how much slower decay gets

Each finer mesh **shuts out one more group** of organisms. The difference between two bags is the share of decay done by the group that only one bag lets in.

<BlockMath math="\text{fine mesh, microorganisms}: 100-66=34\%,\qquad \text{bacteria alone}: 100-91=9\%,\qquad \text{fungi}: 34-9=25\%" />

- I: with earthworms and insects, in the coarse bags, only <InlineMath math="10\%" /> is left, against <InlineMath math="30\%" /> when they are kept out. They tear the leaves into small pieces, giving microorganisms more surface. Statement I is correct.
- II: fine mesh lets in only fungi and bacteria, and <InlineMath math="100-66=34\%" /> of the mass is gone. Statement II is correct.
- III: with the fungi killed only <InlineMath math="9\%" /> is lost, so fungi account for <InlineMath math="25" /> of the <InlineMath math="34" /> percentage points, about three quarters. Statement III is correct.
- IV: even in the coarse bags, the fragments left by animals are finally broken down by fungi and bacteria, which release the **nutrients** back into the soil. Earthworms do not replace them. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, missing that killing the fungi cuts microbial decay from <InlineMath math="34\%" /> to **only** <InlineMath math="9\%" />.
- I and III only rejects II, **although** <InlineMath math="100-66" /> is exactly <InlineMath math="34" />.
- II, III and IV only rejects the clear effect of large animals and makes microorganisms unnecessary.
