# Find the intervals that stretched, because that is where the growing cells are

An interval lengthens only where cells are **dividing and elongating**. Every interval started at <InlineMath math="2\text{ mm}" />, so the stretched intervals mark the growth zone.

<BlockMath math="\text{growth of the grass leaf}=(6-2)+(3-2)=4+1=5\text{ mm}" />

- I: in the grass leaf only the intervals next to the base stretched, to <InlineMath math="6" /> and <InlineMath math="3\text{ mm}" />. The dividing cells sit at the base, at an intercalary meristem. Statement I is correct.
- II: the stretched intervals added <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" />, so the leaf grew <InlineMath math="5\text{ mm}" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: in the bean shoot the same stretching happens in the intervals next to the tip, the place of the **apical meristem**. Statement III is correct.
- IV: the top of the grass leaf did not grow at all, so cutting it off leaves the growing base untouched. That is why grass keeps growing after grazing or mowing. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, **although the bean intervals stretch next to the tip**.
- I and III only rejects II, missing that the gains of <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" /> add up to **five millimetres**.
- II, III and IV only treats the grass leaf like the bean shoot, **but its growing zone is at the base**.
