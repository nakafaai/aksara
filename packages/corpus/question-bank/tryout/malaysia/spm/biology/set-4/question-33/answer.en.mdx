# Compare the ratio and the litter size with what a normal cross predicts

Two yellow parents that both produce grey pups must both be <InlineMath math="Yy" />. A normal <InlineMath math="Yy\times Yy" /> cross gives a **<InlineMath math="3:1" /> ratio**, but here it is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{160}{80}=2:1" />, and the litters are smaller.

If the <InlineMath math="YY" /> quarter dies before birth, the survivors are <InlineMath math="2" /> yellow <InlineMath math="(Yy)" /> to <InlineMath math="1" /> grey <InlineMath math="(yy)" />, which matches <InlineMath math="160:80" />. Losing a quarter of each litter also matches the size: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3}{4}\times8=6" /> pups, exactly the average for two yellow parents. A cross between a yellow and a grey parent never makes <InlineMath math="YY" />, so its litters stay at <InlineMath math="8" />.

So embryos with two yellow alleles die before birth, and every yellow mouse born is heterozygous.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Yellow recessive contradicts the data, **because two recessive parents could never produce grey pups**.
- Death after birth cannot explain the result, **because the pups were counted at birth** and the litters are already smaller.
- Homozygous yellow parents would give only yellow pups, **which would raise the share of yellow pups instead of lowering it**.
