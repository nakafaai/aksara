# Follow each tendon to see which way it pulls the wing

A muscle moves a bone in the direction its **tendon pulls**, not in the direction of the muscle's position. So the key is where each tendon attaches to the humerus.

- I: the pectoralis is <InlineMath math="20\%" /> of the body mass, five times the supracoracoideus, and it is <InlineMath math="90\%" /> active in the downstroke. The powerful downstroke that lifts the bird uses the bigger muscle. Statement I is correct.
- II: the pectoralis is active in the downstroke and almost silent in the upstroke, and the supracoracoideus does the opposite. One contracts while the other relaxes, an **antagonistic pair**. Statement II is correct.
- III: the supracoracoideus sits on the chest, but its tendon runs up over the shoulder like a rope over a pulley and attaches to the top of the humerus. When the muscle contracts, it pulls the wing up. Statement III is correct.
- IV: both muscles attach to the keel. A large keel gives a large surface for these big muscles to anchor to. Statement IV is correct.

So all four statements, I, II, III and IV, are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and IV only rejects III, assuming a muscle below the wing can only pull it down, **but the tendon pulls from above the shoulder**.
- I and IV only also rejects II, **although the activity data show the muscles taking turns**.
- II, III and IV only rejects I, **although the bigger muscle drives the downstroke**.
