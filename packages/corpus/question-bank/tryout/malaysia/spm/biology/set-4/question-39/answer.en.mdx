# Count the cuts on a circle and the doublings in a culture

A restriction enzyme cuts wherever its **recognition sequence** appears. On a circular plasmid every cut is between two pieces, so the number of fragments equals the number of cuts. After that, the bacteria copy the gene each time they divide.

<BlockMath math="\text{expected sites}=\frac{12\,288}{4096}=3,\qquad \text{divisions in }10\text{ hours}=\frac{600}{20}=30,\qquad 2^{30}=1\,073\,741\,824" />

- I: one restriction enzyme cuts both DNAs at the same sequence and leaves **sticky ends** that pair. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="12\,288" /> bases hold about <InlineMath math="3" /> sites. A circle cut in <InlineMath math="3" /> places falls into <InlineMath math="3" /> fragments, not <InlineMath math="4" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: <InlineMath math="30" /> doublings turn one bacterium into <InlineMath math="2^{30}" />, just over a thousand million cells, each carrying the gene. Statement III is correct.
- IV: the bacteria keep their own DNA and read the one added gene, because all living things share the same genetic code. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I, II and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects III, often after multiplying <InlineMath math="30\times2" /> **instead of raising** <InlineMath math="2" /> **to the power** <InlineMath math="30" />.
- II and III only rejects I, missing that **matching sticky ends** need the same restriction enzyme.
- I, II and IV only claims the bacteria become human, **although only one gene was added**.
