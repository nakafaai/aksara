# Multiply haemoglobin by saturation to get the oxygen actually carried

The oxygen in blood depends on **how much haemoglobin** there is and **how full** it is with oxygen. Neither number alone tells the whole story.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{oxygen}=1.34\times\text{haemoglobin}\times\text{saturation}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{sea level}: 1.34\times14.0\times0.98\approx18.4,\quad \text{week }0: 1.34\times14.0\times0.80\approx15.0,\quad \text{week }8: 1.34\times18.3\times0.88\approx21.6\ \text{cm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

- I: thin air at altitude leaves the blood less saturated. The kidneys respond by releasing a hormone that makes the bone marrow produce more red blood cells, which takes weeks. Haemoglobin climbs from <InlineMath math="14.0" /> to <InlineMath math="18.3\text{ g}" />. Statement I is correct.
- II: <InlineMath math="21.6\text{ cm}^3" /> at week <InlineMath math="8" /> is more than the <InlineMath math="18.4\text{ cm}^3" /> at sea level. Extra haemoglobin more than makes up for the lower saturation. Statement II is correct.
- III: the haemoglobin is unchanged on arrival, but its saturation drops from <InlineMath math="98\%" /> to <InlineMath math="80\%" />, so the blood carries only about <InlineMath math="15.0\text{ cm}^3" />. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the air at the camp has the same thin oxygen throughout. Saturation improves because the climber breathes more deeply and faster, not because the air changes. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only looks at haemoglobin alone on arrival, **forgetting that saturation fell to** <InlineMath math="80\%" />.
- II and III only rejects I and keeps the same slip about arrival.
- I and IV only rejects the calculation for week <InlineMath math="8" />, **and the air at a fixed height does not gain oxygen**.
