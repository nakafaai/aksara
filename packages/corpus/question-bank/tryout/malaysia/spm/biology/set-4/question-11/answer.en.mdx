# Separate what the antibody test detects from what the count shows

The standard test detects **antibodies**, not the virus itself, so anything that brings antibodies into a body can make it test positive. The helper T cell count shows how much of the immune system's coordination is left.

- I: antibodies from the mother cross the placenta and stay in the baby for months. A baby who is not infected can still carry her antibodies and test positive. Statement I is correct.
- II: at a steady <InlineMath math="81" /> cells per year from <InlineMath math="800" /> at year <InlineMath math="1" />, the count reaches <InlineMath math="200" /> at about year <InlineMath math="8.4" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: her own antibodies are what make her test positive, so her B lymphocytes are still making them, though the response weakens as **helper T cells** are lost. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the count falls again for years after the recovery, so the virus was never removed. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only claims antibodies stop altogether, **although her test depends on them**.
- I, II and IV only reads the partial recovery as a cure, **but the count falls steadily afterwards**.
- II and IV only rejects the **passive transfer of antibodies** across the placenta.
