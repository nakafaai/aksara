# Turn each group's cases into a rate per sperm, then test it against divisions and a new age mix

Every new mutation here came from the father, and each baby comes from exactly one sperm. So in each age group **the share of babies with a new mutation is the share of fertilising sperm that carried one**:

<BlockMath math="\text{under }30:\ \frac{40}{2\,000\,000}=\frac{2}{100\,000},\qquad 30\text{ to }39:\ \frac{60}{1\,500\,000}=\frac{4}{100\,000},\qquad 40\text{ and over}:\ \frac{60}{500\,000}=\frac{12}{100\,000}" />

- I: compare the rates, not the case counts: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{12}{2}=6" />. The counts, <InlineMath math="60" /> against <InlineMath math="40" />, look like only <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times, but the older group has a quarter as many births. Statement I is correct.
- II: count the divisions behind a sperm at each mean age:

<BlockMath math="\text{age }25:\ 30+23\times10=260,\qquad \text{age }45:\ 30+23\times30=720,\qquad \frac{720}{260}\approx2.8" />

With the same chance per division, the older fathers would mutate only about <InlineMath math="2.8" /> times as often, not <InlineMath math="6" /> times. The chance per division must itself be about <InlineMath math="\tfrac{6}{2.8}\approx2.2" /> times higher in the older men. Statement II is wrong.

- III: apply each group's rate to its share of the new births:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1\,600\,000\times\tfrac{2}{100\,000} &= 32 \\
1\,600\,000\times\tfrac{4}{100\,000} &= 64 \\
800\,000\times\tfrac{12}{100\,000} &= 96
\end{aligned}\qquad 32+64+96=192" />

The table had the same <InlineMath math="4\,000\,000" /> births with <InlineMath math="40+60+60=160" /> cases, and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{192}{160}=1.2" />, so <InlineMath math="20\%" /> more. Only the shift towards older fathers raises the count. Statement III is correct.

- IV: two affected parents are both <InlineMath math="Aa" />, since <InlineMath math="AA" /> doesn't survive. Their children are <InlineMath math="AA" />, <InlineMath math="Aa" /> and <InlineMath math="aa" /> in the ratio <InlineMath math="1:2:1" />, but the <InlineMath math="AA" /> child dies, so among survivors <InlineMath math="P(\text{achondroplasia})=\tfrac{2}{2+1}=\tfrac{2}{3}" />. Statement IV is wrong.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I only averages the three rates, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2+4+12}{3}=6" /> per <InlineMath math="100\,000" />, and predicts <InlineMath math="240" /> cases. **Each rate must be weighted by its own share of the births**, which gives <InlineMath math="192" />.
- I, II and III only lets the extra divisions explain the whole rise. **The divisions grow only about <InlineMath math="2.8" /> times from age <InlineMath math="25" /> to <InlineMath math="45" />**, while the rate grows <InlineMath math="6" /> times.
- I, III and IV only uses the <InlineMath math="3:1" /> ratio of a normal dominant cross. **The <InlineMath math="AA" /> children die**, so among survivors the share with achondroplasia is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{3}" />.
