# A wider trunk gains more area from a thinner ring of new growth

The rubber tree thickens because its vascular cambium adds new xylem and phloem in a ring each year, while the oil palm, a monocot, has no vascular cambium and only grows taller from its apical meristem. **Area grows with the square of the girth**, so the same or even a thinner ring adds more area on a wider trunk.

## Areas and radii of the rubber trunk

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{area} &: \ \tfrac{25^2}{12.56}\approx49.8,\quad \tfrac{38^2}{12.56}\approx115.0,\quad \tfrac{50^2}{12.56}\approx199.0,\quad \tfrac{61^2}{12.56}\approx296.3\text{ cm}^2 \\
\text{radius} &: \ \tfrac{25}{6.28}\approx3.98,\quad \tfrac{38}{6.28}\approx6.05,\quad \tfrac{50}{6.28}\approx7.96,\quad \tfrac{61}{6.28}\approx9.71\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}" />

## Comparing the gains over two years

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{area gained} &: \ 4\text{ to }6=65.2,\qquad 8\text{ to }10=97.3\text{ cm}^2,\qquad \tfrac{97.3}{65.2}\approx1.5 \\
\text{radius gained} &: \ 4\text{ to }6=\tfrac{13}{6.28}\approx2.07,\qquad 8\text{ to }10=\tfrac{11}{6.28}\approx1.75\text{ cm} \\

## Xylem cells added each year

The bark keeps its thickness, so **all of the radius gained is new xylem**. Turn the radius gained into micrometres, share it over the two years, then divide by the width of one cell.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{radius gained, years }8\text{ to }10 &: \ \tfrac{11}{6.28}\approx1.7516\text{ cm}=17\,516\ \mu\text{m} \\
\text{per year} &: \ \tfrac{17\,516}{2}=8758\ \mu\text{m} \\
\text{xylem cells per year} &: \ \tfrac{8758}{40}\approx219
\end{aligned}" />

## Judging each statement

- I: the girth grows by <InlineMath math="11" /> against <InlineMath math="13\text{ cm}" />, but the area grows by <InlineMath math="97.3" /> against <InlineMath math="65.2\text{ cm}^2" />, about <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times as much. Statement I is correct.
- II: the oil palm's girth never changes, so only the rubber tree thickens by a vascular cambium. Its area goes from about <InlineMath math="49.8" /> to <InlineMath math="296.3\text{ cm}^2" />, about six times. Statement II is correct.
- III: **the radius grows by <InlineMath math="1.75\text{ cm}" />, less than the <InlineMath math="2.07\text{ cm}" /> of the first interval**, not <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times as much. The extra area comes from the wider trunk, not from a thicker ring. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: the radius grows by <InlineMath math="1.7516\text{ cm}" /> in two years, <InlineMath math="8758\ \mu\text{m}" /> a year, which is <InlineMath math="219" /> cells of <InlineMath math="40\ \mu\text{m}" />. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II, III and IV takes the area ratio of about <InlineMath math="1.5" /> as the ratio of ring thickness too, **forgetting that the later ring wraps a wider trunk**.
- I and II only uses the radius gained over the two years as a yearly gain, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{17\,516}{40}\approx438" /> cells, **forgetting to share it over the two years**.
- II and IV only squares the girth gains, <InlineMath math="13^2=169" /> against <InlineMath math="11^2=121" />, and so finds less area added later. **The area added is the difference of the squared girths**, <InlineMath math="61^2-50^2" /> against <InlineMath math="38^2-25^2" />.
