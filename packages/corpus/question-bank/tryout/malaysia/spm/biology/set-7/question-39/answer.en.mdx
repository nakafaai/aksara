# Build the match chance locus by locus, turn it into a count of people, then a count into a chance for Y

A person's DNA is the same in every cell, so the true source of the stain must match it at **every locus**. A single mismatch rules a person out. A full match only puts a person on the list of people who could have left it, so the real question is how long that list is.

Compare each suspect with the stain, locus by locus. Z differs at all four loci. X matches loci <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="4" /> but has <InlineMath math="21" /> instead of <InlineMath math="20" /> at locus <InlineMath math="3" />. Y matches at all four.

Now find how common the stain's pair is at each locus. At locus <InlineMath math="2" /> the stain has two copies of allele <InlineMath math="8" />, so use <InlineMath math="p\times p" />. At the other three loci it has two different alleles, so use <InlineMath math="2\times p\times q" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{locus }1 &: \quad 2\times0.10\times0.25 = 0.05 \\
\text{locus }2 &: \quad 0.20\times0.20 = 0.04 \\
\text{locus }3 &: \quad 2\times0.50\times0.20 = 0.20 \\
\text{locus }4 &: \quad 2\times0.10\times0.10 = 0.02
\end{aligned}" />

The four loci are on different chromosomes, so they are inherited independently, and the fractions multiply. Then carry that chance into the rules for the count and for Y:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P(\text{match at all four}) &= 0.05\times0.04\times0.20\times0.02 = 0.000008 = \tfrac{1}{125\,000} \\
\text{other people matching} &= 1\,000\,000\times\tfrac{1}{125\,000} = 8 \\
P(\text{Y left the stain}) &= \tfrac{1}{1+8} = \tfrac{1}{9}\approx11\%
\end{aligned}" />

- I: Y is the only suspect who matches at all four loci. X fails at locus <InlineMath math="3" />, and since the source must match everywhere, **one mismatch excludes** X. Statement I is correct.
- II: about <InlineMath math="8" /> unrelated people besides the source share the profile. Statement II is correct.
- III: the chance that one person matches is still <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{125\,000}" />, but the town has only a tenth of the people, so far fewer others are expected to match:

<BlockMath math="100\,000\times\tfrac{1}{125\,000}=0.8,\qquad P(\text{Y left the stain})=\tfrac{1}{1+0.8}=\tfrac{5}{9}\approx56\%" />

That is about <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{9}" />, five times <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{9}" />. Statement III is wrong.

- IV: with DNA as the only evidence, Y is one of about <InlineMath math="9" /> people in the city who match, so the chance is about <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{9}" />. The tiny <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{125\,000}" /> is the chance that **one random person** matches, not the chance that a matching person is innocent. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and II only rejects IV, reading <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{125\,000}" /> as the chance that Y is innocent, **although about** <InlineMath math="8" /> **other people in the city match too**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts III, keeping <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{9}" /> because the chance that one person matches has not changed, **but the number of other people who match falls with the number of people**, to about <InlineMath math="0.8" /> in the town, so Y's chance rises to <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{9}" />.
- II and IV only rejects I, treating X's match at three loci as close enough, **although the source must match at every locus**.
