# The bacteria keep dividing while the phages are being built

A clear patch counts **one infected bacterium or one free phage**, never both. So the count only jumps when infected bacteria burst and let out their new phages.

Start with the number of phages each bacterium releases. Every phage infected a different bacterium, so <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^5" /> bacteria were infected. When they have all burst, the count levels off at <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^7" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{2.0\times10^7}{2.0\times10^5}=100\text{ phages per bacterium}" />

Next, find the share of bacteria infected in the second round. While the first phages were being built, the uninfected bacteria went on dividing. In <InlineMath math="40" /> minutes they divide <InlineMath math="\tfrac{40}{20}=2" /> times:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{uninfected at 40 min} &\approx 1.0\times10^8\times2^2 = 4.0\times10^8 \\
\text{share infected} &= \frac{2.0\times10^7}{4.0\times10^8} = 0.05 = 5\%
\end{aligned}" />

Then compare the phages and the bacteria after the second burst. The <InlineMath math="4.0\times10^8-2.0\times10^7=3.8\times10^8" /> bacteria left uninfected divide twice more by <InlineMath math="80" /> minutes, while each newly infected bacterium releases <InlineMath math="100" /> phages:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\text{uninfected at 80 min} &= 3.8\times10^8\times2^2 \approx 1.5\times10^9 \\
\text{phages released} &= 2.0\times10^7\times100 = 2.0\times10^9
\end{aligned}" />

- I: the burst size is <InlineMath math="100" />. The <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^8" /> bacteria include the many that were **never infected**, so they are not the right divisor. Statement I is correct.
- II: the first-round phages meet the bacteria **after two divisions**, so <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^7" /> of <InlineMath math="4.0\times10^8" /> are infected, which is <InlineMath math="5\%" />. Statement II is correct.
- III: a virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no metabolism of its own, so it can't divide. New phages are **assembled from parts made by the host** inside the flat stretch of the curve. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^9" /> phages against about <InlineMath math="1.5\times10^9" /> bacteria, so the phages outnumber them. Statement IV is correct.

So statements I, II and IV are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and IV only rejects II by dividing by the <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^8" /> bacteria at the start, which gives <InlineMath math="20\%" />, **forgetting that they doubled twice in** <InlineMath math="40" /> **minutes**.
- I, II, III and IV accepts III, giving the phage a **cell's way of reproducing**, although a virus can't divide.
- II and IV only rejects I by dividing <InlineMath math="2.0\times10^7" /> by all <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^8" /> bacteria, **most of which were never infected**, and gets <InlineMath math="0.2" />.
