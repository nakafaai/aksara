# Link each drink to the osmotic pressure of the blood, then to ADH

ADH responds to the **osmotic pressure** of the blood. More ADH makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed and less urine is made. Less ADH does the opposite.

- Distilled water dilutes the blood, so its osmotic pressure falls. The pituitary releases less ADH, the collecting ducts become **less** permeable, and the urine volume jumps from <InlineMath math="50" /> to <InlineMath math="350\text{ cm}^3" /> per half hour. Statement I is correct, and statement III has the permeability the wrong way round, so it is wrong.
- The salt solution has the same osmotic pressure as blood, so the osmotic pressure hardly changes, ADH stays about the same, and the urine volume only creeps from <InlineMath math="50" /> to <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" />. Statement II is correct.
- The table shows no large volume of urine after the salt solution, and with ADH unchanged the urine is not dilute. Statement IV is wrong.

So only I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I and III accepts III, but less ADH makes the collecting ducts **less** permeable to water, which is why so much urine forms.
- II, III and IV rejects I, the only explanation of the large rise after distilled water, and accepts two wrong statements.
- I, II and IV accepts IV, which the small, steady urine volumes after the salt solution contradict.
