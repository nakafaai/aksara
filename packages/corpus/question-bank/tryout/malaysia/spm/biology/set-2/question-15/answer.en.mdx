# Work out both rates for every interval

Every interval is two years long, so the absolute rate is the gain divided by <InlineMath math="2" />. The relative rate divides that by the **starting** height.

| Interval | Gain | Absolute rate | Relative rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> years | <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ cm per year}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{7.5}{85}\approx0.088" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="6" /> years | <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ cm per year}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{7.5}{100}=0.075" /> |
| <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="14" /> years | <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm per year}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{9}{150}=0.060" /> |
| <InlineMath math="14" /> to <InlineMath math="16" /> years | <InlineMath math="7\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="3.5\text{ cm per year}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{3.5}{168}\approx0.021" /> |

The biggest gain in centimetres is during the adolescent growth spurt, <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="14" /> years. But a small child grows a much **larger share of its own height**, so the relative rate is highest from <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> years. So the absolute growth rate is highest from <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="14" /> years and the relative growth rate is highest from <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> years.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="14" /> years for both assumes the biggest gain is also the biggest share, which ignores that the boy is already <InlineMath math="150\text{ cm}" /> tall.
- The third option swaps the two rates.
- <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="6" /> years has the same gain as <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> years but starts from a taller height, **so its relative rate is lower**.
