# Find the immune share first, then the vaccinated share

An infected person can pass the disease only to people who are **not immune**. If a fraction <InlineMath math="p" /> of the population is immune, each case causes <InlineMath math="5(1-p)" /> new cases on average.

<BlockMath math="5(1-p)<1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-p<\tfrac{1}{5}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p>0.8" />

So at least <InlineMath math="80\%" /> of the population must be immune. The vaccine makes only <InlineMath math="90\%" /> of those vaccinated immune, so the vaccinated fraction <InlineMath math="v" /> must satisfy:

<BlockMath math="0.9\,v\ge0.8\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v\ge\frac{0.8}{0.9}\approx0.889" />

The chart shows the average number of new cases falling as more people are vaccinated. It drops below one only when coverage passes about <InlineMath math="89\%" />.

So at least <InlineMath math="89\%" /> of the population must be vaccinated.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="80\%" /> is the share that must be immune, **but one vaccinated person in ten stays unprotected**.
- <InlineMath math="72\%" /> multiplies <InlineMath math="0.8\times0.9" /> instead of dividing, which **lowers the coverage** when an imperfect vaccine needs more of it.
- <InlineMath math="20\%" /> is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{5}" />, the share that may stay non-immune, **not the share that must be vaccinated**.
