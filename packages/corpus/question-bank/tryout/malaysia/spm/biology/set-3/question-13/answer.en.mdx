# Read each person's glucose and insulin together

High blood glucose can come from **too little insulin** or from cells that **respond poorly** to insulin. Only the insulin curve tells the two apart.

- I: Q's insulin stays between <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> units while P's jumps to <InlineMath math="45" />. Q's beta cells secrete very little insulin. Statement I is correct.
- II: R's insulin rises to <InlineMath math="120" /> units, far above P's, yet R's glucose stays high. R has plenty of insulin, so the cells must be responding poorly to it. Statement II is wrong.
- III: at minute <InlineMath math="60" />, Q is at <InlineMath math="14.8" /> and R at <InlineMath math="12.8\text{ mmol/L}" />, both above <InlineMath math="10\text{ mmol/L}" />, so both pass glucose in the urine. Statement III is correct.
- IV: glucagon raises blood glucose. P's glucose falls while P's insulin is high, so the fall comes from **insulin**. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and III are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only treats every high glucose curve as a lack of insulin, **although R's insulin is the highest of the three**.
- I, III and IV only credits glucagon with lowering P's glucose, **but glucagon raises it**.
- III and IV only rejects I, missing that **Q's insulin barely changes** after the drink.
