# Count the height alleles, not the genotypes

Each <InlineMath math="A" /> or <InlineMath math="B" /> adds the same <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />, so height depends only on **how many** of these alleles a plant has. A plant <InlineMath math="110\text{ cm}" /> tall has exactly two, which three different genotypes provide.

Counting height alleles across all <InlineMath math="16" /> combinations gives <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> offspring with <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> alleles. The middle class, two alleles, is the most common, which is why additive genes give a spread of heights centred on the average. The answer is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3}{8}" />.

# Why the other options are wrong

- <InlineMath math="\tfrac{9}{16}" /> counts plants with at least one <InlineMath math="A" /> and one <InlineMath math="B" />, the **dominant classes of an ordinary dihybrid cross**, which here range from <InlineMath math="110" /> to <InlineMath math="120\text{ cm}" />.
- <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{4}" /> counts only <InlineMath math="AaBb" /> and **misses** <InlineMath math="AAbb" /> **and** <InlineMath math="aaBB" />, which are just as tall.
- <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{16}" /> counts a single genotype such as <InlineMath math="AAbb" /> and **misses the other two** with two height alleles.
