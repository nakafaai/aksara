# Check each patient against what the feedback loop should do

In a healthy loop, **high thyroxine pushes TSH down** and low thyroxine lets it rise. A result that breaks this rule shows where the fault lies, and the size of the gland shows whether TSH has been driving it.

| Patient | Thyroxine | TSH | Fits the loop? | Most likely cause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P | low | high | yes | thyroid cannot make thyroxine, such as a lack of iodine |
| Q | low | low | no | pituitary gland makes too little TSH |
| R | high | low | yes | extra thyroxine from outside, gland not stimulated |
| S | high | low | yes | thyroid gland overactive on its own |
| T | high | high | no | pituitary keeps secreting TSH despite high thyroxine |

- T: thyroxine is high, so TSH should be pushed down, yet it is <InlineMath math="12" />. The pituitary ignores the feedback and keeps stimulating the thyroid, which swells. This fits a **TSH-secreting tumour**.
- R: thyroxine is high and TSH is suppressed, but the gland is **small**. With little TSH, the gland is neither stimulated nor making the thyroxine, so the thyroxine must come from tablets.
- S has the same blood results as R, but its gland is swollen, so its own gland is overactive.

So patient T most likely has the pituitary tumour, and patient R most likely takes too many thyroxine tablets.

# Why the other options are wrong

- P and S reads P's high TSH as a pituitary fault, **but high TSH with low thyroxine is the loop working normally**, and S's swollen gland makes its own thyroxine.
- T and S picks the swollen gland for the tablets, **but a gland that is not stimulated by TSH does not swell**.
- P and R mistakes the normal response of P's pituitary for a tumour.
