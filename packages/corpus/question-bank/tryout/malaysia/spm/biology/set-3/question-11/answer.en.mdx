# Separate protection from memory and protection from borrowed antibodies

Killed bacteria make the mice produce their own antibodies and **memory cells**. Serum hands over ready-made antibodies that are broken down over a few weeks and leave no memory. So the timing of each injection decides what protects the mice on day <InlineMath math="60" />.

- I: group 1 has only <InlineMath math="10" /> units on day <InlineMath math="59" />, yet all its mice survive. Their memory cells recognise bacterium B and launch a fast, large **secondary response**. Statement I is correct.
- II: group 3 received its serum only five days before the exposure, and <InlineMath math="55" /> units are still in its blood. These borrowed antibodies fight the bacteria, so <InlineMath math="90\%" /> survive. Statement II is correct.
- III: group 1 has <InlineMath math="10" /> units and group 3 has <InlineMath math="55" /> units, so group 1 has **less** antibody, yet survives better. Its advantage is memory, not the antibody level. Statement III is wrong.
- IV: group 2 had <InlineMath math="60" /> units on day <InlineMath math="10" />, so its serum did contain antibodies. They were simply gone by day <InlineMath math="59" />. Statement IV is wrong.

So statements I and II are correct.

# Why the other options are wrong

- I, II and III only reads the antibody levels backwards, **since group 1 has less antibody than group 3**.
- I and III only rejects the passive protection of group 3 and misreads the antibody levels.
- II and IV only rejects the memory response and ignores the <InlineMath math="60" /> units group 2 had on day <InlineMath math="10" />.
