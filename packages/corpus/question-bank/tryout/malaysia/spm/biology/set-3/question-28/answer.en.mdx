# Match the rise in acid with the uptake from the air

Carbon dioxide can enter a leaf from the air only through **open stomata**. So the uptake row shows when the stomata are open, and the acid row shows where that carbon dioxide goes.

- From hour <InlineMath math="20" /> to hour <InlineMath math="4" />, uptake is high, so the stomata are open in the cool night, and the acid rises from <InlineMath math="20" /> to <InlineMath math="80" />. The carbon dioxide from the air is being **fixed into an acid** and stored in the cell sap.
- At hour <InlineMath math="8" />, after a whole night of storing, the acid peaks at <InlineMath math="100" />.
- From hour <InlineMath math="8" /> to hour <InlineMath math="16" />, uptake is close to <InlineMath math="0" />, so the stomata are shut against the heat, yet the acid falls to <InlineMath math="10" />. The stored acid releases its carbon dioxide inside the leaf, and the chloroplasts use it in the light.

Keeping the stomata shut in the hot day saves water, which is how the pineapple survives in a dry habitat.

So the acid peaks at hour <InlineMath math="8" /> because carbon dioxide taken in through stomata open at night is stored as an acid until light is available.

# Why the other options are wrong

- Respiration would release carbon dioxide inside the leaf, **but the data show carbon dioxide entering from the air**.
- Photosynthesis in the morning cannot explain the acid, **because the acid rises all night, when there is no light**.
- Closed stomata at night contradicts the data, **since uptake from the air is highest at night**.
