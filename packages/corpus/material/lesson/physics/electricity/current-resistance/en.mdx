## Current and Ohm Law

Picture electric charge like water flowing round a closed pipe: **electric current** measures how much charge passes each second, while **potential difference** is the energy push per coulomb. At constant temperature, ohmic conductors follow **Ohm's law**:

<BlockMath math="I = \frac{Q}{t}, \qquad V = \frac{W}{Q}, \qquad V = IR" />

In metal wires electrons drift from negative to positive, whereas **conventional current** points from positive to negative. A resistor turns electrical energy into heat, yet **never uses up charge**, so series current stays identical everywhere.

## Wire and Circuit Resistance

A wire's resistance depends on its **length**, **cross-sectional area**, **material**, and **temperature**, while resistors combine in **series** or **parallel**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="R = \frac{\rho l}{A}" />
<BlockMath math="R_{\text{series}} = R_1 + R_2, \qquad \frac{1}{R_{\text{parallel}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}" />
</MathContainer>

### Wire Length and Width

Think of a narrow hallway versus a wide highway: doubling length doubles resistance, while doubling **diameter** quadruples cross-sectional area and cuts resistance to one quarter.

> Wire resistance grows directly with length and drops inversely with the square of diameter.

### Broken Bulb Network

When one bulb in a parallel branch burns out, removing that branch raises parallel resistance, lowers battery current, and **shifts a larger voltage share onto the surviving parallel bulb**.

> When one parallel branch breaks, total circuit resistance rises so battery current drops and more supply voltage shifts across the remaining parallel bulb.

## Cell Voltage and Internal

**Electromotive force** (<InlineMath math="E" />) is the energy a cell supplies per coulomb, yet its **internal resistance** <InlineMath math="r" /> consumes part of that voltage whenever current flows:

<BlockMath math="V = E - Ir, \qquad I = \frac{E}{R + r}" />

A voltmeter shows full e.m.f. only on open circuit, and once current flows the terminal voltage drops below <InlineMath math="E" />.

## Electrical Power Efficiency

**Electrical power** is the rate of energy transfer, and home bills count energy in **kilowatt-hours** (<InlineMath math="3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}" /> per <InlineMath math="\text{kWh}" />):

<BlockMath math="P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}, \qquad E = Pt" />

**Efficiency** measures the useful share of input energy: at the same <InlineMath math="1\text{ W}" /> input, a <InlineMath math="40\%" /> efficient LED emits <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ W}" /> of light compared with only <InlineMath math="0.05\text{ W}" /> from a filament bulb.

### Heater on Half Voltage

A heater label of <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" /> and <InlineMath math="1000\text{ W}" /> applies only at <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" />. Plugged into another voltage, **its resistance stays fixed**, not its power.

> For a fixed-resistance heater, power scales with the square of supply voltage so half the voltage delivers only one quarter of the rated power.

### Choosing a Safe Fuse

A <InlineMath math="2000\text{ W}" /> kettle on <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" /> draws <InlineMath math="8.3\text{ A}" />. Pick a **fuse rated just above normal current**, namely <InlineMath math="10\text{ A}" />, rather than <InlineMath math="3\text{ A}" /> (blows immediately) or <InlineMath math="30\text{ A}" /> (fails to protect during a fault).

Quick self-checks: <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ A}" /> for <InlineMath math="2" /> minutes transfers <InlineMath math="60\text{ C}" /> (<InlineMath math="3.75 \times 10^{20}" /> electrons), a <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> copper wire of area <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2" /> has resistance <InlineMath math="0.17\ \Omega" />, and a cell giving <InlineMath math="1.4\text{ V}" /> at <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ A}" /> and <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ V}" /> at <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ A}" /> has <InlineMath math="r = 0.5\ \Omega" /> and <InlineMath math="E = 1.5\text{ V}" />.
