## Current Is a Flow of Charge

**Electric current** is the rate of flow of charge, <InlineMath math="I = \dfrac{Q}{t}" />, measured in amperes. The **potential difference** between two points is the work done to move one coulomb of charge between them, <InlineMath math="V = \dfrac{W}{Q}" />, measured in volts. For an ohmic conductor the two are linked by **Ohm's law**, <InlineMath math="V = IR" />, as long as the conductor's temperature stays constant. In these notes you change a resistance and compare current graphs, combine resistors, find the e.m.f. of a cell, and compare the efficiency of two bulbs.

In a metal wire, the moving charges are electrons. Electrons flow from the negative terminal to the positive one, but **conventional current** is defined to flow from positive to negative. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes a point every second.

## Resistance Depends on the Wire and the Arrangement

The resistance of a wire depends on its **length**, its **cross-sectional area**, its **material** and its **temperature**. At constant temperature the relation is <InlineMath math="R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}" />, where <InlineMath math="\rho" /> is the resistivity of the material. A wire twice as long has twice the resistance, and a wire with twice the area has half the resistance.

Resistors can be arranged in **series** or in **parallel**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Series: } R = R_1 + R_2" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Parallel: } \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}" />
</MathContainer>

In series, the same current flows through every resistor. In parallel, every branch has the same potential difference across it, and the effective resistance is always **smaller** than the smallest resistor. ## Electromotive Force and Internal Resistance

The **electromotive force**, or e.m.f., <InlineMath math="E" />, is the energy a cell supplies to move one coulomb of charge round a complete circuit. A real cell has an **internal resistance**, <InlineMath math="r" />, so part of the energy is lost inside the cell itself. The potential difference across the cell's terminals is:

<BlockMath math="V = E - Ir" />

With no current flowing, a voltmeter reads the e.m.f. As the current grows, the terminal reading drops. Change the e.m.f. and the internal resistance below: the line crosses the vertical axis at <InlineMath math="E" />, and its gradient is <InlineMath math="-r" />.

## Electrical Energy and Power

**Electrical power** is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred. For an appliance with current <InlineMath math="I" /> and potential difference <InlineMath math="V" />:

<BlockMath math="P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}, \qquad E = Pt" />

Electricity bills count energy in **kilowatt-hours**, <InlineMath math="\text{kWh}" />: the energy a <InlineMath math="1\text{ kW}" /> appliance uses in one hour. A <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ kW}" /> heater switched on for <InlineMath math="3" /> hours uses <InlineMath math="2.0 \times 3 = 6.0\text{ kWh}" />, which is <InlineMath math="6.0 \times 3.6 \times 10^6 = 2.16 \times 10^7\text{ J}" />.

## Common Mistakes about Electricity

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Current gets smaller after passing through a resistor. | Charge doesn't disappear, so the current in a series circuit is the same at every point. | Ammeters before and after the resistor read the same value. |
| Adding a resistor in parallel raises the effective resistance. | A new branch gives an extra path, so the effective resistance falls. | A parallel combination is smaller than its smallest resistor. |
| A voltmeter across a cell always reads the e.m.f. | When current flows it reads <InlineMath math="E - Ir" />, which is less than the e.m.f. | Compare the readings with the switch open and closed. |
| <InlineMath math="1\text{ kWh}" /> equals <InlineMath math="1000\text{ J}" />. | <InlineMath math="1\text{ kWh} = 1000 \times 3600 = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}" />. | Convert the hours to seconds. |
| Doubling the current doubles the power in a resistor. | <InlineMath math="P = I^2R" />, so the power becomes four times bigger. | Work out <InlineMath math="2^2 = 4" />. |

The first mistake comes up most often. A resistor **converts the energy** of the charge into heat, but it **doesn't use up the charge**.

## Practice on Electricity

These questions move from charge and resistance to e.m.f. and power. **Draw the circuit diagram** first so the series and parallel parts are clear.

1. A current of <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ A}" /> flows for <InlineMath math="2" /> minutes. Calculate the charge that flows and the number of electrons involved. The charge on one electron is <InlineMath math="1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}" />.

2. A copper wire <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> long has a cross-sectional area of <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2" />. The resistivity of copper is <InlineMath math="1.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{ m}" />. Calculate the resistance of the wire.

3. For one cell, a voltmeter reads <InlineMath math="1.4\text{ V}" /> when the current is <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ A}" />, and <InlineMath math="1.2\text{ V}" /> when the current is <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ A}" />. Calculate the internal resistance and the e.m.f. of the cell.

4. A <InlineMath math="2000\text{ W}" /> kettle is connected to a <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" /> supply. Calculate its normal current and choose a suitable fuse from <InlineMath math="3\text{ A}" />, <InlineMath math="10\text{ A}" /> and <InlineMath math="30\text{ A}" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Convert minutes to seconds, then use <InlineMath math="Q = It" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 0.50 \times 120 = 60\text{ C}" />
<BlockMath math="N = \frac{60}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.75 \times 10^{20}\text{ electrons}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="3.75 \times 10^{20} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 60\text{ C}" />.

**Solution 2**: Use <InlineMath math="R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}" />:

<BlockMath math="R = \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 10}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.17\ \Omega" />

Check the logic: copper is a good conductor, so its resistance should be small, well under <InlineMath math="1\ \Omega" />.

**Solution 3**: From <InlineMath math="V = E - Ir" />, the change in the voltmeter reading comes from the change in <InlineMath math="Ir" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="r = \frac{1.4 - 1.2}{0.6 - 0.2} = \frac{0.2}{0.4} = 0.5\ \Omega" />
<BlockMath math="E = V + Ir = 1.4 + 0.2 \times 0.5 = 1.5\text{ V}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the second reading: <InlineMath math="1.5 - 0.6 \times 0.5 = 1.2\text{ V}" />. That matches the voltmeter.

**Solution 4**: Use <InlineMath math="P = VI" />:

<BlockMath math="I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{2000}{240} = 8.3\text{ A}" />

A fuse should be rated a little above the normal current, so choose the **<InlineMath math="10\text{ A}" /> fuse**. A <InlineMath math="3\text{ A}" /> fuse would blow even when the kettle works normally, and a <InlineMath math="30\text{ A}" /> fuse wouldn't protect the kettle if a fault happened.
