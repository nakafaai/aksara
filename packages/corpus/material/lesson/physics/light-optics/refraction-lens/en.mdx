## Refraction of Light and the Law of Snell

**Refraction of light** is the change in direction of light as it passes from one medium into another of different optical density. **Snell's law** says that the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant, the **refractive index** of the medium:

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{c}{v}" />

Here <InlineMath math="i" /> is the angle of incidence and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the angle of refraction, both measured from the normal. <InlineMath math="c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> is the speed of light in a vacuum and <InlineMath math="v" /> is its speed in the medium. In the models here, you change the angle of a ray, trap light with total internal reflection, and move an object in front of a lens.

Light bends **towards the normal** when it enters an optically denser medium, because it travels more slowly there. Move the sliders for the angle of incidence and the refractive index, then compare the angle of refraction.

### Example Finding an Angle of Refraction

A ray of light enters glass of refractive index <InlineMath math="1.5" /> at an angle of incidence of <InlineMath math="40^\circ" />. Find **the angle of refraction**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sin r = \frac{\sin 40^\circ}{1.5} = \frac{0.643}{1.5} = 0.429" />
<BlockMath math="r = 25.4^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="i = 40" /> and <InlineMath math="n = 1.5" />, you read an angle of refraction of <InlineMath math="25.4^\circ" />. It's smaller than the angle of incidence, so the ray bends towards the normal.

### Example Real and Apparent Depth

A pool looks shallower than it really is because light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it leaves the water. The relationship is <InlineMath math="n = \dfrac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}" />. For a pool <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ m}" /> deep with <InlineMath math="n = 1.33" />:

<BlockMath math="\text{apparent depth} = \frac{2.0}{1.33} = 1.5\text{ m}" />

The pool looks **half a metre shallower**. Check: <InlineMath math="1.5 \times 1.33 = 2.0" />.

## Total Internal Reflection

When light travels from a denser medium into a less dense one, the angle of refraction is larger than the angle of incidence. At the **critical angle** <InlineMath math="c" />, the angle of refraction reaches <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. If the angle of incidence goes beyond the critical angle, all the light reflects back into the denser medium. This is called **total internal reflection**:

<BlockMath math="\sin c = \frac{1}{n}" />

For glass with <InlineMath math="n = 1.5" />, the critical angle is <InlineMath math="41.8^\circ" />. Move the angle of incidence past that value and watch the refracted ray skim along the surface, until only the reflected ray is left.

## Lenses and Image Formation

A **convex lens** brings parallel rays together at one point behind the lens, the **focal point** <InlineMath math="F" />. The distance from the optical centre to the focal point is the **focal length** <InlineMath math="f" />. The **lens formula** gives the image position:

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}, \qquad m = \frac{v}{u}" />

<InlineMath math="u" /> is the object distance, <InlineMath math="v" /> is the image distance and <InlineMath math="m" /> is the linear magnification. For a real image, <InlineMath math="v" /> is positive. The ray diagram below uses a convex lens with <InlineMath math="f = 2" /> units. Move the object and watch the real, inverted image change size.

When the object is further away than <InlineMath math="2f" />, the image is real, inverted and **smaller**, as in a camera. When the object sits between <InlineMath math="f" /> and <InlineMath math="2f" />, the image is real, inverted and **larger**, as in a projector. If the object is closer than <InlineMath math="f" />, the rays don't cross behind the lens and the image becomes virtual, upright and magnified. That's how a magnifying glass works.

### Example Lens Formula

An object is placed <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> in front of a convex lens of focal length <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />. Find **the image distance and the magnification**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{1}{30}" />
<BlockMath math="v = 30\text{ cm}, \qquad m = \frac{30}{15} = 2" />
</MathContainer>

The image is real, inverted and twice as large. Check with the model: <InlineMath math="u = 1.5f" /> in the model is <InlineMath math="o = 3" />, and the model gives <InlineMath math="v = 6 = 3f" />, matching <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm} = 3 \times 10\text{ cm}" />.

## Common Mistakes about Light and Optics

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The angle of incidence is measured from the surface. | The angles of incidence and refraction are measured from the normal. | Draw the normal first, then measure the angle. |
| Total internal reflection happens when light enters glass. | It only happens going from a denser medium to a less dense one. | Check the direction: from high index to low index. |
| A convex lens always gives an inverted image. | If <InlineMath math="u < f" />, the image is virtual and upright. | Compare <InlineMath math="u" /> with <InlineMath math="f" /> first. |
| Using centimetres for lens power. | Lens power <InlineMath math="P = 1/f" /> needs <InlineMath math="f" /> in metres. | Convert <InlineMath math="f" /> to metres before dividing. |

The first mistake is the most common one in Snell's law questions. **The normal is the reference line** for every angle in refraction and reflection.

## Practice on Light and Optics

These questions move from Snell's law to lenses. **Draw a quick sketch** before you calculate.

1. The speed of light in a type of plastic is <InlineMath math="2.0 \times 10^{8}\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. Calculate the refractive index of the plastic.

2. Calculate the critical angle for water with a refractive index of <InlineMath math="1.33" />, and explain what happens to a ray inside the water that hits the surface at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />.

3. An object is placed <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> from a convex lens of focal length <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" />. Calculate the image distance and the magnification, and state the nature of the image.

4. Calculate the power of a convex lens with a focal length of <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Use <InlineMath math="n = c/v" />:

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{2.0 \times 10^{8}} = 1.5" />

**Solution 2**: The critical angle comes from <InlineMath math="\sin c = 1/n" />:

<BlockMath math="c = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{1.33}\right) = 48.8^\circ" />

The angle <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> is larger than <InlineMath math="48.8^\circ" />, so the ray undergoes **total internal reflection** and no light escapes into the air.

**Solution 3**: Use the lens formula:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{30} = \frac{1}{60}" />
<BlockMath math="v = 60\text{ cm}, \qquad m = \frac{60}{30} = 2" />
</MathContainer>

The object sits between <InlineMath math="f" /> and <InlineMath math="2f" />, so the image is real, inverted and magnified twice.

**Solution 4**: Convert the focal length to metres, <InlineMath math="f = 0.25\text{ m}" />:

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{1}{0.25} = 4\text{ D}" />

A positive power means a convex lens.
