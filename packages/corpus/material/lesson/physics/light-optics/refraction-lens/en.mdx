## Snell Law and Refraction

**Refraction of light** is the change in direction of light as it enters a medium of different optical density. **Snell's law** states that the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant, the **refractive index**:

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{c}{v}" />

Here <InlineMath math="i" /> is the angle of incidence and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the angle of refraction, both always **measured from the normal**, never from the surface. Light travelling at <InlineMath math="c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> in vacuum slows to <InlineMath math="v" /> and bends towards the normal inside a denser medium.

### Angle of Refraction

A ray of light enters glass of refractive index <InlineMath math="1.5" /> at an angle of incidence of <InlineMath math="40^\circ" />. Find **the angle of refraction** with Snell's law:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sin r = \frac{\sin 40^\circ}{1.5} = \frac{0.643}{1.5} = 0.429" />
<BlockMath math="r = 25.4^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

At <InlineMath math="i = 40^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="n = 1.5" />, the model gives <InlineMath math="25.4^\circ" />, smaller than the incidence angle because the ray bends towards the normal.

### Apparent Depth Layers

Imagine dropping a coin into a glass of water and pouring a layer of oil on top. As rays escape into air, they **bend away from the normal**, making the coin look lifted closer to the surface:

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}" />

> Each liquid layer adds an apparent depth equal to its thickness divided by its refractive index, raising the image above the true floor.

For a <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ m}" /> pool with <InlineMath math="n = 1.33" />, the apparent depth is <InlineMath math="2.0 / 1.33 = 1.5\text{ m}" />. Now stack two liquid layers in the 3D lab below.

## Total Internal Reflection

When light goes from a denser medium into a less dense one, the angle of refraction exceeds the angle of incidence. At the **critical angle** <InlineMath math="c" />, refraction reaches <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, and beyond it comes **total internal reflection**:

<BlockMath math="\sin c = \frac{1}{n}" />

Total internal reflection **only happens from high index to low index**, never when light enters glass from air. For glass with <InlineMath math="n = 1.5" />, the critical angle is <InlineMath math="41.8^\circ" />.

## Convex Lens Images

A **convex lens** focuses parallel rays at its **focal point** <InlineMath math="F" />, a **focal length** <InlineMath math="f" /> from the optical centre. Image position and magnification obey the **lens formula**:

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}, \qquad m = \frac{v}{u}" />

Here <InlineMath math="u" /> is object distance and <InlineMath math="v" /> is image distance. Move the object in the ray diagram with <InlineMath math="f = 2" /> units below to watch the real image change size.

A convex lens does **not always form an inverted image**: whenever <InlineMath math="u < f" />, the image becomes virtual and upright like a magnifying glass. For <InlineMath math="u = 15\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="f = 10\text{ cm}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{1}{30}" />
<BlockMath math="v = 30\text{ cm}, \qquad m = \frac{30}{15} = 2" />
</MathContainer>

> The moment an object crosses the focal point of a convex lens, its image flips from real and inverted behind the lens to virtual and upright in front.

### Astronomical Telescope

How do two ordinary convex lenses bring lunar craters close? Pair a **low-power objective** at the front with a **high-power eyepiece** at the back so their focal points overlap inside the tube.

> In an astronomical telescope at normal adjustment, both focal points coincide so the lens separation equals the sum of focal lengths and magnification equals their ratio.

With a <InlineMath math="+1.0\text{ D}" /> objective and a <InlineMath math="+20\text{ D}" /> eyepiece, convert each power to focal length first:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="f_o = 100\text{ cm}, \qquad f_e = 5\text{ cm}" />
<BlockMath math="L = f_o + f_e = 105\text{ cm}, \qquad M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = 20" />
</MathContainer>

## Quick Optics Checks

Work through these four short problems in order, then **check every result** against the summary below.

1. Light travels at <InlineMath math="2.0 \times 10^{8}\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> in plastic. Find its refractive index.
2. Find the critical angle of water (<InlineMath math="n = 1.33" />) and what happens to a ray hitting the surface from inside water at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />.
3. An object stands <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" /> from a convex lens (<InlineMath math="f = 20\text{ cm}" />). Find <InlineMath math="v" />, <InlineMath math="m" />, and the nature of the image.
4. Calculate the power of a convex lens of focal length <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}" />.

Quick answers: problem one gives <InlineMath math="n = 1.5" />, problem two gives <InlineMath math="c = 48.8^\circ" /> so <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> undergoes **total internal reflection**, problem three yields <InlineMath math="v = 60\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="m = 2" /> (real, inverted, magnified), and problem four gives <InlineMath math="P = 4\text{ D}" /> after converting <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="0.25\text{ m}" />.
