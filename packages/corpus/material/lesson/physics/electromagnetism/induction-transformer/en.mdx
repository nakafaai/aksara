## Electricity and Magnetism Are Linked

**Electromagnetism** studies the link between electric current and magnetic fields. A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a **force**, <InlineMath math="F = BIL" />, and that's how a motor works. The other way round, a conductor cutting a magnetic field produces an **induced electromotive force**, and that's how a generator works. A transformer changes the voltage of alternating current according to <InlineMath math="\dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = \dfrac{N_s}{N_p}" />. In these notes you change a current and a field, spin a generator at different speeds, change the turns on a transformer, and compare the energy lost in a cable.

When current flows in a wire, a magnetic field forms around the wire. If the wire sits in another magnetic field, the two fields combine into a **catapult field**. The field on one side of the wire gets stronger and on the other side weaker, so the wire is pushed towards the weaker field.

## The Force on a Conductor Carrying Current

The direction of the force comes from **Fleming's left-hand rule**: the first finger points along the magnetic field, the second finger along the current, and the thumb along the force. The size of the force depends on the field strength <InlineMath math="B" />, the current <InlineMath math="I" /> and the length of wire in the field <InlineMath math="L" />:

In a **direct current motor**, a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field feels two opposite forces on its two sides, so the coil turns. The **commutator** reverses the current every half turn so the coil keeps turning the same way. The motor turns faster if the current increases, the magnetic field is stronger, or the coil has more turns.

## Electromagnetic Induction and Generators

When a conductor cuts magnetic field lines, or the magnetic field through a coil changes, an **induced electromotive force** appears. **Faraday's law** says the size of the induced e.m.f. is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux. **Lenz's law** says the induced current always opposes the change that produces it. The direction of the current in a moving wire comes from **Fleming's right-hand rule**.

Lenz's law is a form of energy conservation. If you push the north pole of a magnet into a coil, the near end of the coil becomes a north pole and pushes the magnet back. You have to do work to push the magnet in, and that work becomes electrical energy.

An **alternating current generator** spins a coil in a magnetic field, so the induced e.m.f. follows a sine wave. Spin the coil faster below: the peak voltage and the frequency both go up, because the flux changes faster.

## A Transformer Changes the Voltage

A **transformer** has two coils, the primary coil and the secondary coil, wound on a soft iron core. Alternating current in the primary coil makes a constantly changing magnetic flux in the core, and that flux induces an e.m.f. in the secondary coil. A transformer **works only with alternating current**, because direct current doesn't make a changing flux.

<BlockMath math="\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="V_s = 240 \times \frac{60}{1200} = 12\text{ V}" />
<BlockMath math="V_pI_p = V_sI_s \Rightarrow I_s = \frac{240 \times 0.10}{12} = 2.0\text{ A}" />

Check with power: input <InlineMath math="240 \times 0.10 = 24\text{ W}" /> and output <InlineMath math="12 \times 2.0 = 24\text{ W}" />. An ideal transformer loses no energy, so the voltage falls twenty times and the current rises twenty times.

A real transformer loses some energy through **coil resistance**, **eddy currents** in the core, **hysteresis** and **flux leakage**. A laminated core, thick copper wire and a soft iron core reduce those losses. **Efficiency** is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{P_{\text{output}}}{P_{\text{input}}} \times 100\%" />.

## Transmitting Energy at High Voltage

Energy from a power station travels through cables that have resistance. The power lost as heat in the cables is <InlineMath math="P_{\text{lost}} = I^2R" />. For the same power, a higher voltage means a smaller current, so far less is lost. That's why the **National Grid** uses step-up transformers at the power station and step-down transformers near the users.

## Common Mistakes about Electromagnetism

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fleming's right-hand rule is used for a motor. | A motor uses the left-hand rule. The right-hand rule is for generators and induction. | Ask whether the current is supplied or induced. |
| A transformer can step up a direct current voltage. | Direct current makes no changing flux, so no e.m.f. is induced in the secondary coil. | A transformer works only with alternating current. |
| A step-up transformer increases the power. | For an ideal transformer, <InlineMath math="V_pI_p = V_sI_s" />. The voltage goes up but the current comes down. | The output power can't exceed the input power. |
| The induced current helps the magnet move into the coil. | Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change, so the magnet is pushed back. | If the current helped, energy would appear without work. |
| A high voltage in the cables increases the energy lost. | For the same power, a high voltage means a small current, and the loss is <InlineMath math="I^2R" />. | Compare <InlineMath math="I^2R" /> at two voltages. |

The first mistake comes up most often. Here's an easy way to remember: **motors use the left hand, generators use the right hand**.

## Practice on Electromagnetism

These questions move from motors to induction and transformers. **Write the equation before you substitute numbers** so the turns ratio doesn't get flipped.

1. State two ways to make a direct current motor turn faster.

2. The north pole of a magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a galvanometer. State the pole at the end of the coil nearest the magnet and explain why.

3. A step-down transformer changes <InlineMath math="240\text{ V}" /> to <InlineMath math="6\text{ V}" />. Its primary coil has <InlineMath math="2000" /> turns. Calculate the number of secondary turns.

4. A transformer has <InlineMath math="V_p = 240\text{ V}" />, <InlineMath math="I_p = 0.50\text{ A}" />, <InlineMath math="V_s = 12\text{ V}" /> and <InlineMath math="I_s = 9.0\text{ A}" />. Calculate its efficiency.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The force on each side of the coil is <InlineMath math="F = BIL" />, so increase the **current** in the coil or use a **stronger magnet**. Adding **more turns** to the coil also increases the force, because more wire sits in the field.

**Solution 2**: The near end of the coil becomes a **north pole**. By Lenz's law, the induced current opposes the change that produces it, which is the approaching magnet. The coil's north pole repels the magnet's north pole, so work has to be done to push the magnet in.

**Solution 3**: Rearrange the transformer ratio:

<BlockMath math="N_s = N_p \times \frac{V_s}{V_p} = 2000 \times \frac{6}{240} = 50\text{ turns}" />

Check: the ratio <InlineMath math="\dfrac{50}{2000} = \dfrac{1}{40}" /> equals <InlineMath math="\dfrac{6}{240}" />.

**Solution 4**: Work out the input and output power:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P_{\text{input}} = 240 \times 0.50 = 120\text{ W}, \quad P_{\text{output}} = 12 \times 9.0 = 108\text{ W}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Efficiency} = \frac{108}{120} \times 100\% = 90\%" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the missing <InlineMath math="12\text{ W}" /> becomes heat in the coils and core, so the efficiency must be below <InlineMath math="100\%" />.
