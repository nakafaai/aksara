## Electronics Controls the Flow of Electrons

**Electronics** is the study of controlling the flow of electrons in a vacuum and in semiconductors. Electrons can be released from a hot metal by **thermionic emission** and accelerated by a potential difference, with <InlineMath math="eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2" />. In a semiconductor, a **diode** lets current flow one way only, and a **transistor** uses a small base current to control a large collector current, <InlineMath math="I_C = \beta I_B" />. In these notes you deflect an electron beam, read a diode curve, turn alternating current into direct current, and work out the current gain of a transistor.

## Thermionic Emission and Cathode Rays

**Thermionic emission** is the release of electrons from the surface of a heated metal. In a vacuum tube, a filament heats the cathode, and the released electrons are accelerated towards the positive anode. This beam of electrons is called a **cathode ray**. The vacuum is needed so the electrons don't collide with air molecules.

Cathode rays travel in straight lines, carry negative charge and make a screen fluoresce. Because cathode rays are charged, electric and magnetic fields can deflect them. Change the anode voltage and the electric field in the tube below and watch the position of the spot on the screen.

The electrical potential energy an electron loses as it accelerates becomes kinetic energy:

<BlockMath math="eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m}}" />

Try an example. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of <InlineMath math="2000\text{ V}" />. Calculate its final speed. The electron's charge is <InlineMath math="1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}" /> and its mass <InlineMath math="9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}}" />
<BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{7.02 \times 10^{14}} = 2.65 \times 10^7\text{ m s}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the graph below: at <InlineMath math="2000\text{ V}" /> the speed reading is also about <InlineMath math="2.65 \times 10^7\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. The speed is proportional to the **square root** of the voltage, so four times the voltage only doubles the speed.

## Semiconductors and Diodes

A **semiconductor** such as silicon has a conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator. Its conductivity is raised by **doping**, which means adding a few impurity atoms. Pentavalent atoms such as phosphorus make an **n-type semiconductor**, with free electrons as the majority charge carriers. Trivalent atoms such as boron make a **p-type semiconductor**, with holes as the majority charge carriers.

A **diode** is a junction between p-type and n-type semiconductor. At the junction a **depletion layer** forms, with no charge carriers in it. In **forward bias**, the p terminal is connected to the positive terminal, the depletion layer narrows, and current flows once the voltage passes about <InlineMath math="0.7\text{ V}" /> for silicon. In **reverse bias**, the depletion layer widens and almost no current flows.

## Rectification with Diodes

**Rectification** is turning alternating current into direct current. In **half-wave rectification**, one diode lets only the positive half-cycle through, so the negative half-cycle disappears. In **full-wave rectification**, four diodes in a bridge flip the negative half-cycle, so both half-cycles come out positive. A **capacitor** connected in parallel with the load smooths the output by storing charge while the voltage is high and releasing it while the voltage is low.

## The Transistor as a Switch and an Amplifier

A **transistor** has three terminals: the **emitter**, the **base** and the **collector**. A small base current controls a large collector current, and the emitter current is the sum of the two:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="I_E = I_B + I_C" />
<BlockMath math="I_C = \beta I_B" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="\beta" /> is the **current gain**, usually between <InlineMath math="50" /> and <InlineMath math="300" />. Change the current gain and the base current below.

A transistor also works as an **automatic switch**. In a street light, a **light-dependent resistor** and a fixed resistor form a potential divider on the base. In daylight the resistance of the light-dependent resistor is low, the base voltage is small, and the transistor is off. At night its resistance is high. For example, with a <InlineMath math="6\text{ V}" /> supply, a <InlineMath math="10\text{ k}\Omega" /> fixed resistor and a light-dependent resistor at <InlineMath math="90\text{ k}\Omega" />, the voltage across the light-dependent resistor is:

<BlockMath math="V_{\text{base}} = 6 \times \frac{90}{10 + 90} = 5.4\text{ V}" />

That's above <InlineMath math="0.7\text{ V}" />, so the transistor switches on and the lamp lights. Check: in daylight, if the light-dependent resistor drops to <InlineMath math="1\text{ k}\Omega" />, its voltage is only <InlineMath math="6 \times \tfrac{1}{11} = 0.55\text{ V}" />, below <InlineMath math="0.7\text{ V}" />, so the lamp stays off.

## Common Mistakes about Electronics

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cathode rays bend towards the negative plate. | Electrons are negative, so they're pulled towards the positive plate. | Opposite charges attract. |
| Doubling the accelerating voltage doubles the electron's speed. | <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{\tfrac{2eV}{m}}" />, so the speed grows by only <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" />. | It takes four times the voltage to double the speed. |
| A forward-biased diode conducts at any voltage. | A silicon diode needs about <InlineMath math="0.7\text{ V}" /> to overcome the depletion layer. | Read the diode curve below <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ V}" />. |
| A p-type semiconductor is positively charged. | The material is neutral overall. Holes are only its majority charge carriers. | Count the protons and electrons in the doped atoms. |
| The emitter current equals the collector current. | <InlineMath math="I_E = I_B + I_C" />, so the emitter current is slightly bigger. | Add the base current to the collector current. |

The first mistake comes up most often. Cathode rays are **electrons**, so they're always pulled towards the **positive plate**.

## Practice on Electronics

These questions move from cathode rays to diodes and transistors. **Write microamperes and milliamperes clearly** so the factor of a thousand doesn't slip.

1. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of <InlineMath math="500\text{ V}" />. Calculate its kinetic energy in joules.

2. A <InlineMath math="50\text{ Hz}" /> alternating supply is rectified. State the number of output pulses per second for half-wave and full-wave rectification, and the number of diodes each needs.

3. A transistor has a current gain of <InlineMath math="150" />. Calculate the collector current when the base current is <InlineMath math="20\ \mu\text{A}" />.

4. Explain why a reverse-biased diode conducts almost no current.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The kinetic energy equals the work done by the electric field:

<BlockMath math="E_k = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 500 = 8.0 \times 10^{-17}\text{ J}" />

Check with electronvolts: an electron accelerated through <InlineMath math="500\text{ V}" /> gains <InlineMath math="500\text{ eV}" />, and <InlineMath math="500 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 8.0 \times 10^{-17}\text{ J}" />.

**Solution 2**: Half-wave rectification needs **one diode** and gives **<InlineMath math="50" /> pulses per second**, one per cycle. Full-wave rectification needs **four diodes** in a bridge and gives **<InlineMath math="100" /> pulses per second**, because each cycle gives two positive half-cycles.

**Solution 3**: Use <InlineMath math="I_C = \beta I_B" />:

<BlockMath math="I_C = 150 \times 20\ \mu\text{A} = 3000\ \mu\text{A} = 3.0\text{ mA}" />

Check: the collector current must be much bigger than the base current, and <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ mA}" /> is indeed <InlineMath math="150" /> times <InlineMath math="0.020\text{ mA}" />.

**Solution 4**: In reverse bias, the p terminal is connected to the negative terminal of the supply. The holes in the p-type and the electrons in the n-type are pulled **away from the junction**, so the **depletion layer widens**. That layer has no charge carriers, so it acts as an insulator and almost no current flows.
