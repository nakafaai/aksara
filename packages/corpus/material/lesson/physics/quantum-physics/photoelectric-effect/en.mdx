## Light Comes in Packets of Energy

**Quantum theory** says that light energy doesn't flow continuously but comes in small packets called **photons**. The energy of one photon depends only on its frequency:

<BlockMath math="E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}" />

Here <InlineMath math="h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}" /> is the Planck constant, <InlineMath math="f" /> the frequency and <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> the wavelength of the light. In these notes you watch a hydrogen atom emit spectral lines, change the frequency of light to knock electrons out of a metal, and calculate the wavelength of a moving particle.

Blue light carries more energy per photon than red light because its wavelength is shorter. Move the wavelength slider below and read the photon energy in electronvolts, where <InlineMath math="1\text{ eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}" />.

## Atoms Emit Photons at Fixed Energies

Electrons in an atom can only sit at **fixed energy levels**. When an electron drops from a high level to a low one, the atom emits one photon whose energy **equals the difference** between the two levels. Because the levels are fixed, the light comes out only at certain wavelengths, and you see a **line spectrum** instead of a continuous rainbow.

## The Photoelectric Effect and the Threshold Frequency

The **photoelectric effect** is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when light shines on it. The emitted electrons are called **photoelectrons**. Experiments show three things that wave theory can't explain:

| Observation | What it means |
| :--- | :--- |
| No electrons come out below the **threshold frequency**, <InlineMath math="f_0" />, however bright the light. | One photon must carry enough energy to free one electron. |
| Above <InlineMath math="f_0" />, electrons come out instantly. | Energy arrives in one packet instead of building up slowly. |
| The maximum kinetic energy of the electrons depends on frequency, not intensity. | Intensity only adds photons. It doesn't change the energy of each one. |

The minimum energy needed to free one electron is the **work function**, <InlineMath math="W" />. For sodium, <InlineMath math="W = 2.28\text{ eV}" />, and for zinc <InlineMath math="W = 4.3\text{ eV}" />. The threshold frequency is <InlineMath math="f_0 = \dfrac{W}{h}" />.

## The Photoelectric Equation of Einstein

Einstein explained the photoelectric effect with **conservation of energy** for one photon and one electron. The photon's energy pays the work function, and what's left becomes the electron's maximum kinetic energy:

<BlockMath math="hf = W + K_{\text{max}}" />

## Particles Behave like Waves Too

If light, which we treat as a wave, can act like a particle, then particles can act like waves too. This is **wave-particle duality**. De Broglie proposed that every particle with momentum <InlineMath math="p = mv" /> has a wavelength:

<BlockMath math="\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}" />

Electrons in a cathode-ray tube are deflected by an electric field like charged particles. Yet the same electron beam makes a diffraction pattern when it passes through a crystal, which is evidence of its wave nature. Watch electrons being deflected in the tube below.

## Common Mistakes about Quantum Physics

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Brighter light knocks out faster electrons. | Intensity adds photons. The energy of each photon depends on frequency. | Change the intensity, and <InlineMath math="K_{\text{max}}" /> stays the same. |
| Very bright red light can still free electrons from zinc. | If <InlineMath math="hf" /> is less than <InlineMath math="W" />, no photon has enough energy. | Compare the photon energy with the work function. |
| A longer wavelength means a bigger photon energy. | <InlineMath math="E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}" />, so energy is inversely proportional to wavelength. | Blue light is more energetic than red light. |
| A wavelength in nanometres goes straight into <InlineMath math="E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}" />. | The formula needs metres to give joules. | Multiply by <InlineMath math="10^{-9}" /> first. |
| Only light behaves like a wave. | Electrons diffract too, so particles also have a wavelength. | Work out <InlineMath math="\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}" /> for the particle. |

The first mistake comes up most often. Remember the split: **frequency sets the energy of each photon, intensity sets the number of photons**.

## Practice on Quantum Physics

These questions move from photon energy to the photoelectric effect and de Broglie wavelengths. **Convert every unit to SI** before you use the Planck constant.

1. A <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mW}" /> laser emits red light at <InlineMath math="650\text{ nm}" />. Calculate the number of photons it emits each second.

2. The work function of zinc is <InlineMath math="4.3\text{ eV}" />. Does green light at <InlineMath math="500\text{ nm}" /> free electrons from zinc? Give a reason.

3. Light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal. Its intensity is doubled without changing the frequency. State the effect on the number of photoelectrons and on their maximum kinetic energy.

4. An electron and a proton move at the same speed. Calculate the ratio of the electron's de Broglie wavelength to the proton's, where <InlineMath math="m_p = 1836\,m_e" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Find the energy of one photon, then divide the power by it:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^8}{6.50 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="N = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.06 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.27 \times 10^{15}\text{ photons s}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="3.27 \times 10^{15} \times 3.06 \times 10^{-19} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ J}" /> each second, the same as the laser's power.

**Solution 2**: A <InlineMath math="500\text{ nm}" /> photon carries <InlineMath math="2.49\text{ eV}" />, as in the example above. That's **less than** the work function of zinc, <InlineMath math="4.3\text{ eV}" />, so **no electrons come out**, however bright the light. One photon doesn't carry enough energy, and an electron can't collect energy from several photons.

**Solution 3**: Doubling the intensity means **twice as many photons**, so **twice as many photoelectrons** come out and the photocurrent grows. The frequency hasn't changed, so each photon has the same energy and the **maximum kinetic energy stays the same**:

<BlockMath math="K_{\text{max}} = hf - W" />

**Solution 4**: At the same speed, the wavelength is inversely proportional to the mass:

<BlockMath math="\frac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p} = \frac{h / (m_e v)}{h / (m_p v)} = \frac{m_p}{m_e} = 1836" />

The electron's wavelength is <InlineMath math="1836" /> times longer. Check: a lighter particle at the same speed has less momentum, so its wavelength is longer.
