## Blackbody Radiation Quanta

Heat an iron poker in a furnace and its glow shifts from dull red to bright orange and eventually white. Classical wave physics predicted that a hot cavity should pour out infinite ultraviolet energy, a breakdown known as the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

## Photon Energy and Frequency

Light itself travels in discrete packets called **photons**. Each photon carries an energy set solely by its frequency <InlineMath math="f" />, or inversely by its wavelength <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

<BlockMath math="E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}" />

Here <InlineMath math="h" /> is the Planck constant (<InlineMath math="6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s}" />) and <InlineMath math="c" /> is the speed of light (<InlineMath math="3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}" />). Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon, so violet light packs far more energy per packet than red light.

## Photoelectric Emission

When high-frequency light strikes a clean metal plate inside an evacuated tube, the **photoelectric effect** frees electrons straight from the surface. Measuring both the threshold frequency and the energy of those escaping electrons proved that light delivers energy in discrete single-photon collisions.

### Threshold and Intensity

Classical wave theory failed here because bright red light releases zero electrons from zinc, whereas dim ultraviolet light frees **photoelectrons** immediately.

> Frequency sets the energy of every individual photon, whereas intensity only changes how many photons arrive each second.

### Einstein Energy Law

Albert Einstein explained photoelectric emission by applying energy conservation to a single photon absorbed by a single electron. Part of the photon's energy pays the metal's **work function** <InlineMath math="W" />, and any leftover energy becomes the electron's **maximum kinetic energy** <InlineMath math="K_{\text{max}}" />.

<BlockMath math="hf = W + K_{\text{max}}" />

## Matter Waves and Diffraction

Louis de Broglie reasoned that nature should be symmetric: if electromagnetic waves behave like particles, then moving particles with momentum <InlineMath math="p" /> must also carry a **matter wavelength** <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

<BlockMath math="\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}" />

> Greater momentum compresses a particle's de Broglie wavelength, tightening the concentric diffraction rings formed through a crystal lattice.

## Quantum Calculation Checks

Check two benchmark problems using SI units throughout.

**Laser photon check.** A <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mW}" /> laser emits <InlineMath math="650\text{ nm}" /> red light. Find how many photons leave the laser every second.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{6.50 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="N = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.06 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.27 \times 10^{15}\text{ photons s}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Multiplying photon count by energy per photon recovers the exact <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mW}" /> beam power.

**Electron wave check.** An electron (<InlineMath math="9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}" />) travels at <InlineMath math="2.0 \times 10^6\text{ m s}^{-1}" />. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

<BlockMath math="\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(2.0 \times 10^6)} = 3.64 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.364\text{ nm}" />

That sub-nanometre wavelength matches atomic spacing in graphite, explaining why electrons diffract cleanly through thin crystal films.
