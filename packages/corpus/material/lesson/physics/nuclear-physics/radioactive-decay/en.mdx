## Radioactive Decay Modes

Inside a heavy atomic nucleus, positively charged protons repel one another intensely while the **strong nuclear force** tries to hold them together. When that balance fails, an unstable nucleus undergoes **radioactive decay** to drop into a more stable state.

### Alpha Beta and Gamma

Every nuclear decay conserves both total **nucleon number** and total **proton number** across both sides of the equation:

- **Alpha decay** ejects a helium-4 nucleus, dropping the nucleon number by <InlineMath math="4" /> and the proton number by <InlineMath math="2" />.
- **Beta decay** turns a neutron into a proton and emits an electron, leaving the nucleon number unchanged while raising the proton number by <InlineMath math="1" />.
- **Gamma decay** releases a high-energy electromagnetic photon from an excited nucleus without changing either count.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="{}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" />
<BlockMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}e" />
</MathContainer>

Notice that beta decay raises the proton number rather than lowering it because a neutral neutron becomes a positive proton inside the nucleus.

### Half Life Background

Individual decays occur completely at random, and neither heating nor pressure can speed up a nucleus. Across billions of atoms, however, the **half-life** <InlineMath math="T_{1/2}" /> is fixed: the exact time required for half of the remaining unstable nuclei to decay.

## Exponential Decay Curves

A half-life never subtracts a fixed amount. Instead, **exponential decay** multiplies what remains by one half across every interval of <InlineMath math="T_{1/2}" />, so after two half-lives one quarter remains, not zero.

<BlockMath math="N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / T_{1/2}}" />

> Each half-life cuts the remaining fraction in half again, so the decay curve approaches zero smoothly without ever hitting zero in finite steps.

## Binding Energy and Stability

Weigh a helium-4 nucleus and compare it with two separate protons plus two separate neutrons. The bound nucleus is lighter. That missing mass is the **mass defect** <InlineMath math="\Delta m" />, released as **binding energy** when nucleons lock together.

<BlockMath math="E = \Delta m\,c^2" />

Here <InlineMath math="c" /> is <InlineMath math="3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}" />, and <InlineMath math="\Delta m" /> must be converted from atomic mass units into kilograms using <InlineMath math="1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}" /> per unit before computing joules.

> Moving upward toward the iron-56 peak always releases nuclear energy, whether by fusing light nuclei together or by splitting a heavy nucleus apart.

## Nuclear Energy Checks

Verify two core calculations with exact conservation and SI unit conversions.

**Radium alpha check.** Radium-226 (<InlineMath math="{}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}" />) emits one alpha particle. Balance its decay equation.

<BlockMath math="{}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" />

Both top nucleon counts sum to <InlineMath math="226" /> and both bottom proton counts sum to <InlineMath math="88" />.

**Fission mass check.** One nuclear fission loses <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ u}" /> of mass. Find the energy released in joules.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Delta m = 0.20 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.32 \times 10^{-28}\text{ kg}" />
<BlockMath math="E = (3.32 \times 10^{-28})(3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.99 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J}" />
</MathContainer>

Cross-checking via <InlineMath math="931.5\text{ MeV}" /> per atomic mass unit gives <InlineMath math="186\text{ MeV}" />, matching <InlineMath math="2.98 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J}" /> within rounding.
