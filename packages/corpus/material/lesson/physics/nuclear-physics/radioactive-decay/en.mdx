## Unstable Nuclei Decay at Random

**Radioactive decay** is the process in which an unstable nucleus emits alpha, beta or gamma radiation to become more stable. Decay happens **at random**: nobody can predict when one particular nucleus will decay. In a large sample, though, the number of nuclei left follows the half-life law, <InlineMath math="N = N_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}" />. In these notes you watch a carbon-14 sample decay, see radiation bent by a magnetic field, and calculate the energy that comes from a mass defect.

## Three Types of Radioactive Decay

In every decay, the **nucleon number** and the **proton number** are conserved. The total on the left of the equation must equal the total on the right.

**Alpha decay** emits an alpha particle, which is a helium nucleus. The nucleon number falls by <InlineMath math="4" /> and the proton number by <InlineMath math="2" />:

<BlockMath math="{}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" />

**Beta decay** happens when a neutron turns into a proton and an electron. The electron leaves as a beta particle, so the nucleon number stays the same but the proton number rises by <InlineMath math="1" />:

<BlockMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}e" />

**Gamma decay** emits a high-energy electromagnetic wave from an excited nucleus. The nucleon number and the proton number don't change. Only the energy of the nucleus drops.

The three radiations differ in **charge**, so a magnetic field separates them. Alpha particles are positive, beta particles are negative and gamma radiation has no charge. ## The Decay Curve

The **half-life**, <InlineMath math="T_{1/2}" />, is the time it takes for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. Half-life is a property of the nuclide itself and doesn't change with temperature, pressure or the mass of the sample. Iodine-131 has a half-life of <InlineMath math="8" /> days, while carbon-14 has <InlineMath math="5730" /> years.

Try an example. A hospital receives <InlineMath math="80\text{ g}" /> of iodine-131. Calculate the mass of iodine-131 left after <InlineMath math="24" /> days.

**Count the half-lives first**, then halve that many times:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{24}{8} = 3" />
<BlockMath math="80\text{ g} \rightarrow 40\text{ g} \rightarrow 20\text{ g} \rightarrow 10\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the formula: <InlineMath math="N = 80 \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = 80 \times \tfrac{1}{8} = 10\text{ g}" />. Both ways give <InlineMath math="10\text{ g}" />.

## Mass Defect and Nuclear Energy

In a nuclear reaction, the total mass of the products is **slightly less** than the total mass of the reactants. The difference is the **mass defect**, <InlineMath math="\Delta m" />, and the missing mass turns into energy according to Einstein's equation:

<BlockMath math="E = \Delta m\,c^2" />

Here <InlineMath math="c = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}" /> is the speed of light, and <InlineMath math="\Delta m" /> must be in kilograms. Nuclear masses are usually given in **atomic mass units**, where <InlineMath math="1\text{ u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}" />.

Calculate the energy released when deuterium fuses with tritium:

<BlockMath math="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{0}n" />

The masses are <InlineMath math="2.014102\text{ u}" />, <InlineMath math="3.016049\text{ u}" />, <InlineMath math="4.002603\text{ u}" /> and <InlineMath math="1.008665\text{ u}" />. **Subtract the product mass from the reactant mass**, convert to kilograms, then multiply by <InlineMath math="c^2" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Delta m = (2.014102 + 3.016049) - (4.002603 + 1.008665) = 0.018883\text{ u}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta m = 0.018883 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.13 \times 10^{-29}\text{ kg}" />
<BlockMath math="E = 3.13 \times 10^{-29} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.82 \times 10^{-12}\text{ J}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the energy equivalent of <InlineMath math="1\text{ u}" />, which is <InlineMath math="931.5\text{ MeV}" />: <InlineMath math="0.018883 \times 931.5 = 17.6\text{ MeV}" />, and <InlineMath math="17.6\text{ MeV} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\text{ J MeV}^{-1} = 2.82 \times 10^{-12}\text{ J}" />. The same.

## Nuclear Fission and Fusion

**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei after it absorbs a neutron. One fission of uranium-235 is:

<BlockMath math="{}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} + {}^{1}_{0}n \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}n + \text{energy}" />

The mass defect of this fission is about <InlineMath math="0.186\text{ u}" />, an energy of about <InlineMath math="173\text{ MeV}" />, ten times the energy of one fusion. **Nuclear fusion** is the joining of two light nuclei into a heavier one, like the deuterium and tritium example above. Fusion needs extremely high temperatures, and it's what powers the Sun.

Each fission releases several neutrons that can split other nuclei. This is a **chain reaction**. If on average <InlineMath math="k" /> neutrons from each fission cause a new fission, the number of fissions in generation <InlineMath math="g" /> is <InlineMath math="k^g" />. Change <InlineMath math="k" /> and compare <InlineMath math="k = 1" /> with a larger <InlineMath math="k" />.

In an atomic bomb, <InlineMath math="k" /> is greater than <InlineMath math="1" />, so the number of fissions explodes in a very short time. In a **nuclear reactor**, **control rods** of boron or cadmium absorb the extra neutrons so that <InlineMath math="k = 1" /> and energy is released at a steady rate. A **moderator**, such as water or graphite, slows the neutrons down so uranium-235 absorbs them more easily.

## Common Mistakes about Nuclear Physics

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| After two half-lives, the sample has fully decayed. | Each half-life halves what's left, so after two half-lives a quarter remains. | Work out <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}" />. |
| Beta decay lowers the proton number. | A neutron turns into a proton, so the proton number rises by <InlineMath math="1" />. | The proton numbers on the right must add up to the left. |
| Heating a sample shortens its half-life. | Half-life is a property of the nucleus and doesn't depend on temperature. | Carbon-14 keeps its half-life of <InlineMath math="5730" /> years. |
| A mass defect in <InlineMath math="\text{u}" /> is multiplied straight by <InlineMath math="c^2" />. | <InlineMath math="E = \Delta m c^2" /> needs kilograms to give joules. | Convert with <InlineMath math="1\text{ u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}" /> first. |
| Control rods slow neutrons down. | Control rods absorb neutrons. The moderator is what slows them down. | Ask whether the neutron disappears or only gets slower. |

The first mistake comes up most often. A half-life **doesn't subtract** a fixed amount. It **halves** what's left every time.

## Practice on Nuclear Physics

These questions move from decay equations to half-life and nuclear energy. **Write the nucleon and proton numbers** in every equation so mistakes are easy to catch.

1. Radium-226, <InlineMath math="{}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}" />, decays by emitting one alpha particle. Write the decay equation.

2. The activity of a radioactive sample falls from <InlineMath math="800" /> to <InlineMath math="100" /> counts per minute in <InlineMath math="15" /> hours. Calculate its half-life.

3. One fission has a mass defect of <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ u}" />. Calculate the energy released in joules.

4. Explain the role of control rods in a nuclear reactor in terms of the value of <InlineMath math="k" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: An alpha particle is <InlineMath math="{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" />, so the nucleon number falls by <InlineMath math="4" /> and the proton number by <InlineMath math="2" />. The element with proton number <InlineMath math="86" /> is radon:

<BlockMath math="{}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" />

Check: nucleon numbers <InlineMath math="222 + 4 = 226" /> and proton numbers <InlineMath math="86 + 2 = 88" />. Both balance.

**Solution 2**: Count how many times the activity halves:

<BlockMath math="800 \rightarrow 400 \rightarrow 200 \rightarrow 100" />

That's three half-lives in <InlineMath math="15" /> hours, so <InlineMath math="T_{1/2} = 15 \div 3 = 5" /> hours. Check: <InlineMath math="800 \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{15/5} = 800 \times \tfrac{1}{8} = 100" />.

**Solution 3**: Convert the mass defect to kilograms first:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Delta m = 0.20 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.32 \times 10^{-28}\text{ kg}" />
<BlockMath math="E = 3.32 \times 10^{-28} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.99 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with MeV: <InlineMath math="0.20 \times 931.5 = 186\text{ MeV}" />, and <InlineMath math="186 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13} = 2.98 \times 10^{-11}\text{ J}" />. The small difference comes from rounding the constants.

**Solution 4**: Each fission releases two or three neutrons. If all of them caused new fissions, <InlineMath math="k" /> would be greater than <InlineMath math="1" /> and the number of fissions would grow exponentially. **Control rods absorb the extra neutrons** so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes the next fission, which means <InlineMath math="k = 1" />. Lowering the rods deeper slows the reaction, and raising them speeds it up.
