## A Gas Changes Its Energy through Heat and Work

**Thermodynamics** studies how the energy inside a gas changes when the gas takes in heat or does work. Its main rule is the **first law of thermodynamics**:

<BlockMath math="\Delta U = Q - W" />

Here <InlineMath math="Q" /> is the heat that enters the gas, <InlineMath math="W" /> the work the gas does, and <InlineMath math="\Delta U" /> the change in internal energy. An ideal gas obeys <InlineMath math="pV = nRT" />, and the work a gas does at constant pressure is <InlineMath math="W = p\,\Delta V" />. A heat engine turns part of the heat into work, and the best possible engine, the **Carnot engine**, has efficiency <InlineMath math="\eta = 1 - \dfrac{T_c}{T_h}" />.

In these notes you change the temperature of a gas and watch its pressure, work out work as the area under a graph, compare isothermal and adiabatic processes, and find the efficiency of a heat engine.

## The Ideal Gas and Kinetic Theory

An ideal gas is a model gas whose particles are tiny and collide only elastically. Its pressure, volume and temperature are linked by the **ideal gas equation**:

<BlockMath math="pV = nRT, \qquad R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}" />

The temperature <InlineMath math="T" /> is always in kelvin. At constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume, so the graph of <InlineMath math="p" /> against <InlineMath math="V" /> is a curve called an **isotherm**. Raise the temperature below: the isotherm moves up, because the particles move faster and hit the walls harder.

## The Work of a Gas Is the Area under the Graph

When a gas expands, it pushes a piston and does **positive work**. When it is compressed, its work is negative. The work equals **the area under the graph** of <InlineMath math="p" /> against <InlineMath math="V" />. At constant pressure, that area is a rectangle:

<BlockMath math="W = p\,(V_2 - V_1)" />

## Four Thermodynamic Processes

The first law holds for every process, but it takes a simple form in four special processes:

| Process | What stays fixed | Work <InlineMath math="W" /> | The first law becomes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Isobaric | pressure | <InlineMath math="p\,\Delta V" /> | <InlineMath math="\Delta U = Q - p\,\Delta V" /> |
| Isochoric | volume | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="\Delta U = Q" /> |
| Isothermal | temperature | <InlineMath math="nRT \ln \dfrac{V_2}{V_1}" /> | <InlineMath math="Q = W" />, because <InlineMath math="\Delta U = 0" /> |
| Adiabatic | no heat | <InlineMath math="-\Delta U" /> | <InlineMath math="\Delta U = -W" /> |

In an **adiabatic** process the gas exchanges no heat, so the work it does comes from its own internal energy and its temperature falls. That is why the adiabatic curve <InlineMath math="pV^\gamma = \text{constant}" /> is steeper than the isotherm <InlineMath math="pV = \text{constant}" />. Change the adiabatic index <InlineMath math="\gamma" /> below. For a monatomic gas <InlineMath math="\gamma = 1.67" />, and for air <InlineMath math="\gamma = 1.4" />.

An isothermal example: <InlineMath math="1" /> mole of gas at <InlineMath math="300\text{ K}" /> expands until its volume doubles.

<BlockMath math="W = nRT \ln \frac{V_2}{V_1} = 1 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times \ln 2 = 1729\text{ J}" />

Because the temperature stays fixed, <InlineMath math="\Delta U = 0" />, so the gas must take in <InlineMath math="Q = 1729\text{ J}" /> of heat, exactly the work it does.

## Heat Engines and the Second Law

A **heat engine** takes in heat <InlineMath math="Q_h" /> from a hot reservoir, turns part of it into work <InlineMath math="W" />, and gives the rest <InlineMath math="Q_c" /> to a cold reservoir. Its efficiency is:

<BlockMath math="\eta = \frac{W}{Q_h} = 1 - \frac{Q_c}{Q_h}" />

The **second law of thermodynamics** says that no engine turns all of its heat into work. The Carnot engine is the ideal engine with the highest efficiency for two given reservoir temperatures, <InlineMath math="\eta = 1 - \dfrac{T_c}{T_h}" />. Change the hot and cold reservoir temperatures below.

For <InlineMath math="T_h = 600\text{ K}" /> and <InlineMath math="T_c = 300\text{ K}" />, a Carnot engine that takes in <InlineMath math="2000\text{ J}" /> does <InlineMath math="W = 0.5 \times 2000 = 1000\text{ J}" /> of work and gives out <InlineMath math="1000\text{ J}" />. A **refrigerator** works the other way. Its performance is measured by the Carnot coefficient of performance <InlineMath math="K = \dfrac{T_c}{T_h - T_c}" />. A fridge at <InlineMath math="273\text{ K}" /> inside a room at <InlineMath math="300\text{ K}" /> has at best <InlineMath math="K = \dfrac{273}{27} = 10.1" />.

## Common Mistakes in Thermodynamics

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Using a Celsius temperature in <InlineMath math="pV = nRT" />. | The ideal gas equation uses absolute temperature. | Add <InlineMath math="273" /> to the Celsius temperature. |
| Taking <InlineMath math="W" /> as positive when a gas is compressed. | The work done by a gas is positive only when its volume grows. | See whether <InlineMath math="\Delta V" /> is positive or negative. |
| Writing <InlineMath math="\Delta U = Q + W" /> with <InlineMath math="W" /> as the work by the gas. | The work a gas does lowers its internal energy. | Use <InlineMath math="\Delta U = Q - W" /> with one sign convention. |
| Thinking adiabatic means constant temperature. | Adiabatic means no heat exchange, and the temperature usually changes. | Isothermal means constant temperature. |
| Using Celsius in the Carnot efficiency. | The ratio <InlineMath math="\dfrac{T_c}{T_h}" /> is only correct in kelvin. | The efficiency never reaches <InlineMath math="100\%" />. |

The first mistake is the most common. **Every temperature in thermodynamics goes into kelvin** before it goes into a formula.

## Thermodynamics Practice

Write the sign convention for heat and work at the start so that **the first law is used correctly**.

1. <InlineMath math="0.5" /> mole of ideal gas is in a <InlineMath math="10\text{ L}" /> container at <InlineMath math="27^\circ\text{C}" />. Work out its pressure.

2. A gas at a constant <InlineMath math="150\text{ kPa}" /> is compressed from <InlineMath math="6\text{ L}" /> to <InlineMath math="2\text{ L}" />. Work out the work of the gas and explain its sign.

3. A gas takes in <InlineMath math="800\text{ J}" /> of heat and does <InlineMath math="300\text{ J}" /> of work. Work out the change in its internal energy.

4. A Carnot engine works between <InlineMath math="527^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="127^\circ\text{C}" />, and takes in <InlineMath math="4000\text{ J}" /> of heat each cycle. Work out its efficiency and its work.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Convert the temperature to kelvin and the volume to cubic metres:

<BlockMath math="p = \frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{0.5 \times 8.314 \times 300}{0.010} = 1.25 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}" />

**Solution 2**: At constant pressure, <InlineMath math="W = p\,\Delta V" />:

<BlockMath math="W = 150 \times (2 - 6) = -600\text{ J}" />

The sign is negative because **the gas is compressed**, so the surroundings do work on the gas.

**Solution 3**: Use the first law:

<BlockMath math="\Delta U = Q - W = 800 - 300 = 500\text{ J}" />

The internal energy rises by <InlineMath math="500\text{ J}" />, so the temperature of the gas rises.

**Solution 4**: Convert the temperatures to kelvin, <InlineMath math="T_h = 800\text{ K}" /> and <InlineMath math="T_c = 400\text{ K}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\eta = 1 - \frac{400}{800} = 0.5 = 50\%" />
<BlockMath math="W = \eta\,Q_h = 0.5 \times 4000 = 2000\text{ J}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: using Celsius would give <InlineMath math="1 - \tfrac{127}{527} = 76\%" />, a value that is too large and wrong.
