## What a Vector Arrow Shows

A vector is commonly drawn as an <Highlight>arrow</Highlight>. Its tail shows the starting point, its tip shows the direction, and its length represents the vector magnitude.

If a force arrow is longer, the force is larger on the same drawing scale. If the arrow points up and to the right, the force acts up and to the right.

> A complete vector notation shows both magnitude and direction.

## Two Point Notation

A vector can be written from a starting point to an ending point. For example, <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> means the vector from point <InlineMath math="A" /> to point <InlineMath math="B" />.

The order of the letters matters. <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> use the same two points, but their directions are opposite.

| Notation | How to read it | Direction meaning |
| :------- | :------------- | :---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> | vector from <InlineMath math="A" /> to <InlineMath math="B" /> | tail at <InlineMath math="A" />, tip at <InlineMath math="B" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> | vector from <InlineMath math="B" /> to <InlineMath math="A" /> | tail at <InlineMath math="B" />, tip at <InlineMath math="A" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\lvert\overrightarrow{AB}\rvert" /> | magnitude of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> | Gives the vector's length. Direction does not affect this value |

<Highlight>The arrow above the letters</Highlight> tells us that the quantity is a vector. Absolute-value bars tell us that only the magnitude is being discussed.

Both arrows below have the same length, but the order of the letters flips the direction: <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> starts at <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> starts at <InlineMath math="B" />. So <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}" />.

## Single Letter Notation

In calculations, vectors are often named with **one letter carrying an arrow** such as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />, <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" />, or <InlineMath math="\vec{F}" />. The letter helps identify the physical quantity.

For example, <InlineMath math="\vec{F}" /> is often used for force, <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> for velocity, and <InlineMath math="\vec{s}" /> for displacement. The magnitude is written without the arrow, such as <InlineMath math="F" /> or <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert" />.

  <BlockMath math="\vec{F}=20\text{ N to the right}" />
  <BlockMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert=20\text{ N}" />
</MathContainer>

The first line still includes direction. The second line states only the force magnitude.

## Zero Vectors and Unit Vectors

When calculating components, you need to distinguish a vector with no length from a vector used to specify direction. **Zero vectors** and **unit vectors** serve these two different roles.

### A Vector with Zero Magnitude

A **zero vector** has magnitude <InlineMath math="0" />. Its tail and tip coincide, so its direction is undefined. In physics, a zero vector can appear when two equal and opposite forces cancel each other.

For example, take right as the positive direction. Forces of <InlineMath math="20\text{ N}" /> to the right and to the left add to:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\vec{F}_1+\vec{F}_2 &= 20\hat{i}\text{ N}-20\hat{i}\text{ N} \\ &= (20-20)\hat{i}\text{ N} \\ &= \vec{0}\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />

On this force table, three strings pull one ring. Turn the third pulley from <InlineMath math="200^\circ" /> toward <InlineMath math="233^\circ" />: the resultant shrinks until it is almost <InlineMath math="0\text{ N}" />, the zero vector, and the ring rests on the pin.

## Common Reading Mistakes

The first mistake is **ignoring the order of the points** and treating <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> as the same vector as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> because they use the same two points. Their directions are opposite.

The second mistake is writing a **negative vector magnitude**. A magnitude is always <InlineMath math="0" /> or positive. A negative sign on a vector usually points the vector opposite to the chosen reference direction. It does not make the length negative.

For example, <InlineMath math="-\vec{a}" /> does not mean the vector has negative magnitude. It means the vector has the same magnitude as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> but points in the opposite direction.
