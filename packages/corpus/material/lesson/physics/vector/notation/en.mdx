## What a Vector Arrow Shows

A vector is drawn as an <Highlight>arrow</Highlight>. Its tail marks the starting point, its tip marks the direction, and its length represents the magnitude on a chosen scale.

If one force arrow is longer than another on the same scale, that force is larger. If the arrow points up and to the right, the force acts up and to the right.

> A complete vector notation shows both magnitude and direction.

Consider wind blowing toward <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> north of east. As wind speed changes from <InlineMath math="8\text{ m/s}" /> upward, **only the arrow length changes** while its direction stays fixed.

## Two Point Notation

A vector can be named from its **starting point** to its **ending point**. For example, <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> means the vector starting at point <InlineMath math="A" /> and ending at point <InlineMath math="B" />.

Letter order always matters. Treating <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> as the same vector is a classic reading mistake: they connect the same two points, yet point in opposite directions.

| Notation | How to read it | Direction meaning |
| :------- | :------------- | :---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> | vector from <InlineMath math="A" /> to <InlineMath math="B" /> | tail at <InlineMath math="A" />, tip at <InlineMath math="B" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}" /> | vector from <InlineMath math="B" /> to <InlineMath math="A" /> | tail at <InlineMath math="B" />, tip at <InlineMath math="A" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\lvert\overrightarrow{AB}\rvert" /> | magnitude of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> | gives the length only without direction |

<Highlight>The arrow above the letters</Highlight> marks the quantity as a vector, whereas absolute-value bars isolate its length. Reversing the letter order reverses the vector:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}" />

## Single Letter Notation

In calculations, vectors are usually named with **one letter carrying an arrow** such as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />, <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" />, or <InlineMath math="\vec{F}" />. Writing <InlineMath math="F" /> or <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert" /> drops the direction and refers only to the magnitude.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{F}=20\text{ N to the right}" />
  <BlockMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert=20\text{ N}" />
</MathContainer>

A vector magnitude is **always zero or positive**, never negative. Writing <InlineMath math="-\vec{a}" /> does not give the vector a negative length: it keeps the exact same magnitude as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> while reversing its direction.

Name a ferry's <InlineMath math="10\text{ km}" /> leg simply <InlineMath math="\vec{s}" />. At bearing <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> clockwise from north, that single symbol packages both the <InlineMath math="8.66\text{ km}" /> east component and the <InlineMath math="5\text{ km}" /> north component into one object.

## Zero Vectors and Unit Vectors

When working with components, you need two special symbols with opposite jobs. A **zero vector** records complete cancellation with length zero, whereas a **unit vector** isolates pure direction with length one.

### A Vector with Zero Magnitude

A **zero vector** <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" /> has magnitude <InlineMath math="0" />. Its tail and tip coincide, so its direction is undefined. Taking right as positive, two opposite <InlineMath math="20\text{ N}" /> forces sum to zero:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\vec{F}_1+\vec{F}_2 &= 20\hat{i}\text{ N}-20\hat{i}\text{ N} \\ &= (20-20)\hat{i}\text{ N} \\ &= \vec{0}\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />

Placed head to tail in true newtons, the second arrow returns straight to the starting point of the first.

  footer="Equal opposite arrows return to the origin, leaving the zero vector."

Because the final tip lands back on the initial tail, the net vector has zero length and no direction.
