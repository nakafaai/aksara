## Equal and Negative Vectors

Two vectors are judged solely by their **magnitude and direction**, never by where their tails sit on a page. Flipping the direction while keeping the exact same length creates the **negative vector** that cancels the original.

> Equal vectors share the same magnitude and direction regardless of where they are drawn.

### Equal Vectors Do Not Depend on Location

Two vectors are equal when they share the <Highlight>same magnitude and direction</Highlight>. Their starting positions in a diagram may differ. This equality compares the vectors themselves, whereas a physical force applied at a different point on a rigid body can still change its torque.

### Negative Vectors Reverse Direction

The <Highlight>negative vector</Highlight> of <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> is written <InlineMath math="-\vec{a}" />. It preserves the magnitude of <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> while reversing its direction:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\lvert-\vec{a}\rvert=\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a}+(-\vec{a})=\vec{0}" />
</MathContainer>

A <InlineMath math="12\text{ N}" /> pull to the right and a <InlineMath math="12\text{ N}" /> pull to the left along the same line form an opposite pair whose sum is the zero vector.

On the park map below, running from <InlineMath math="A" /> to <InlineMath math="B" /> covers <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" /> east and <InlineMath math="80\text{ m}" /> north. ## Scalar Multiplication Changes Magnitude and Direction

A scalar is an ordinary number without direction. Multiplying a vector by a scalar <InlineMath math="k" /> scales its length by <InlineMath math="\lvert k\rvert" />, and a **negative scalar reverses direction** without ever making length negative.

| Form | Result magnitude | Result direction |
| :--- | :--------------- | :--------------- |
| <InlineMath math="2\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | same as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | same as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-3\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="3\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | opposite to <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | becomes the zero vector |

A negative scalar <Highlight>does not make the magnitude negative</Highlight>. It flips the arrow along the same line.

## The Zero Vector as Cancellation

The zero vector <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" /> has magnitude <InlineMath math="0" /> and an <Highlight>undefined direction</Highlight> because its tail and tip coincide. It appears whenever balanced vectors cancel each other:

<BlockMath math="\vec{F}_1+\vec{F}_2=\vec{0}" />

In mechanical equilibrium, an object stays at rest even while several nonzero forces act on it, because their vector sum closes back to zero. Three equal <InlineMath math="1.96\text{ N}" /> pulls spaced <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> apart show this exact symmetry.
