## Equal Vectors Do Not Depend on Location

Two vectors are equal if they have the <Highlight>same magnitude and direction</Highlight>. Their positions in a diagram may be different. This equality compares the vectors themselves. For a force, its point of application can still change the physical turning effect even when the force vector is unchanged.

In the visual, <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> appear at different positions. They are still equal because both have the same magnitude and direction.

This freedom to slide an abstract vector in a diagram does not mean every physical application point is interchangeable. A force applied at a different point may produce a different torque.

## A Negative Vector Reverses Direction

The <Highlight>negative vector</Highlight> of <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> is written as <InlineMath math="-\vec{a}" />. It has the same magnitude as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />, but points in the opposite direction.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\lvert-\vec{a}\rvert=\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a}+(-\vec{a})=\vec{0}" />
</MathContainer>

For example, a <InlineMath math="12\text{ N}" /> force to the right and a <InlineMath math="12\text{ N}" /> force to the left form an opposite-vector pair. If both act on the same object along the same line, their resultant is the zero vector.

## Scalar Multiplication Changes Magnitude and Direction

A scalar is an ordinary number with no direction. When a vector is multiplied by a scalar <InlineMath math="k" />, the vector length changes by a factor of <InlineMath math="\lvert k\rvert" />.

| Form | Result magnitude | Result direction |
| :--- | :--------------- | :--------------- |
| <InlineMath math="2\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | same as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | same as <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-3\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="3\lvert\vec{a}\rvert" /> | opposite to <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0\vec{a}" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | becomes the zero vector |

A negative scalar <Highlight>does not make the magnitude negative</Highlight>. It reverses the vector direction.

## The Zero Vector as Cancellation

The zero vector is written as <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" />. Its magnitude is <InlineMath math="0" />, and its <Highlight>direction is undefined</Highlight> because the tail and tip coincide.

The zero vector often appears when vectors cancel each other. For example, two students may pull a box with equal forces in opposite directions. If the forces are exactly along the same line, their combination does not produce a net push to the right or to the left.

<BlockMath math="\vec{F}_1+\vec{F}_2=\vec{0}" />

In equilibrium, an object can remain at rest while several forces act on it because their resultant is zero.

Multiplying by <InlineMath math="k" /> scales the length to <InlineMath math="\lvert k\rvert\,\lvert\vec a\rvert" />, and the sign of <InlineMath math="k" /> decides the direction. The V shape is the length for a unit vector: it grows on both sides, but only the right side keeps the direction. Zoom in near <InlineMath math="k=0" />.
