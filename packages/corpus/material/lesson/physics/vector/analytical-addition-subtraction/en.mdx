## Calculating a Resultant with Components

The graphical method shows the resultant direction, but its numerical accuracy depends on the drawing. The analytical method calculates components and therefore <Highlight>gives a reproducible numerical result</Highlight>.

The method is to resolve every vector into [vector components](/en/subjects/physics/vector/component) along the <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> axes, add the components along each axis, and then rebuild the resultant.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_x=\sum F_x" />
  <BlockMath math="R_y=\sum F_y" />
</MathContainer>

> In the analytical method, add the vector components on each axis. The arrow lengths alone do not give the sum.

## Steps for Vector Addition

Suppose several forces <InlineMath math="\vec{F}_1" />, <InlineMath math="\vec{F}_2" />, and <InlineMath math="\vec{F}_3" /> act on an object. Each force is first split into **horizontal and vertical components**.

After all components are known, the resultant components are:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_x=F_{1x}+F_{2x}+F_{3x}+\cdots" />
  <BlockMath math="R_y=F_{1y}+F_{2y}+F_{3y}+\cdots" />
</MathContainer>

The resultant magnitude is:

<BlockMath math="R=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}" />

Use the two-argument arctangent for the direction of the resultant relative to the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis:

<BlockMath math="\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(R_y,R_x)" />

Unlike <InlineMath math="\tan^{-1}(R_y/R_x)" />, <InlineMath math="\operatorname{atan2}" /> uses the signs of both components to place the direction in the correct quadrant.

If both components are zero, the resultant is the zero vector and its **direction is undefined**. Do not assign a physical direction to a calculator or program output for <InlineMath math="\operatorname{atan2}(0,0)" />.

### Two Stage Displacement

A person walks in two stages. The first stage is equivalent to <InlineMath math="80\text{ m}" /> east and <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" /> north. The second stage is equivalent to <InlineMath math="120\text{ m}" /> east and <InlineMath math="90\text{ m}" /> north.

| Displacement vector | <InlineMath math="x" /> component | <InlineMath math="y" /> component |
| :------------------ | :-------------------------------- | :-------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\vec{s}_1" /> | <InlineMath math="80\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\vec{s}_2" /> | <InlineMath math="120\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="90\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" /> | <InlineMath math="200\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="150\text{ m}" /> |

Add **components along the same axis** to obtain the resultant components:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_x=80+120=200\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R_y=60+90=150\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

The total displacement magnitude is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
R&=\sqrt{200^2+150^2} \\
&=\sqrt{40\,000+22\,500} \\
&=\sqrt{62\,500} \\
&=250\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

The resultant direction is:

<BlockMath math="\tan\theta=\frac{150}{200}=0.75\quad\Rightarrow\quad\theta=\tan^{-1}(0.75)\approx36.9^\circ" />

The total displacement is <InlineMath math="250\text{ m}" /> at <InlineMath math="36.9^\circ" /> north of east.

## Subtraction with Components

Subtract vectors **component by component along the same axes**. If <InlineMath math="\vec{C}=\vec{A}-\vec{B}" />, then:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="C_x=A_x-B_x" />
  <BlockMath math="C_y=A_y-B_y" />
</MathContainer>

For example, let <InlineMath math="\vec{A}=7\hat{i}+4\hat{j}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}=2\hat{i}+6\hat{j}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\vec{C}&=(7-2)\hat{i}+(4-6)\hat{j} \\
&=5\hat{i}-2\hat{j}
\end{aligned}" />

The negative sign in <InlineMath math="-2\hat{j}" /> means the resulting <InlineMath math="y" /> component points downward.

The component signs give a rightward and downward direction. To find the arrow length, add the squared components. Squaring removes the negative sign.

<BlockMath math="\lvert\vec{C}\rvert=\sqrt{5^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt{25+4}=\sqrt{29}" />


So subtraction does not need a new rule. Keep the same axes, subtract matching components, then read the signs to recover the direction of the result.
