## Two Kinds of Vector Multiplication

Vector multiplication does not always produce another vector. Two kinds of multiplication appear throughout physics: the **dot product** and the **cross product**.

The dot product produces a scalar. The cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both original vectors, and when the result is nonzero its direction follows the **right-hand rule**.

| Type of multiplication | Symbol | Result | Physics example |
| :--------------------- | :----- | :----- | :-------------- |
| dot product | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}" /> | scalar | work |
| cross product | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\times\vec{B}" /> | vector | torque |

> The multiplication sign changes whenever the physical question shifts from alignment to turning effect.

## Dot Product Measures the Same Direction Part

The **dot product** of <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> projects one vector onto the other and returns a scalar value without a direction.

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}=AB\cos\theta" />

For two nonzero vectors, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the angle between them, while <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are their magnitudes. For a **constant force** acting throughout a displacement, the work done equals the force component along the displacement times the distance moved:

<BlockMath math="W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{s}=Fs\cos\theta" />

This component is positive when the force helps the motion and negative when it opposes the motion.

### Work Done by an Angled Pull

A person pulls a box with a constant <InlineMath math="40\text{ N}" /> force while the box moves <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" />. The force makes a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> angle with the displacement direction, so the work is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
W&=Fs\cos\theta \\
&=40(5)\cos60^\circ \\
&=200(0.5) \\
&=100\text{ J}
\end{aligned}" />

If the force stands perpendicular to the displacement, <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos90^\circ=0" />, so the force does zero work along the path. If the same force opposes the displacement at <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, **negative work** removes energy from the motion:

<BlockMath math="W=40(5)\cos180^\circ=200(-1)=-200\text{ J}" />

## Cross Product Measures Turning Effect

The **cross product** of <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> returns a vector perpendicular to the plane containing both inputs, with magnitude:

<BlockMath math="\lvert\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\rvert=AB\sin\theta" />

For two nonzero, nonparallel vectors, its direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl your fingers from the first vector toward the second through the smaller angle, and your thumb points along the result. Parallel vectors or a zero input produce the zero vector with no defined direction.

Here <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> points along positive <InlineMath math="x" /> with length <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> points along positive <InlineMath math="y" /> with length <InlineMath math="3" />. Both vectors are dimensionless, and their cross product points along positive <InlineMath math="z" /> with length <InlineMath math="12" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
C_x&=0\cdot0-0\cdot3=0 \\
C_y&=0\cdot0-4\cdot0=0 \\
C_z&=4\cdot3-0\cdot0=12 \\
\vec{A}\times\vec{B}&=(C_x,C_y,C_z)=(0,0,12)
\end{aligned}" />

In rotational mechanics, **torque** uses this exact cross product:

<BlockMath math="\vec{\tau}=\vec{r}\times\vec{F}" />

Here <InlineMath math="\vec{r}" /> is the position vector from a pivot point to the force application point, and the perpendicular lever arm has length <InlineMath math="r\sin\theta" />.

### Pushing a Door Open

Choose a reference point on the hinge at the height of the push. A door is pushed horizontally with <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" /> at a distance of <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> from the hinge, **perpendicular to the door** so <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\tau&=rF\sin\theta \\
&=0.8(30)\sin90^\circ \\
&=24\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}
\end{aligned}" />

For fixed <InlineMath math="r" /> and <InlineMath math="F" />, a perpendicular push maximizes torque because <InlineMath math="\sin90^\circ=1" />. Sliding your hand closer to the hinge or slanting the push lowers both torque and door swing.

## Comparing Dot and Cross Products

Compare both operations with **both vector magnitudes fixed and nonzero**. The dot product peaks when vectors align because <InlineMath math="\cos0^\circ=1" />, whereas the cross product vanishes there because <InlineMath math="\sin0^\circ=0" />.

| Angle condition | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}" /> | <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\rvert" /> |
| :-------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> | maximum | zero |
| <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> | zero | maximum |
| <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | negative | zero |
