## Two Kinds of Vector Multiplication

Vector multiplication does not always produce another vector. Two kinds of multiplication are common in physics: the **dot product** and the **cross product**.

The dot product produces a scalar. The cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both original vectors. When the result is nonzero, its direction follows the **right-hand rule**.

| Type of multiplication | Symbol | Result | Physics example |
| :--------------------- | :----- | :----- | :-------------- |
| dot product | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}" /> | scalar | work |
| cross product | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\times\vec{B}" /> | vector | torque |

> The multiplication sign changes because the physical question changes: how much is in the same direction, or how strong is the turning effect.

## Dot Product Measures the Same Direction Part

The dot product of <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> is defined as:

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}=AB\cos\theta" />

For two nonzero vectors, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the angle between them, while <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are their magnitudes. The result is a scalar, so it has a value without a direction.

For a **constant force** throughout the displacement, the work done by that force is:

<BlockMath math="W=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{s}=Fs\cos\theta" />

The force component **along the displacement axis** determines the work. This component is positive with the motion and negative against the motion.

### Work from Dot Product

A person pulls a box with a constant <InlineMath math="40\text{ N}" /> force while the box moves <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" />. The force makes a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> angle with the displacement direction. The work is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
W&=Fs\cos\theta \\
&=40(5)\cos60^\circ \\
&=200(0.5) \\
&=100\text{ J}
\end{aligned}" />

If the force is perpendicular to the displacement, <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos90^\circ=0" />. In that case, the force does no work in the displacement direction.

If the same constant force opposes the displacement, the angle is <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. **Negative work** means the force removes energy from the motion:

<BlockMath math="W=40(5)\cos180^\circ=200(-1)=-200\text{ J}" />

## Cross Product Measures Turning Effect

The cross product of <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> has magnitude:

<BlockMath math="\lvert\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\rvert=AB\sin\theta" />

For two nonzero, nonparallel vectors, the result is a vector perpendicular to the plane containing <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />. Its direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl your fingers from the first vector toward the second through the smaller angle. Your thumb gives the resulting direction. Parallel vectors or a zero input vector give the zero vector, which has no defined direction.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
C_x&=0\cdot0-0\cdot3=0 \\
C_y&=0\cdot0-4\cdot0=0 \\

For torque, the cross product is written:

<BlockMath math="\vec{\tau}=\vec{r}\times\vec{F}" />

Vector <InlineMath math="\vec{r}" /> is the position vector from a reference point on the hinge to the point where the force acts. The perpendicular lever arm has length <InlineMath math="r\sin\theta" />. At the same angle, the same force produces a larger torque when applied farther from a door hinge.

### Torque from Perpendicular Force

Choose a reference point on the hinge at the height of the push. A door is pushed horizontally with a <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" /> force at a distance of <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> from the hinge. The force is **perpendicular to the door**, so <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\tau&=rF\sin\theta \\
&=0.8(30)\sin90^\circ \\
&=24\text{ N m}
\end{aligned}" />

For fixed <InlineMath math="r" /> and <InlineMath math="F" />, a perpendicular force gives the maximum torque because <InlineMath math="\sin90^\circ=1" />. A force almost parallel to the position vector <InlineMath math="\vec{r}" /> gives little torque because its sine factor is close to zero.

## Comparing Dot and Cross Products

Compare the operations with **both vector magnitudes fixed and nonzero**. The dot product is largest when two vectors point the same way because <InlineMath math="\cos0^\circ=1" />. The cross product is zero in that same condition because <InlineMath math="\sin0^\circ=0" />.

| Angle condition | <InlineMath math="\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}" /> | <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\rvert" /> |
| :-------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> | maximum | zero |
| <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> | zero | maximum |
| <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | negative | zero |

So the dot product is useful for asking about alignment, while the cross product is useful for perpendicular effects such as rotation.

Keep both magnitudes fixed and turn the angle. The orange dot product is largest when the vectors line up, while the violet cross product magnitude peaks at <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. At <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> the cross product vanishes, because parallel vectors give no turning effect.
