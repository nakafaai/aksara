## Vectors Have Magnitude and Direction

Imagine telling a friend, "walk <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" />." Your friend will probably ask, "which way?" Forward, right, or up the stairs?

In physics, some quantities cannot be explained by a number alone. They need both **magnitude** and **direction**. This kind of quantity is called a **vector**.

<BlockMath math="\text{vector} = \text{magnitude} + \text{direction}" />

Examples include a displacement of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" /> east, a force of <InlineMath math="20 \text{ N}" /> upward, or a velocity of <InlineMath math="12 \text{ m s}^{-1}" /> to the right. If the direction is missing, the physical information is incomplete.

## Vectors and Scalars

Not every quantity needs direction. A **scalar** is described well enough by a value and a unit. A **vector** needs a value, a unit, and a direction.

| Situation | Quantity | Type |
| :-------- | :------- | :--- |
| Water with mass <InlineMath math="2 \text{ kg}" /> | mass | scalar |
| Room temperature <InlineMath math="27^\circ\text{C}" /> | temperature | scalar |
| A car travels a distance of <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km}" /> | distance | scalar |
| A car is displaced <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km}" /> north | displacement | vector |
| A student pushes a table with <InlineMath math="30 \text{ N}" /> to the right | force | vector |
| Wind moves at <InlineMath math="6 \text{ m s}^{-1}" /> west | velocity | vector |

A quick way to decide is this: if the question "which way?" matters for understanding the quantity, you are probably looking at a vector.

## Reading a Vector Arrow

Vectors are often drawn as arrows. The tail shows where the vector starts, the head shows the direction, and the length represents the vector magnitude.

For example, the magnitude of a force directed to the right can be written as <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert = 20 \text{ N}" />. The symbol <InlineMath math="\vec{F}" /> denotes the force vector, <InlineMath math="20 \text{ N}" /> is its magnitude, and "to the right" is its direction.

A vector can also be written from one point to another, such as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" />. This means the vector starts at point <InlineMath math="A" /> and ends at point <InlineMath math="B" />.

<BlockMath math="\lvert\overrightarrow{AB}\rvert = \text{the vector length from } A \text{ to } B" />

The absolute value bars around a vector denote its magnitude. Direction is not part of that value.

## Vector Equality Depends on Magnitude and Direction

Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction. Their locations may be different.

Two forces of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> directed to the right have equal vector values, even when the arrows are drawn at different locations. The point where a force acts affects the torque on a rigid body. Moving that point can change the torque even when the magnitude and direction stay the same.

By contrast, a force of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> to the right and a force of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> to the left are not equal vectors. Their magnitudes are equal, but their directions are opposite.

## Distance and Displacement Measure Different Quantities

A person walks <InlineMath math="4 \text{ m}" /> east, then <InlineMath math="3 \text{ m}" /> north. The distance traveled is the total path length:

<BlockMath math="4 \text{ m} + 3 \text{ m} = 7 \text{ m}" />

Displacement is the vector from the starting position directly to the final position, so it differs from the total distance traveled.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\lvert\Delta\vec{r}\rvert &= \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} \\
&= 5 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

The distance is <InlineMath math="7 \text{ m}" />. The displacement has magnitude <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" /> and points about <InlineMath math="36.9^\circ" /> north of east.
