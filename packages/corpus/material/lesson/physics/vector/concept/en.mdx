## Vectors Have Magnitude and Direction

Imagine telling a friend, "walk <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" />." Your friend will probably ask, "which way?" Forward, right, or up the stairs?

In physics, some quantities cannot be explained by a number alone. They need both **magnitude** and **direction**. This kind of quantity is called a **vector**.

Magnitude and direction are two pieces of information describing a vector. They are not numbers to add. For example, a force magnitude is measured in newtons, while its direction is specified relative to reference axes.

Examples include a displacement of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" /> east, a force of <InlineMath math="20 \text{ N}" /> upward, or a velocity of <InlineMath math="12 \text{ m s}^{-1}" /> to the right. If the direction is missing, the physical information is incomplete.

> Every vector carries two inseparable facts, how large it is and which way it points. Drop the direction and the physical prediction breaks.

### Reading a Vector Arrow

Vectors are often drawn as arrows. The **tail** shows where the vector starts, the **head** shows the direction, and the **length** represents the vector magnitude.

For example, the magnitude of a force directed to the right can be written as <InlineMath math="\lvert\vec{F}\rvert = 20 \text{ N}" />. The symbol <InlineMath math="\vec{F}" /> denotes the force vector, <InlineMath math="20 \text{ N}" /> is its magnitude, and "to the right" is its direction.

A vector can also be written from one point to another, such as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" />. This means the vector starts at point <InlineMath math="A" /> and ends at point <InlineMath math="B" />. The notation <InlineMath math="\lvert\overrightarrow{AB}\rvert" /> gives only the length from <InlineMath math="A" /> to <InlineMath math="B" />, without its direction.

Consider a drone that travels <InlineMath math="6\text{ m}" /> east and <InlineMath math="8\text{ m}" /> north, putting it <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> across the ground from its pad, and also climbs to height <InlineMath math="h" />. At <InlineMath math="h = 4\text{ m}" />, its straight arrow from the pad to the drone is <InlineMath math="10.8\text{ m}" /> long even though the flown path is <InlineMath math="18\text{ m}" />.

## Vectors and Scalars

Not every quantity needs direction. A **scalar** is described completely by a value and a unit, whereas a **vector** needs a value, a unit, and a direction to make physical sense.

### Comparing Speed and Velocity

The table below sorts everyday quantities into **scalars and vectors** based on whether direction changes the physical meaning.

| Situation | Quantity | Type |
| :-------- | :------- | :--- |
| Water with mass <InlineMath math="2 \text{ kg}" /> | mass | scalar |
| Room temperature <InlineMath math="27^\circ\text{C}" /> | temperature | scalar |
| A car travels a distance of <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km}" /> | distance | scalar |
| A car is displaced <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km}" /> north | displacement | vector |
| A student pushes a table with <InlineMath math="30 \text{ N}" /> to the right | force | vector |
| Wind moves at <InlineMath math="6 \text{ m s}^{-1}" /> west | velocity | vector |

A quick way to decide is this: if the question "which way?" matters for understanding the quantity, you are probably looking at a vector.

## Vector Equality Depends on Magnitude and Direction

Two vectors match completely only when they share <Highlight>both magnitude and direction</Highlight>, regardless of where they are drawn on a page.

Two forces of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> directed to the right have equal vector values, even when the arrows are drawn at different locations. The point where a force acts affects the torque on a rigid body, so moving that point can change rotation even when the force vector stays the same.

By contrast, a force of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> to the right and a force of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ N}" /> to the left are not equal vectors. Their magnitudes match, yet their directions oppose each other.

Picture two tug-of-war teams pulling opposite ways on one rope, with each puller exerting <InlineMath math="400\text{ N}" />. The left team keeps <InlineMath math="3" /> pullers (<InlineMath math="-1200\text{ N}" />) while the right team has <InlineMath math="p" /> pullers. At <InlineMath math="p = 3" />, both forces have size <InlineMath math="1200\text{ N}" /> yet cancel to zero because they point opposite ways.

## Distance and Displacement Measure Different Quantities

Following a bent path adds every step into scalar **distance**, while vector **displacement** draws one straight arrow from start to finish. Comparing both side by side keeps the two quantities clear.

### Right Angle Path and Displacement

A person walks <InlineMath math="4 \text{ m}" /> east, then <InlineMath math="3 \text{ m}" /> north. The distance traveled is the **total path length**:

<BlockMath math="4 \text{ m} + 3 \text{ m} = 7 \text{ m}" />

Displacement is the vector **from the starting position to the final position**, so it differs from the total distance traveled.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\lvert\Delta\vec{r}\rvert &= \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2}\text{ m} \\ &= \sqrt{16+9}\text{ m} \\ &= \sqrt{25}\text{ m} \\
&= 5 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

For direction, measure the angle from east toward north. The ratio of opposite to adjacent sides is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\tan\theta &= \frac34 \\ \theta &= \arctan\left(\frac34\right) \\ &\approx 36.9^\circ\end{aligned}" />

The distance is <InlineMath math="7 \text{ m}" />. The displacement has magnitude <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" /> and points about <InlineMath math="36.9^\circ" /> north of east.
