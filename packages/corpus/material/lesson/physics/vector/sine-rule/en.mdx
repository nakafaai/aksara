## Resultant Direction Requires Another Angle

The cosine rule gives the resultant magnitude, but it does not complete the answer. In physics problems, a magnitude such as <InlineMath math="15.6\text{ N}" /> stays incomplete until the direction is stated.

For two vectors, the resultant direction follows from the **sine rule** on the triangle formed by both vectors and their resultant:

<BlockMath math="\frac{\sin\phi}{B}=\frac{\sin\theta}{R}" />

Here <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the included angle between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />, <InlineMath math="R" /> is the resultant magnitude, and <InlineMath math="\phi" /> is the angle between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />. Use this triangle for **two nonzero, noncollinear vectors** whose included angle lies strictly between <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. A zero resultant has no direction to calculate.

> The sine rule is usually used after the resultant magnitude is known.

### Choosing the Reference Vector

Before calculating, decide the **reference direction**. When a question asks for direction relative to <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />, measure from <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />. When it asks relative to <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />, inspect the triangle so you do not swap opposite sides.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sin\phi=\frac{B\sin\theta}{R}" />
  <BlockMath math="\phi_0=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{B\sin\theta}{R}\right)" />
</MathContainer>

The value <InlineMath math="\phi_0" /> is an **acute or right-angle candidate**. Check the resultant components to choose between <InlineMath math="\phi_0" /> and its supplement <InlineMath math="180^\circ-\phi_0" />. Because side <InlineMath math="B" /> lies opposite angle <InlineMath math="\phi" />, <InlineMath math="B" /> appears in the numerator.

Pilots use the exact same triangle for **crosswind drift**. For an airspeed of <InlineMath math="400\text{ km/h}" /> at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> east of north and an eastward wind of <InlineMath math="300\text{ km/h}" />, the included angle between airspeed and wind is <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />, giving ground speed <InlineMath math="608.3\text{ km/h}" /> and drift angle:

<BlockMath math="\sin\delta=\frac{300\sin60^\circ}{\sqrt{370\,000}}\approx0.4271 \implies \delta\approx25.3^\circ" />

## Worked Force and Tow Directions

Once the cosine rule yields an **unrounded resultant magnitude**, substitute that exact radical into the sine rule to avoid rounding error in the angle. Both the force diagram and the harbour tow below share the same triangle geometry.

### Two Forces Acting at One Ring

Suppose a ring experiences pulls <InlineMath math="A=10\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="B=8\text{ N}" /> with an included angle of <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. From the cosine rule, the resultant has magnitude <InlineMath math="\sqrt{244}\text{ N}" />, or about <InlineMath math="15.6\text{ N}" />.

Keep the **unrounded radical** when finding angle <InlineMath math="\phi" /> between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\sin\phi&=\frac{B\sin\theta}{R} \\
&=\frac{8\sin60^\circ}{\sqrt{244}} \\
&=\frac{4\sqrt3}{\sqrt{244}} \\
&\approx0.4435
\end{aligned}" />

Taking the inverse sine gives:

<BlockMath math="\phi=\sin^{-1}(0.4435)\approx26.3^\circ" />

So the resultant force is about <InlineMath math="15.6\text{ N}" /> directed <InlineMath math="26.3^\circ" /> from <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />.

## Checking Direction with Components

The sine rule gives an angle magnitude quickly, yet **component signs** provide an independent check on which quadrant holds the resultant. Checking horizontal and vertical sums settles every acute versus obtuse ambiguity before you write a final direction.

### Verifying an Acute Direction

Placing <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> along the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis gives the **resultant components**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_x=A+B\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="R_y=B\sin\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="\tan\phi=\frac{R_y}{R_x}" />
</MathContainer>

For the <InlineMath math="10\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" /> example:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \tan\phi &=\frac{8\sin60^\circ}{10+8\cos60^\circ} \\ &=\frac{4\sqrt3}{14} \\ &\approx0.495 \end{aligned}" />

This yields <InlineMath math="\phi\approx26.3^\circ" />, confirming the sine rule result. Placing the two forces head to tail shows both the included angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> and resultant angle <InlineMath math="\phi" /> in one triangle.

### Resolving Obtuse Angles

Because supplementary angles share the same sine, inverse sine alone can return an acute angle even when the triangle demands an obtuse angle:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sin\phi=\sin(180^\circ-\phi)" />
  <BlockMath math="\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(R_y,R_x)" />
</MathContainer>

Test this with <InlineMath math="A=1\text{ N}" />, <InlineMath math="B=\sqrt3\text{ N}" />, and <InlineMath math="\theta=150^\circ" />. Place the first force along the positive horizontal axis and compute both components first:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_x &= A+B\cos150^\circ \\ &= 1+\sqrt3\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \\ &= -\frac12\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_y &= B\sin150^\circ \\ &= \sqrt3\cdot\frac12 \\ &= \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The horizontal component is **negative** while the vertical component is **positive**, placing the resultant in the second quadrant.

## Component Sums for Three or More Vectors

When more than two vectors act together, the **component method** places every vector on shared <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> axes and sums their components directly. That avoids chaining several triangles and guessing which interior angle fits each sine-rule step.

Use the sine rule when the setup is clean: two vectors, a known included angle, a known resultant magnitude, and a direction measured from one vector. Always <Highlight>resolve the inverse-sine ambiguity</Highlight> before stating the final direction.

Consider a three-leg voyage: <InlineMath math="6\text{ km}" /> east, <InlineMath math="4\text{ km}" /> north, and <InlineMath math="5\text{ km}" /> on an adjustable bearing. At a <InlineMath math="315^\circ" /> bearing, the sums give <InlineMath math="2.46\text{ km}" /> east and <InlineMath math="7.54\text{ km}" /> north, placing the ferry <InlineMath math="7.93\text{ km}" /> from harbour on bearing <InlineMath math="18.1^\circ" />.
