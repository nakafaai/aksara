## Resultant Direction Requires Another Angle

The cosine rule gives the resultant magnitude, but it does not fully give the direction. In vector problems, an answer such as <InlineMath math="15.6\text{ N}" /> is incomplete if the force direction is not stated.

For two vectors, the resultant direction can be found using the **sine rule**. This rule works on the resultant triangle formed by the two vectors and their resultant.

<BlockMath math="\frac{\sin\phi}{B}=\frac{\sin\theta}{R}" />

Here, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the included angle between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />, <InlineMath math="R" /> is the resultant magnitude, and <InlineMath math="\phi" /> is the angle between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and resultant <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />.


Use this triangle for **two nonzero vectors that are not collinear**, with <InlineMath math="0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ" />. A zero resultant has no direction to calculate.

> The sine rule is usually used after the resultant magnitude is known.

## Reading the Angle Being Asked For

Before calculating, decide the **reference direction**. If the question asks for the resultant direction relative to <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />, the required angle is from <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />.

If the question asks for direction relative to <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />, the angle is different. Do not swap angles without checking the vector triangle.

A common relationship is:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sin\phi=\frac{B\sin\theta}{R}" />
  <BlockMath math="\phi_0=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{B\sin\theta}{R}\right)" />
</MathContainer>

The value <InlineMath math="\phi_0" /> is an **acute or right-angle candidate**. Check the resultant components to choose between <InlineMath math="\phi=\phi_0" /> and <InlineMath math="\phi=180^\circ-\phi_0" />.

This formula finds the resultant angle relative to vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />. Because the side opposite angle <InlineMath math="\phi" /> has magnitude <InlineMath math="B" />, <InlineMath math="B" /> appears in the numerator.

## Resultant Force Direction

Two forces act at one point. <InlineMath math="A=10\text{ N}" />, <InlineMath math="B=8\text{ N}" />, and the included angle is <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. From the cosine rule, the resultant magnitude is <InlineMath math="R\approx15.6\text{ N}" />.

Use the **unrounded resultant magnitude** to find angle <InlineMath math="\phi" /> between <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{R}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\sin\phi&=\frac{B\sin\theta}{R} \\
&=\frac{8\sin60^\circ}{\sqrt{244}} \\
&=\frac{4\sqrt3}{\sqrt{244}} \\
&\approx0.4435
\end{aligned}" />

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\phi=\sin^{-1}(0.4435)\approx26.3^\circ" />

So the resultant force is about <InlineMath math="15.6\text{ N}" /> at <InlineMath math="26.3^\circ" /> from <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />.

## Checking with Components

The same direction can be checked using **resultant components**. If <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> is placed on the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis, then:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_x=A+B\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="R_y=B\sin\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="\tan\phi=\frac{R_y}{R_x}" />
</MathContainer>

For the same example:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \tan\phi &amp;=\frac{8\sin60^\circ}{10+8\cos60^\circ} \\ &amp;=\frac{4\sqrt3}{14} \\ &amp;\approx0.495 \end{aligned}" />

This gives <InlineMath math="\phi\approx26.3^\circ" />, matching the sine rule. This check is useful when you are unsure which angle is being found.

Because <InlineMath math="\sin\phi=\sin(180^\circ-\phi)" />, inverse sine alone can return an acute angle even when the triangle requires its obtuse supplement. Use the vector geometry or the component form <InlineMath math="\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(R_y,R_x)" /> to choose the correct quadrant.

### Choosing an Obtuse Angle

Let <InlineMath math="A=1\text{ N}" />, <InlineMath math="B=\sqrt3\text{ N}" />, and <InlineMath math="\theta=150^\circ" />. Place the first force along the positive horizontal axis. Calculate the components before choosing an angle:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_x &= A+B\cos150^\circ \\ &= 1+\sqrt3\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \\ &= -\frac12\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_y &= B\sin150^\circ \\ &= \sqrt3\cdot\frac12 \\ &= \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\text{ N}\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The horizontal component is **negative** and the vertical component is **positive**. The resultant lies in the second quadrant.

## Component Methods for More than Two Vectors

When there are more than two vectors, the component method puts every vector on the same <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> axes and then adds the components. This avoids constructing several triangles and deciding which triangle angle belongs in each sine-rule equation. The same component method also fits directions already measured from the coordinate axes.

Use the sine rule when the problem is clear: two vectors, a known included angle, a known resultant magnitude, and a requested resultant direction relative to one of the vectors. Always <Highlight>resolve the inverse-sine ambiguity</Highlight> before stating the direction.
