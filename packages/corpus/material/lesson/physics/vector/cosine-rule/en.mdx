## When Two Vectors Form an Angle

For two vectors pointing in the same direction, add their magnitudes. For opposite directions, take the absolute difference of their magnitudes. Many forces and displacements, however, <Highlight>form an angle with each other</Highlight>. In that situation, the resultant magnitude can be found using the **cosine rule**.

This rule is used when the magnitudes of two vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> are known, and the included angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> between them is known.

<BlockMath math="R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta}" />

> Angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> in the cosine rule is the angle between the two vectors when their tails are placed together.

Pulls of <InlineMath math="2.94\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="3.92\text{ N}" /> with <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> between them give a resultant of <InlineMath math="5.96\text{ N}" />, **less than their plain sum** of <InlineMath math="6.86\text{ N}" />. Open the angle and watch the resultant shrink.

## The Cosine in the Triangle Formula

The cosine rule appears because the resultant of two vectors forms a triangle with two known sides. When vectors are arranged **head to tail**, the interior triangle angle is supplementary to the tail-to-tail angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />, turning the triangle's minus sign into a plus sign.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R^2 &= A^2+B^2-2AB\cos(180^\circ-\theta) \\ &= A^2+B^2-2AB(-\cos\theta) \\ &= A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta\end{aligned}" />

Always check three details before pressing equals: measure <InlineMath math="\theta" /> with both tails together rather than inside the head-to-tail triangle, keep the **plus sign** in front of <InlineMath math="2AB\cos\theta" />, and confirm your calculator sits in degree mode (check that <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ = 0.5" />) before taking the final square root.

The table below shows how <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> changes with the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />:

| Included angle | Value of <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> | Physical meaning |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :--------------- |

When <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" />, the cosine rule becomes the Pythagorean form because <InlineMath math="\cos90^\circ=0" />.

## Finding the Included Angle from the Resultant

Two forces of <InlineMath math="5\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="3\text{ N}" /> give a resultant of <InlineMath math="7\text{ N}" />. Find the angle between the two forces.

The cosine rule also works backwards to find the angle. **Square the resultant**, then solve for <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="7^2 = 5^2 + 3^2 + 2(5)(3)\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="49 = 34 + 30\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="\cos\theta = \frac{15}{30} = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = 60^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Substitute <InlineMath math="\theta = 60^\circ" /> back in to verify the magnitude:

<BlockMath math="R = \sqrt{25 + 9 + 30\cdot\frac12} = \sqrt{49} = 7\text{ N}" />

The check passes and <InlineMath math="7\text{ N}" /> sits neatly between the lower bound <InlineMath math="2\text{ N}" /> and the upper bound <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" />.

## Checking with Boundary Cases

**Boundary cases** test whether any cosine-rule answer is physically sensible and catch forgetting the final square root. Every resultant of <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> must stay inside the closed range from <InlineMath math="\lvert A-B\rvert" /> up to <InlineMath math="A+B" />.

At <InlineMath math="0^\circ" />, the formula reduces to the plain sum, while at <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> it reduces to the absolute difference:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R &=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB} =\sqrt{(A+B)^2}=A+B \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R &=\sqrt{A^2+B^2-2AB} =\sqrt{(A-B)^2}=\lvert A-B\rvert \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

For forces of <InlineMath math="10\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" />, the curve below traces every resultant directly in degrees and newtons between <InlineMath math="2\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="18\text{ N}" />.

Watch the <InlineMath math="2AB\cos a" /> readout: it is positive for angles under <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, zero at <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, and negative above <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, proving Pythagoras is simply one slice of the cosine rule.

## Choosing and Checking the Cosine Rule

Use the cosine rule directly when all four conditions hold:

- there are only two vectors.
- both vector magnitudes are known.
- the **included angle** is known.
- the question asks for resultant magnitude.

For more than two vectors, resolve every vector into coordinate components and add all <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> components using the [analytical method](/en/subjects/physics/vector/analytical-addition-subtraction).

Follow the decision tree before writing equations: jumping straight to components saves time once a third vector enters the problem.

Apply the cosine rule and its bound check to three quick cases:

1. Two perpendicular forces of <InlineMath math="6\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" /> have <InlineMath math="\theta = 90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos 90^\circ = 0" />, giving a resultant between <InlineMath math="2\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="14\text{ N}" />:

<BlockMath math="R = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10\text{ N}" />

2. Two vectors of <InlineMath math="12" /> and <InlineMath math="5" /> units at <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> use <InlineMath math="\cos 120^\circ = -\tfrac12" />:

<BlockMath math="R = \sqrt{144 + 25 + 2(12)(5)\left(-\frac12\right)} = \sqrt{109} \approx 10.4" />

That value sits between <InlineMath math="7" /> and <InlineMath math="17" />, and stays below the perpendicular resultant <InlineMath math="13" /> because the obtuse angle makes the vectors partially oppose.

3. Two equal <InlineMath math="4\text{ N}" /> forces producing a <InlineMath math="4\text{ N}" /> resultant reverse the formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="4^2 = 4^2 + 4^2 + 2(4)(4)\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = -\frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = 120^\circ" />
  <BlockMath math="R = \sqrt{16 + 16 + 32\left(-\frac12\right)} = \sqrt{16} = 4\text{ N}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking the final magnitude back against <InlineMath math="\lvert A-B\rvert" /> and <InlineMath math="A+B" /> catches arithmetic and sign slips before you move on.
