## When Two Vectors Form an Angle

For two vectors pointing in the same direction, add their magnitudes. For opposite directions, take the absolute difference of their magnitudes. Many forces and displacements, however, <Highlight>form an angle with each other</Highlight>. In that situation, the resultant magnitude can be found using the **cosine rule**.

This rule is used when the magnitudes of two vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" /> are known, and the included angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> between them is known.

<BlockMath math="R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta}" />

> Angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> in the cosine rule is the angle between the two vectors when their tails are placed together.

## The Cosine in the Triangle Formula

The cosine rule appears because the resultant of two vectors forms a triangle with two known sides. If the angle between the vectors is small, the vectors reinforce each other more strongly. If the angle approaches <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, they cancel each other more.

When the vectors are arranged **head to tail**, the interior triangle angle is supplementary to the included vector angle. This changes the minus sign in the triangle cosine rule into the plus sign in the resultant formula.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R^2 &= A^2+B^2-2AB\cos(180^\circ-\theta) \\ &= A^2+B^2-2AB(-\cos\theta) \\ &= A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta\end{aligned}" />

The table below shows how <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> changes with the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />:

| Included angle | Value of <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> | Physical meaning |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :--------------- |
| <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | vectors point the same way and fully reinforce |
| <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | vectors are perpendicular |
| <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | vectors point in opposite directions |

When <InlineMath math="\theta=90^\circ" />, the cosine rule becomes the Pythagorean form because <InlineMath math="\cos90^\circ=0" />.

Substitute **both force magnitudes and the included angle**, using newtons for the force values:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
R&=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta} \\
&=\sqrt{10^2+8^2+2(10)(8)\cos60^\circ} \\
&=\sqrt{100+64+160(0.5)} \\
&=\sqrt{244} \\
&\approx15.6\text{ N}

The angle between the two forces makes the resultant less than <InlineMath math="18\text{ N}" />. Since the forces still point in fairly similar directions, the resultant is greater than either force on its own. Check the bounds: <InlineMath math="15.6" /> lies between <InlineMath math="10 - 8 = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="10 + 8 = 18" />. ## Example of Finding the Included Angle from the Resultant

Two forces of <InlineMath math="5\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="3\text{ N}" /> give a resultant of <InlineMath math="7\text{ N}" />. Find the angle between the two forces.

The cosine rule also works backwards to find the angle. **Square the resultant**, then solve for <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="7^2 = 5^2 + 3^2 + 2(5)(3)\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="49 = 34 + 30\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="\cos\theta = \frac{15}{30} = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = 60^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check by putting the angle back in: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{25 + 9 + 30 \cdot \frac12} = \sqrt{49} = 7\text{ N}" />. The resultant also makes sense, because it lies between <InlineMath math="5 - 3 = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="5 + 3 = 8" />.

## Checking with Boundary Cases

**Boundary cases** help check whether an answer is reasonable. If the angle is <InlineMath math="0^\circ" />, the formula should give <InlineMath math="A+B" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R &amp;=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB} \\ &amp;=\sqrt{(A+B)^2} \\ &amp;=A+B \end{aligned}" />

If the angle is <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, the formula should give the difference of the magnitudes.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R &amp;=\sqrt{A^2+B^2-2AB} \\ &amp;=\sqrt{(A-B)^2} \\ &amp;=\lvert A-B\rvert \end{aligned}" />

This check shows that the cosine rule is connected to the simpler same-direction, opposite-direction, and perpendicular-vector cases.

Check any answer against the ends of this curve. <InlineMath math="R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta}" /> falls from <InlineMath math="A+B" /> when the forces point the same way to <InlineMath math="\lvert A-B\rvert" /> when they point opposite ways, so every resultant of <InlineMath math="10\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" /> lies between 2 N and 18 N.

## When to Choose the Cosine Rule

All of the following must hold:

- there are only two vectors.
- both vector magnitudes are known.
- the **included angle** is known.
- the question asks for resultant magnitude.

For more than two vectors, resolve every vector into coordinate components and add all <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> components. This is the [analytical method](/en/subjects/physics/vector/analytical-addition-subtraction). The cosine rule applies directly when exactly two vector magnitudes and their included angle are known.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the interior angle of the tip-to-tail triangle as <InlineMath math="\theta" /> | The resultant formula uses **the angle between the vectors placed tail to tail** | For vectors in the same direction, <InlineMath math="\theta = 0^\circ" /> must give <InlineMath math="A + B" /> |
| Using the minus sign of the triangle cosine rule | The interior angle is the supplement of <InlineMath math="\theta" />, so the sign changes | For <InlineMath math="\theta = 60^\circ" />, the result must exceed each single vector |
| Forgetting the square root | The formula gives <InlineMath math="R^2" />, not <InlineMath math="R" /> | The resultant must lie between <InlineMath math="\lvert A - B \rvert" /> and <InlineMath math="A + B" /> |
| Calculator in radian mode | <InlineMath math="\cos 60" /> in radians is about <InlineMath math="-0.95" /> | Check that <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ = 0.5" /> |

## Practice Problems

Compute with the cosine rule, then **check that the resultant lies between <InlineMath math="\lvert A - B \rvert" /> and <InlineMath math="A + B" />**.

1. Two forces of <InlineMath math="6\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="8\text{ N}" /> are perpendicular. Find the magnitude of the resultant.

2. Two vectors of magnitude <InlineMath math="12" /> and <InlineMath math="5" /> units form an angle of <InlineMath math="120^\circ" />. Find the magnitude of the resultant.

3. Two equal forces of <InlineMath math="4\text{ N}" /> each give a resultant of <InlineMath math="4\text{ N}" />. Find the included angle.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. **Perpendicular means** <InlineMath math="\theta = 90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos\theta = 0" />, so <InlineMath math="R = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10\text{ N}" />. Check: <InlineMath math="10" /> lies between <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="14" />.

**Problem 2**. With <InlineMath math="\cos 120^\circ = -\frac12" />:

<BlockMath math="R = \sqrt{144 + 25 + 2(12)(5)\left(-\frac12\right)} = \sqrt{109} \approx 10.4" />

Check: <InlineMath math="10.4" /> lies between <InlineMath math="12 - 5 = 7" /> and <InlineMath math="17" />, and it is smaller than the same magnitudes give at <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, namely <InlineMath math="\sqrt{144 + 25} = 13" />, because the vectors point further apart.

**Problem 3**. Reverse the formula:

<BlockMath math="R^2 = 16 = 16 + 16 + 32\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = -\frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = 120^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{16 + 16 + 32 \cdot \left(-\frac12\right)} = \sqrt{16} = 4\text{ N}" />.
