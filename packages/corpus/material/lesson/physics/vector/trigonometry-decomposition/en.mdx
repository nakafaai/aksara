## The Angle Determines the Components

When a vector forms an angle with an axis, its components can be found using trigonometry. The idea is simple: the slanted vector becomes <Highlight>the hypotenuse of a right triangle</Highlight>, while its components become the horizontal and vertical sides.

For a vector in quadrant I, measure <InlineMath math="\theta" /> from the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis. The horizontal component is adjacent to the angle and the vertical component is opposite it. For vector magnitude <InlineMath math="F>0" />, divide each side by the hypotenuse, then multiply by <InlineMath math="F" /> again.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\cos\theta=\frac{F_x}{F}\quad\Rightarrow\quad F_x=F\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="\sin\theta=\frac{F_y}{F}\quad\Rightarrow\quad F_y=F\sin\theta" />
</MathContainer>

> Identify the side adjacent to the reference angle before assigning <InlineMath math="\cos" /> and the opposite side before assigning <InlineMath math="\sin" />.

### Calculating the Pulling Force Components

A <InlineMath math="50\text{ N}" /> force acts <InlineMath math="37^\circ" /> above the horizontal. Use the **trigonometric approximations** <InlineMath math="\cos37^\circ\approx0.8" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin37^\circ\approx0.6" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
  F_x&=F\cos\theta \\
  &\approx50(0.8) \\
  &=40\text{ N}
  \end{aligned}" />

  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
  F_y&=F\sin\theta \\
  &\approx50(0.6) \\
  &=30\text{ N}
  \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

With this rounding, the components of the <InlineMath math="50\text{ N}" /> force are approximately a <InlineMath math="40\text{ N}" /> pull to the right and a <InlineMath math="30\text{ N}" /> pull upward.

Without early rounding, the values are about <InlineMath math="39.93\text{ N}" /> and <InlineMath math="30.09\text{ N}" />. The model uses the angle <InlineMath math="37^\circ" /> directly, so its coordinates are not exactly <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />.

## Angle from Vertical Axis

Keep the force in quadrant I, but measure the **angle from the positive vertical axis** toward the force. The component adjacent to <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is now <InlineMath math="F_y" />, while <InlineMath math="F_x" /> is opposite the angle.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="F_y=F\cos\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="F_x=F\sin\theta" />
</MathContainer>

Before using a formula, always ask: from which axis is the angle measured? This small check prevents a common error when resolving vectors.

## Component Signs Follow the Quadrant

If <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the **full standard-position angle** measured counterclockwise from positive <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin\theta" /> already produce the component signs. If an acute reference angle is used instead, first calculate the component magnitudes and then assign signs from the quadrant.

| Vector direction | Sign of <InlineMath math="F_x" /> | Sign of <InlineMath math="F_y" /> |
| :--------------- | :------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| up and right | positive | positive |
| up and left | negative | positive |
| down and left | negative | negative |
| down and right | positive | negative |

If a force points up and to the left, its horizontal component is negative because it opposes the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis, while its vertical component remains positive.

Measure the angle from the positive x axis, counterclockwise, and the quadrant gives the signs. Each arrow has the same size, but its components take the signs of the quadrant it points into. Zoom in on each arrow tip.
