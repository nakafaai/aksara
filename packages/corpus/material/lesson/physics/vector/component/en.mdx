## Why Vectors Are Split into Components

A slanted vector acts along more than one coordinate axis. We split it into parts parallel to those axes so that the effect in each direction can be calculated separately. These parts are called **vector components**.

The horizontal component shows how much of the vector lies along the <InlineMath math="x" /> axis. The vertical component shows how much lies along the <InlineMath math="y" /> axis. In three-dimensional space, there is also a component along the <InlineMath math="z" /> axis.

> A vector equals the sum of its components along the coordinate axes.

The weight of a <InlineMath math="2\text{ kg}" /> block points straight down, <InlineMath math="19.6\text{ N}" />, but **the block can only slide along the plank**. ## Reading Components in a Plane

In a plane, vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> can be written as the combination of its <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> components.

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}=A_x\hat{i}+A_y\hat{j}" />

The notation <InlineMath math="\hat{i}" /> is the **unit vector** in the <InlineMath math="x" /> direction, while <InlineMath math="\hat{j}" /> is the unit vector in the <InlineMath math="y" /> direction. The signed scalars <InlineMath math="A_x" /> and <InlineMath math="A_y" /> give the components along those axes. Each sign records which way along its axis the vector points.

The rope pulls the sled with <InlineMath math="80\text{ N}" /> at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> above the snow. On the axes that pull is <InlineMath math="69.3\text{ N}" /> along the snow and <InlineMath math="40\text{ N}" /> up, **drawn tip to tail under the rope**.

### Calculating Length from Two Components

For example, <InlineMath math="\vec{A}=6\hat{i}+8\hat{j}" /> means the vector has a <InlineMath math="6" /> unit component in the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> direction and an <InlineMath math="8" /> unit component in the positive <InlineMath math="y" /> direction.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\lvert\vec{A}\rvert &= \sqrt{6^2+8^2} \\ &= \sqrt{36+64} \\ &= \sqrt{100}=10\end{aligned}" />

The vector length comes from the **Pythagorean theorem** because the <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> components are perpendicular.

The two component arrows below and the vector form a right triangle, so the length is the hypotenuse: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10" />.

## Components in Three Dimensional Space

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}=A_x\hat{i}+A_y\hat{j}+A_z\hat{k}" />

## Positive and Negative Component Signs

The vector's magnitude stays zero or positive. A component's sign marks <Highlight>the direction along its axis</Highlight>.

| Component | Positive sign means | Negative sign means |
| :-------- | :------------------ | :------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="A_x" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="x" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A_y" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="y" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="y" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A_z" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="z" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="z" /> |

If <InlineMath math="\vec{B}=-5\hat{i}+2\hat{j}" />, the vector has a <InlineMath math="5" /> unit component toward negative <InlineMath math="x" /> and a <InlineMath math="2" /> unit component toward positive <InlineMath math="y" />.

## Displacement Written as Components

A student walks <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> east, then <InlineMath math="4\text{ m}" /> north. If east is the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> direction and north is the positive <InlineMath math="y" /> direction, the displacement is:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{s}=(3\hat{i}+4\hat{j})\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\lvert\vec{s}\rvert &= \sqrt{3^2+4^2}\text{ m} \\ &= \sqrt{9+16}\text{ m} \\ &= 5\text{ m}\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Displacement components have **units of meters**. Unit vectors specify direction only, so the vector equation must retain the physical unit.

The component form shows a net displacement toward the east and north. Its magnitude <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" /> comes from a right triangle with side lengths <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> and <InlineMath math="4\text{ m}" />.

Measure the displacement direction from east toward north. Both components are positive, so the angle is in the first quadrant.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\tan\theta &= \frac{4}{3} \\ \theta &= \arctan\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \\ &\approx 53.1^\circ\end{aligned}" />

The displacement therefore points about <InlineMath math="53.1^\circ" /> north of east.
