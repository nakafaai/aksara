## Why Vectors Are Split into Components

A slanted vector acts along more than one coordinate axis. We split it into parts parallel to those axes so that the effect in each direction can be calculated separately. These parts are called **vector components**.

The horizontal component shows how much of the vector lies along the <InlineMath math="x" /> axis. The vertical component shows how much lies along the <InlineMath math="y" /> axis. In three-dimensional space, there is also a component along the <InlineMath math="z" /> axis.

> A vector equals the sum of its components along the coordinate axes.

The weight of a <InlineMath math="2\text{ kg}" /> block points straight down, <InlineMath math="19.6\text{ N}" />, but **the block can only slide along the plank**. Splitting the weight shows what matters: <InlineMath math="9.8\text{ N}" /> down the slope at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="16.97\text{ N}" /> into it. Tilt the plank until the slope part wins and the block slides.

## Reading Components in a Plane

In a flat plane, every vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> splits cleanly into **unit vector** parts along the horizontal <InlineMath math="x" /> axis and the vertical <InlineMath math="y" /> axis.

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}=A_x\hat{i}+A_y\hat{j}" />

The notation <InlineMath math="\hat{i}" /> is the **unit vector** in the <InlineMath math="x" /> direction, while <InlineMath math="\hat{j}" /> is the unit vector in the <InlineMath math="y" /> direction. The signed scalars <InlineMath math="A_x" /> and <InlineMath math="A_y" /> give the components along those axes, and each sign records which way along its axis the vector points.

### Finding Length from Plane Components

For example, <InlineMath math="\vec{A}=6\hat{i}+8\hat{j}" /> means the vector has a <InlineMath math="6" /> unit component in the positive <InlineMath math="x" /> direction and an <InlineMath math="8" /> unit component in the positive <InlineMath math="y" /> direction.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\lvert\vec{A}\rvert &= \sqrt{6^2+8^2} \\ &= \sqrt{36+64} \\ &= \sqrt{100}=10\end{aligned}" />

The vector length comes from the **Pythagorean theorem** because the <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> components are perpendicular. The two component arrows and the vector form a right triangle whose hypotenuse has length <InlineMath math="10" />.

## Components in Three Dimensional Space

In three-dimensional space, a vector needs three perpendicular components along <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" />, written with the **third unit vector** <InlineMath math="\hat{k}" />.

<BlockMath math="\vec{A}=A_x\hat{i}+A_y\hat{j}+A_z\hat{k}" />

## Positive and Negative Component Signs

The vector's magnitude stays zero or positive. A component's sign marks <Highlight>the direction along its axis</Highlight>.

| Component | Positive sign means | Negative sign means |
| :-------- | :------------------ | :------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="A_x" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="x" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A_y" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="y" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="y" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A_z" /> | toward positive <InlineMath math="z" /> | toward negative <InlineMath math="z" /> |

If <InlineMath math="\vec{B}=-5\hat{i}+2\hat{j}" />, the vector has a <InlineMath math="5" /> unit component toward negative <InlineMath math="x" /> and a <InlineMath math="2" /> unit component toward positive <InlineMath math="y" />.

On a compass bearing <InlineMath math="b" /> measured clockwise from north, an <InlineMath math="8\text{ km}" /> ferry trip points southwest at <InlineMath math="b = 225^\circ" />, so **both components turn negative**: <InlineMath math="-5.66\text{ km}" /> east and <InlineMath math="-5.66\text{ km}" /> north. Turn <InlineMath math="b" /> through every quadrant and watch the component signs flip.

## Displacement Written as Components

Any path across a grid or city street breaks cleanly into **axis components with physical units**. Adding the east-west steps separately from the north-south steps produces the net displacement vector, its straight distance, and its angle directly from right-triangle geometry.

### Two Perpendicular Steps on Foot

A hiker walks <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> east and then turns <InlineMath math="4\text{ m}" /> north. Taking east as positive <InlineMath math="x" /> and north as positive <InlineMath math="y" />, the displacement is:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{s}=(3\hat{i}+4\hat{j})\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\lvert\vec{s}\rvert &= \sqrt{3^2+4^2}\text{ m} \\ &= \sqrt{9+16}\text{ m} \\ &= 5\text{ m}\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Displacement components carry **units of meters**. Unit vectors specify direction only, so the vector equation must retain the physical unit. Measuring the angle from east toward north in the first quadrant gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\tan\theta &= \frac{4}{3} \\ \theta &= \arctan\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \\ &\approx 53.1^\circ\end{aligned}" />

The displacement therefore points about <InlineMath math="53.1^\circ" /> north of east.
