## Heat and Temperature Are Different

**Heat** is energy transferred from a hot body to a cold body, measured in joules. **Temperature** measures how hot something is, linked to the average kinetic energy of its particles, measured in degrees Celsius or kelvin. The two main formulas of this chapter give the energy to raise a temperature and the energy to change a state of matter:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = mc\theta" />
<BlockMath math="Q = ml" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="m" /> is the mass in kilograms, <InlineMath math="c" /> is the **specific heat capacity**, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the change in temperature, and <InlineMath math="l" /> is the **specific latent heat**. In these notes, you heat water in a kettle, feed energy into ice until it becomes steam, and squeeze a gas in a syringe.

## Thermal Equilibrium

When two bodies touch, heat flows from the hotter one to the colder one. The net flow stops when both reach the same temperature. This state is called **thermal equilibrium**. A thermometer works because of it: the thermometer reaches thermal equilibrium with a body, and then its reading equals the body's temperature.

## Specific Heat Capacity

The **specific heat capacity**, <InlineMath math="c" />, is the heat needed to raise the temperature of <InlineMath math="1\text{ kg}" /> of a substance by <InlineMath math="1^\circ\text{C}" />. Its unit is <InlineMath math="\text{J kg}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" />. A substance with a large <InlineMath math="c" /> heats up slowly and cools down slowly.

Water has a very large specific heat capacity. That's why water is used as the coolant in a car radiator, and why the sea warms up slowly during the day and drives a sea breeze.

### Example Energy to Heat Water

Find **the heat needed** to warm <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ kg}" /> of water from <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" />.

<BlockMath math="Q = mc\theta = (2.0)(4{,}200)(100 - 25) = 630{,}000\text{ J}" />

Check the units: <InlineMath math="\text{kg} \times \text{J kg}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \times {}^\circ\text{C} = \text{J}" />. The answer is <InlineMath math="6.3 \times 10^{5}\text{ J}" />.

### Example Kettle Heating Time

A <InlineMath math="2{,}000\text{ W}" /> kettle heats <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ kg}" /> of water from <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" />. Assume no heat is lost, so **the electrical energy equals the heat gained**, <InlineMath math="Pt = mc\theta" />:

<BlockMath math="t = \frac{mc\theta}{P} = \frac{(1.5)(4{,}200)(75)}{2{,}000} = 236\text{ s}" />

That's about four minutes. In practice it takes longer, because some heat warms the kettle and the air.

## Latent Heat and the Heating Curve

While matter changes state, its temperature stays constant even though heat keeps flowing in. That energy goes into **breaking the bonds between particles**, not into extra kinetic energy. It's called **latent heat**. For ice, the specific latent heat of fusion is <InlineMath math="3.34 \times 10^{5}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" />, and for water, the specific latent heat of vaporisation is <InlineMath math="2.26 \times 10^{6}\text{ J kg}^{-1}" />.

## Gas Laws

For a fixed mass of gas, the pressure <InlineMath math="P" />, volume <InlineMath math="V" /> and absolute temperature <InlineMath math="T" /> are linked by three laws. The temperature has to be in **kelvin**: <InlineMath math="T = \theta + 273" />.

| Law | Quantity kept constant | Relationship |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Boyle's law | Temperature | <InlineMath math="P_1V_1 = P_2V_2" /> |
| Charles' law | Pressure | <InlineMath math="\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}" /> |
| Gay-Lussac's law | Volume | <InlineMath math="\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}" /> |

Kinetic theory explains **Boyle's law**: when the volume shrinks at constant temperature, the gas molecules hit the container walls more often, so the pressure rises. ## Common Mistakes about Heat

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Heat and temperature are the same thing. | Heat is energy transferred. Temperature is how hot something is. | Check the units: joules for heat, degrees or kelvin for temperature. |
| The temperature rises while ice melts. | The energy goes into breaking bonds, so the temperature stays constant. | Find the flat part of the heating curve. |
| Using degrees Celsius in the gas laws. | The gas laws need absolute temperature. | Add <InlineMath math="273" /> before you substitute. |
| Using <InlineMath math="Q = mc\theta" /> during boiling. | There's no temperature change during a change of state. | Use <InlineMath math="Q = ml" /> for the flat parts. |

The third mistake is the most common one in gas law questions. **Convert to kelvin first**, then calculate.

## Practice on Heat

These questions start with one formula and finish with the gas laws. **Write the unit of every quantity** before you substitute.

1. Find the heat needed to raise the temperature of <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ kg}" /> of aluminium by <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />.

2. A <InlineMath math="0.4\text{ kg}" /> portion of water at <InlineMath math="90^\circ\text{C}" /> is mixed with <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ kg}" /> of water at <InlineMath math="15^\circ\text{C}" />. Find the final temperature, assuming no heat is lost.

3. A gas in a syringe at <InlineMath math="100\text{ kPa}" /> has a volume of <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^3" />. It's compressed slowly to <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}^3" /> at constant temperature. Find the new pressure.

4. A tyre holds air at <InlineMath math="150\text{ kPa}" /> and <InlineMath math="27^\circ\text{C}" />. The air is heated to <InlineMath math="127^\circ\text{C}" /> at constant volume. Find the new pressure.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Use <InlineMath math="Q = mc\theta" /> with <InlineMath math="c = 900\text{ J kg}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" />:

<BlockMath math="Q = (0.5)(900)(40) = 18{,}000\text{ J}" />

**Solution 2**: The heat given out by the hot water equals the heat taken in by the cold water. Let the final temperature be <InlineMath math="\theta" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(0.4)(4{,}200)(90 - \theta) = (0.1)(4{,}200)(\theta - 15)" />
<BlockMath math="36 - 0.4\theta = 0.1\theta - 1.5" />
<BlockMath math="\theta = \frac{37.5}{0.5} = 75^\circ\text{C}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="75^\circ\text{C}" /> lies between <InlineMath math="15^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="90^\circ\text{C}" />, closer to the hot water because its mass is larger.

**Solution 3**: The temperature is constant, so use Boyle's law:

<BlockMath math="P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{(100)(60)}{20} = 300\text{ kPa}" />

The volume becomes a third, so **the pressure triples**.

**Solution 4**: The volume is constant, so use Gay-Lussac's law with absolute temperatures: <InlineMath math="T_1 = 300\text{ K}" /> and <InlineMath math="T_2 = 400\text{ K}" />.

<BlockMath math="P_2 = \frac{P_1T_2}{T_1} = \frac{(150)(400)}{300} = 200\text{ kPa}" />

If you used degrees Celsius by mistake, you'd get <InlineMath math="150 \times 127 / 27 \approx 706\text{ kPa}" />, an impossible value for an ordinary tyre.
