## Thermal Equilibrium

Touch a metal spoon resting in hot soup and energy streams straight into your fingers. **Heat** is thermal energy in transit from a hotter body to a colder one, measured in joules, whereas **temperature** tracks the average kinetic energy of particles in degrees Celsius or kelvin.

When two bodies touch, net heat flows until both settle at the same temperature, a state called **thermal equilibrium**. A thermometer works precisely by reaching thermal equilibrium with whatever it measures. ## Heat Capacity and State

Supplying heat to a substance raises its **internal energy** in one of two ways: speeding up molecules to raise temperature, or breaking intermolecular bonds to change state.

### Specific Heat Capacity

The **specific heat capacity** <InlineMath math="c" /> is the energy needed to raise <InlineMath math="1\text{ kg}" /> of a substance by <InlineMath math="1^\circ\text{C}" />.

<BlockMath math="Q = mc\Delta\theta" />

### Latent Heat Plateaus

During melting or boiling, a thermometer stops climbing even though the heater keeps running. Never use <InlineMath math="Q = mc\Delta\theta" /> along a flat plateau because temperature is unchanged. All incoming energy breaks intermolecular bonds as **latent heat**:

<BlockMath math="Q = ml" />

> While a pure substance melts or boils, heat breaks intermolecular bonds instead of raising average particle kinetic energy, keeping temperature flat.

## Kinetic Theory of Gases

A trapped gas builds **gas pressure** because billions of fast molecules strike the container walls every second. Holding one variable constant links pressure <InlineMath math="P" />, volume <InlineMath math="V" />, and absolute temperature <InlineMath math="T" /> through three classic laws:

| Law | Held constant | Relation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Boyle's law | Temperature | Inverse pressure and volume |
| Charles's law | Pressure | Direct volume and kelvin temperature |
| Gay-Lussac's law | Volume | Direct pressure and kelvin temperature |

## Absolute Zero and Kelvin

Plot gas volume against Celsius temperature at constant pressure and extend the straight line backward. Every ideal gas extrapolates to zero volume at <InlineMath math="-273^\circ\text{C}" />, defining **absolute zero** (<InlineMath math="0\text{ K}" />).

<BlockMath math="T = \theta + 273" />

> Every gas-law ratio requires absolute kelvin temperature because zero Celsius is merely the freezing point of water, not zero molecular motion.

## Thermal Calculation Checks

Verify two everyday heat and gas calculations with exact unit conversions.

**Water mixing check.** Mix <InlineMath math="0.4\text{ kg}" /> of water at <InlineMath math="90^\circ\text{C}" /> with <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ kg}" /> of water at <InlineMath math="15^\circ\text{C}" /> inside an insulated cup. Find the equilibrium temperature <InlineMath math="\theta" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(0.4)(4{,}200)(90 - \theta) = (0.1)(4{,}200)(\theta - 15)" />
<BlockMath math="36 - 0.4\theta = 0.1\theta - 1.5 \implies \theta = 75^\circ\text{C}" />
</MathContainer>

The final temperature sits much closer to <InlineMath math="90^\circ\text{C}" /> because four times as much hot water was poured in.

**Tyre pressure check.** Air inside a rigid tyre sits at <InlineMath math="150\text{ kPa}" /> and <InlineMath math="27^\circ\text{C}" /> (<InlineMath math="300\text{ K}" />). After driving, it warms to <InlineMath math="127^\circ\text{C}" /> (<InlineMath math="400\text{ K}" />). Find the new pressure.

<BlockMath math="P_2 = \frac{P_1 T_2}{T_1} = \frac{(150)(400)}{300} = 200\text{ kPa}" />

Using Celsius by mistake would falsely predict <InlineMath math="706\text{ kPa}" />, illustrating why kelvin conversion always comes first.
