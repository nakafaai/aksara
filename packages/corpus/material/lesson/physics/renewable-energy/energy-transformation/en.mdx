## Energy Conversion Sequence in a Device

**Energy transformation** is a change from one form of energy into another. Energy can pass between a device and its surroundings, but [the total amount of energy remains constant](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/what-is-energy/laws-of-energy.php).

When you switch on a flashlight, chemical energy in the battery becomes electrical energy supplied to the lamp. The lamp produces **the light you need** while also heating parts of the flashlight and its surroundings. That heating still counts in the energy balance, even though your purpose is to illuminate the room.

Swap the bulb and the energy path stays the same while the useful share changes. Two AA cells hold <InlineMath math="3\text{ V}\times2.5\text{ Ah}=27\,000\text{ J}" />, and the bulb draws <InlineMath math="1\text{ W}" />, so the battery lasts <InlineMath math="7.5\text{ h}" /> either way. A filament bulb turns about <InlineMath math="5\%" /> of that power into light, an LED about <InlineMath math="40\%" />: eight times as much light from the same battery.

In [hydropower](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/how-hydropower-works), water flows through a penstock and spins turbine blades. The turbine drives a **generator that produces electricity**.

In [wind power](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/how-do-wind-turbines-work), wind turns the turbine blades, the rotor drives a generator, and the generator produces electricity.

In a [photovoltaic panel](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/solar/photovoltaics-and-electricity.php), cells convert sunlight directly into electricity. Only photons absorbed by the semiconductor material contribute energy to electricity generation.

| Technology | Main conversion path | Energy often transferred to the surroundings as |
| :--------- | :------------------- | :------------------------- |
| Hydropower plant | gravitational potential of water → kinetic energy of water → turbine → generator → electricity | heat from shaft friction, sound, vibration |
| Wind turbine | wind kinetic energy → turbine → generator → electricity | sound, vibration, heat from components |
| Photovoltaic solar panel | solar radiation → electricity | reflected light and heat |
| Biomass | chemical energy → combustion → heat → turbine → generator → electricity | waste heat, exhaust gases, sound |
| Biogas | chemical energy → combustion in an engine or turbine → shaft motion → generator → electricity | waste heat, exhaust gases, sound |
| Geothermal power | reservoir thermal energy → flowing steam or working fluid → turbine → generator → electricity | waste heat and system friction |

[Biopower technologies](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/fuels/biopower-energy-heat-and-electricity) convert biomass into heat and electricity through combustion, bacterial decomposition, or conversion into gaseous and liquid fuels.

[Geothermal power plants](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/geothermal/geothermal-electricity-generation) use hot fluid or steam from below Earth's surface to drive turbines and produce electricity.

A power plant converts energy in stages, and every stage passes on only part of what it gets, so the stage efficiencies multiply. With illustrative values of <InlineMath math="90\%" /> for the turbine and <InlineMath math="95\%" /> each for the generator and the lines, the chain delivers <InlineMath math="\eta=0.9\times0.95\times0.95\approx81\%" /> of the water's energy. Rotate the bars and watch the gray part grow.

## Efficiency Measures the Useful Share

Because not all input energy becomes the output we need, we use **efficiency**. Efficiency compares useful energy with input energy.

<BlockMath math="\eta=\frac{E_{\text{useful}}}{E_{\text{input}}}\times 100\%" />

Symbol guide:

| Symbol | Meaning |
| :----- | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="\eta" /> | efficiency |
| <InlineMath math="E_{\text{useful}}" /> | energy delivered as the intended useful output |
| <InlineMath math="E_{\text{input}}" /> | energy received by the device |

During the following measurement, assume **the energy stored in the panel does not change**. Suppose a panel receives <InlineMath math="800 \text{ J}" /> of light energy and produces <InlineMath math="160 \text{ J}" /> of electrical energy. Its efficiency is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\eta
&= \frac{E_{\text{useful}}}{E_{\text{input}}}\times 100\% \\
&= \frac{160 \text{ J}}{800 \text{ J}}\times 100\% \\
&= 20\%
\end{aligned}" />

The energy that does not become useful electricity is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{surroundings}}
&= E_{\text{input}}-E_{\text{useful}} \\
&= 800 \text{ J}-160 \text{ J} \\
&= 640 \text{ J}
\end{aligned}" />

So, an efficiency of <InlineMath math="20\%" /> does not mean the remaining <InlineMath math="80\%" /> disappears. The remaining <InlineMath math="80\%" /> is transferred to the surroundings, for example through heating or reflected and transmitted radiation.

[Energy efficiency](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/use-of-energy/efficiency-and-conservation.php) measures the share of input energy converted into useful output by a device.

Move the efficiency of the panel and watch the <InlineMath math="800\text{ J}" /> split. At <InlineMath math="\eta=20\%" /> the amber band is the <InlineMath math="160\text{ J}" /> of electricity from the example and the pink band the <InlineMath math="640\text{ J}" /> that goes to the surroundings, and the two bands always add back to the input.

## Comparing Conversion Paths across Devices

Solar panels, wind turbines, generators, and biogas reactors convert existing energy from one form to another. The device name alone <Highlight>does not state the source</Highlight>, conversion path, useful output, or energy transferred to the surroundings.

| When you see this device | Ask this question |
| :---------------- | :------- |
| Solar panel | How much radiant energy becomes electricity? |
| Wind turbine | Is the wind fast enough to rotate the rotor? |
| Hydropower plant | How large are the water flow rate and height difference? |
| Biomass | Is the feedstock sufficient and managed sustainably? |
| Geothermal power plant | Does the site provide sufficient underground heat, fluid, and flow for power generation? |

The conversion path records the source, initial energy form, output form, useful energy, and energy transferred to the surroundings.

Now compare whole chains side by side. In the chart, <InlineMath math="100\text{ J}" /> enters each plant, every machine passes on its share, and each loss bends down into the surroundings. With typical round shares, a solar panel delivers <InlineMath math="19.2\text{ J}" />, a wind turbine <InlineMath math="40.61\text{ J}" />, a hydropower plant <InlineMath math="85.5\text{ J}" />, a biogas engine <InlineMath math="33.25\text{ J}" />, and a geothermal plant <InlineMath math="11.4\text{ J}" />. The lost joules never vanish: together with the electricity they always make <InlineMath math="100\text{ J}" />.
