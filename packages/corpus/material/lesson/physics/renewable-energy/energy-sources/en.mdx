## Distinguishing Energy Sources and Energy Forms

An energy source identifies <Highlight>where the energy comes from before conversion</Highlight>. An [energy form](/en/subjects/physics/renewable-energy/energy-forms) identifies how energy is stored or transferred.

Trace the energy from its source through a [conversion device](/en/subjects/physics/renewable-energy/energy-transformation) to the useful energy it supplies.

[**Primary energy sources**](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/what-is-energy/sources-of-energy.php) occur in nature. **Energy carriers** such as electricity and hydrogen are produced using those sources. Electricity can be generated from coal, flowing water, sunlight, wind, geothermal heat, or biogas.

## Renewable Sources Still Depend on Time and Place

A renewable energy source is replenished by natural processes on a human time scale. It is **not necessarily available at every moment**. Solar output depends on the time of day and weather, wind output on wind speed, and hydropower output on season and water flow rate.

Wind shows how strongly "available right now" can swing. The power the wind carries through a rotor of area <InlineMath math="A" /> is <InlineMath math="P=\tfrac12\rho Av^3" />, because faster air brings more kilograms each second and each kilogram carries more energy. In the graph below, the rotor has a radius of <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> and air has <InlineMath math="\rho=1.2\text{ kg/m}^3" />. Compare the two points: doubling the wind speed from <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="8\text{ m/s}" /> makes the power <InlineMath math="2^3=8" /> times larger.

## Compare Sources through Energy Conversion Paths

Compare an energy source by tracing its initial energy form, conversion device, and **practical limitation**. The table summarizes each pattern briefly.

| Energy source | Short conversion pattern | Constraint to check |
| :------------ | :----------------------- | :--- |
| Coal, petroleum, natural gas | Burning fossil fuel converts its chemical energy into heat that can drive a turbine. | Finite reserves |
| Biomass | Chemical energy stored in organic material becomes heat through combustion, or the material is processed into another fuel before use. | Feedstock that protects ecosystems and maintained equipment |
| Biogas | Microorganisms produce methane-rich gas from organic material without oxygen. Burning the gas releases its chemical energy as heat. That heat can drive machinery or generate electricity. | Feedstock that protects ecosystems and maintained equipment |
| Water | Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy of flowing water spin a turbine. | Flow rate and height difference |
| Wind | Kinetic energy from moving air spins turbine blades. | Wind speed |
| Sunlight | Radiant energy from the Sun becomes electricity in solar cells or heat in solar heating systems. | Incoming sunlight |
| Ocean waves and tides | Technologies such as buoys, oscillating water columns, and marine turbines convert the mechanical energy of moving seawater into electricity. | Coastal conditions |
| Geothermal heat | Hot fluid or steam carries thermal energy from inside Earth and drives a turbine. | Geological conditions |

[Coal, petroleum, and natural gas](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/what-is-energy/sources-of-energy.php) formed from ancient living remains over very long periods.

Ocean waves show how strongly a source depends on its conditions. A regular wave of height <InlineMath math="H" /> and period <InlineMath math="T" /> carries <InlineMath math="P=\frac{\rho g^2H^2T}{64\pi}" /> across every metre of its crest, so twice the height brings four times the power. A calm <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> swell carries about <InlineMath math="2.9\text{ kW}" /> per metre, while a rough <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> sea carries about <InlineMath math="44.2\text{ kW}" />. The buoy rides the waves, and its generator on the seabed turns that motion into electricity.

## Distinguishing Similar Energy Terms

**Biogas** comes from biomass broken down by microorganisms without oxygen. This process is called [*anaerobic digestion*](https://www.epa.gov/anaerobic-digestion/basic-information-about-anaerobic-digestion). Biogas consists mainly of methane, <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_4" />, and carbon dioxide, <InlineMath math="\text{CO}_2" />.

**Solar energy** can be converted in different ways. [Photovoltaic panels](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/solar/photovoltaics-and-electricity.php) convert light directly into electricity, while solar thermal systems collect heat from sunlight.

[**Marine energy**](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/water/marine-energy-basics) includes waves, tides, ocean currents, and temperature differences in seawater. Some technologies use the kinetic energy of waves, currents, and tides. Ocean thermal energy conversion instead uses the temperature difference between warm surface water and colder deep water.

[**Geothermal energy**](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/geothermal/geothermal-basics) is heat from inside Earth. Geothermal resources can supply electricity, heating, cooling, and direct heat.

Here's anaerobic digestion working in a household digester. Fresh cattle dung goes in, microorganisms break it down without oxygen, and biogas collects under the dome. With typical yields, <InlineMath math="25\text{ kg}" /> of dung a day gives about <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}^3" /> of biogas, of which <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ m}^3" /> is methane holding <InlineMath math="21.6\text{ MJ}" />, enough to keep one stove burner lit for <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ h}" />. Feed it more and watch the flame grow.
