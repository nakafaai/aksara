## Where Energy Is Present and How It Crosses System Boundaries

Energy is measured in joules, with the symbol <InlineMath math="\text{J}" />. In a calculation, we track <Highlight>where energy is present</Highlight> and <Highlight>how it moves from one system to another</Highlight>. A moving object has kinetic energy, an elevated object has gravitational potential energy, and a battery or biomass contains chemical energy. Energy can move between systems through heating, radiation, mechanical work, or electrical work.

All of these descriptions refer to the same physical quantity. The relevant formula depends on the object or process being analyzed.

## Four Common Energy Calculations

Renewable-energy systems often involve **motion, height, temperature differences**, and moving electric charge. Each situation uses a different calculation.

| Quantity being calculated | When it appears | Mathematical model | Renewable-energy example |
| :---------- | :-------------- | :------------------------ | :----------------------- |
| Kinetic energy | an object moves | <InlineMath math="E_k=\frac{1}{2}mv^2" /> | wind turns a turbine |
| Gravitational potential energy | an object is above a reference level | <InlineMath math="E_p=mgh" /> | stored water is higher than a turbine |
| Energy transferred by heating | two systems have different temperatures | <InlineMath math="Q=mc\Delta T" /> | geothermal fluid heats a power system |
| Energy transferred electrically | electric charge moves through a potential difference | <InlineMath math="E=VIt" /> | a generator sends energy into the grid |

Biomass and batteries also contain chemical energy, while solar panels receive energy carried by sunlight. The four formulas in the table calculate energy from motion, height, temperature change, and electrical power.

A stretched spring stores elastic energy <InlineMath math="E=\tfrac12kx^2" />. With <InlineMath math="k=200\text{ N/m}" />, three times the stretch stores nine times the energy, <InlineMath math="1\text{ J}" /> at <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="9\text{ J}" /> at <InlineMath math="30\text{ cm}" />. Follow the curve.

## Match the Formula to the Situation

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has **because of its motion**.

<BlockMath math="E_k=\frac{1}{2}mv^2" />

Symbol guide:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI unit |
| :----- | :------ | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="E_k" /> | kinetic energy | <InlineMath math="\text{J}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="m" /> | object mass | <InlineMath math="\text{kg}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="v" /> | object speed | <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}" /> |

If the air mass passing a turbine is <InlineMath math="2 \text{ kg}" /> and its speed is <InlineMath math="3 \text{ m/s}" />, its kinetic energy is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
E_k &= \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(2 \text{ kg})(3 \text{ m/s})^2 \\
&= 9 \text{ J}
\end{aligned}" />

The graph below runs the same formula for every speed from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="6\text{ m/s}" />. Compare the two marked points. Doubling the speed from <InlineMath math="3\text{ m/s}" /> to <InlineMath math="6\text{ m/s}" /> doesn't double the energy: it goes from <InlineMath math="9\text{ J}" /> to <InlineMath math="36\text{ J}" />, four times as much, because the speed is squared. That's why a small rise in wind speed matters so much for a turbine.

The work-energy theorem relates net work to the change in kinetic energy.

Gravitational potential energy is energy related to an object's position in a gravitational field. For objects near Earth's surface, the common model is:

<BlockMath math="E_p=mgh" />

Symbol guide:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI unit |
| :----- | :------ | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="E_p" /> | gravitational potential energy | <InlineMath math="\text{J}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="m" /> | object mass | <InlineMath math="\text{kg}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="g" /> | gravitational acceleration | <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="h" /> | height relative to a reference level | <InlineMath math="\text{m}" /> |

The value of <InlineMath math="g" /> near Earth's surface is about <InlineMath math="9.8 \text{ m/s}^2" />. Many basic calculations use <InlineMath math="g=10 \text{ m/s}^2" /> to make multiplication by hand simpler. Results calculated with that rounded value are approximations.

Calculate the potential energy of <InlineMath math="2 \text{ kg}" /> of water <InlineMath math="5 \text{ m}" /> above a turbine. Taking the turbine as zero height and using <InlineMath math="g=10 \text{ m/s}^2" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E_p &= mgh \\ &= (2 \text{ kg})(10 \text{ m/s}^2)(5 \text{ m}) \\ &= 100 \text{ J} \end{aligned}" />

An object contains internal energy. When two systems have different temperatures, energy moves from the hotter system to the colder system. Energy transferred because of this temperature difference is called heat. If the substance does not change phase and its specific heat is treated as constant over the temperature interval, the transferred energy is:

<BlockMath math="Q=mc\Delta T" />

Symbol guide:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI unit |
| :----- | :------ | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="Q" /> | heat or transferred energy | <InlineMath math="\text{J}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="m" /> | object mass | <InlineMath math="\text{kg}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="c" /> | specific heat capacity | <InlineMath math="\text{J/(kg K)}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\Delta T" /> | temperature change | <InlineMath math="\text{K}" /> |

Suppose we heat <InlineMath math="0.5 \text{ kg}" /> of liquid water through <InlineMath math="10 \text{ K}" /> without boiling. Using the approximate specific heat capacity <InlineMath math="c=4\,200 \text{ J/(kg K)}" />, the energy received by the water is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} Q &= mc\Delta T \\ &= (0.5 \text{ kg})(4\,200 \text{ J/(kg K)})(10 \text{ K}) \\ &= 21\,000 \text{ J} \end{aligned}" />

Electrical energy can be calculated from electric power over a time interval. The model below is used when <InlineMath math="V" /> and <InlineMath math="I" /> are treated as constant during the time interval.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
P &= VI \\
E &= Pt \\
E &= VIt
\end{aligned}" />

Symbol guide:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI unit |
| :----- | :------ | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="E" /> | electrical energy | <InlineMath math="\text{J}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="P" /> | electric power | <InlineMath math="\text{W}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="V" /> | electric potential difference | <InlineMath math="\text{V}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="I" /> | electric current | <InlineMath math="\text{A}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="t" /> | time | <InlineMath math="\text{s}" /> |

Here <InlineMath math="E" /> denotes electrical energy. Upright <InlineMath math="\text{W}" /> is the unit symbol for watt, while italic <InlineMath math="W" /> often represents work.

A device operates at a constant voltage of <InlineMath math="12 \text{ V}" /> and current of <InlineMath math="0.5 \text{ A}" /> for <InlineMath math="60 \text{ s}" />. First calculate power, then energy:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} P &= VI = (12 \text{ V})(0.5 \text{ A})=6 \text{ W} \\ E &= Pt = (6 \text{ W})(60 \text{ s})=360 \text{ J} \end{aligned}" />

Compare this straight line with the kinetic energy curve above. Potential energy grows in proportion to height, so every extra meter adds the same <InlineMath math="mg=20\text{ J}" />.

## One Power Plant Converts Several Forms of Energy

Hydropower, for example, follows this chain:

The water first has **gravitational potential energy**. As it flows downward, energy becomes kinetic energy of the flowing water, then kinetic energy of the turning turbine, and finally electrical energy.

A solar panel receives radiant energy from sunlight, then changes part of that energy into electrical energy.

A complete energy chain names the initial energy, the desired output, and each conversion between them.

Follow those forms through a real dam. Every second, <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}^3" /> of water drops through the head, the height between the reservoir's surface and the turbine. Its potential energy each second is <InlineMath math="\rho g Q h" />, so at a head of <InlineMath math="50\text{ m}" /> it releases <InlineMath math="5\text{ MW}" /> with <InlineMath math="g=10\text{ m/s}^2" />. The water reaches the turbine at <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2gh}\approx31.6\text{ m/s}" />, the shaft keeps <InlineMath math="90\%" />, and the generator sends <InlineMath math="95\%" /> of that to the grid. Raise the head and watch every bar grow in step.

## Distinguish Commonly Confused Ideas

| Misconception | Correction |
| :------ | :------------------- |
| Kinetic energy is treated as depending only on mass | Kinetic energy also depends on the square of speed, written as <InlineMath math="v^2" /> |
| Heat is treated as a substance stored inside an object | Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference |
| <InlineMath math="\text{kWh}" /> is treated as a unit of power | <InlineMath math="\text{kWh}" /> is a unit of energy because it comes from <InlineMath math="P \times t" /> |
| All energy from a renewable source is treated as directly becoming electricity | A conversion device also transfers some energy to the surroundings, so only part of the input becomes useful electrical output |

To choose the relevant formula for a renewable-energy device, <Highlight>identify the form of energy entering it</Highlight>. Moving air has kinetic energy, elevated water has gravitational potential energy, geothermal fluids carry thermal energy, biomass stores chemical energy, and sunlight carries radiant energy.
