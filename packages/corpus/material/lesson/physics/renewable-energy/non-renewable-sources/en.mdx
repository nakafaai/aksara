## Geological Reserves of Nonrenewable Sources Are Finite

[Non-renewable energy sources](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/what-is-energy/sources-of-energy.php) come from limited stocks that form much more slowly than people consume them.

Let <InlineMath math="r_{\mathrm{use}}" /> be the human use rate and <InlineMath math="r_{\mathrm{form}}" /> the natural formation rate:

<BlockMath math="r_{\mathrm{use}}\gg r_{\mathrm{form}}" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="\gg" /> means "much greater than". To judge a fuel supply, check <Highlight>the amount available and the rate of use</Highlight>.

Try the inequality with round, made-up numbers. A stock of <InlineMath math="S_0=100" /> units is used at <InlineMath math="r_{\mathrm{use}}=2" /> units per year, so it shrinks by <InlineMath math="r_{\mathrm{use}}-r_{\mathrm{form}}" /> each year. If nature replaced half of what we use, the violet line would last a full <InlineMath math="100" /> years, twice as long. When <InlineMath math="r_{\mathrm{use}}\gg r_{\mathrm{form}}" />, the orange line hits zero after only about <InlineMath math="50" /> years, almost as if nothing formed at all.

## Fossil Fuels and Nuclear Fuel

The two main groups of non-renewable sources are fossil fuels and nuclear fuel. [**Fossil fuels**](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/what-is-energy/sources-of-energy.php) formed from ancient living remains that were buried, then changed by pressure and heat over millions of years.

**Nuclear fuel** is used in [fission](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/nuclear/), a process in which a heavy atomic nucleus splits and releases energy. Nuclear power plants do not burn carbon at the plant. They use uranium as fuel.

| Source | Initial energy | Origin and use |
| :----- | :------------- | :-------------------------- |
| Coal | Chemical energy | Coal forms from ancient plant remains that changed into carbon-rich solid fuel. |
| Petroleum | Chemical energy | Petroleum is a liquid hydrocarbon mixture refined into fuels such as gasoline and diesel. |
| Natural gas | Chemical energy | Natural gas consists mainly of methane, which can be burned to produce heat. |
| Uranium | Nuclear energy | Uranium comes from minerals mined for nuclear reactor fuel. |

The term "non-renewable" <Highlight>includes both fossil fuels and nuclear fuel</Highlight>. Coal, petroleum, and natural gas are burned, while uranium releases energy through nuclear fission.

## Heat Is a Shared Conversion Step

Many power plants using non-renewable sources produce electricity through heat. The plants differ in how they produce that heat.

In steam power plants, combustion and fission produce **heat through different processes**. Their conversion paths then meet:

Fossil fuels and uranium both reach the same final energy form, electricity. The early path is different, but each source still needs a conversion device before its initial energy becomes useful energy.

## Different Roles on the Power Grid

Power plants do not all supply electricity in the same way. Nuclear plants usually run continuously at a steady output. Natural-gas combined-cycle plants can **raise or lower their output** as demand changes. When demand reaches a peak, combustion turbines fueled by natural gas or petroleum can add electricity for a few hours.

In the U.S. electricity system, these are called [base-load, intermediate-load, and peak-load roles](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/electricity-in-the-us-generation-capacity-and-sales.php).

This operating pattern determines whether a plant supplies steady base load, follows changes in demand, or serves a short peak. Evaluating its energy source also requires checking the fuel stock, emissions, waste, and the effects of mining or drilling.

In this illustrative day, the gray line is the steady base load that a plant running at constant output can carry. The orange demand rises and falls above it, and the short evening peak near 19:00 is what fast-responding plants have to cover.

## Risks Across the Full Energy Chain

The effects of a non-renewable source begin **before the power plant** and continue after energy production.

| Risk | What to check |
| :--- | :------------ |
| Limited stock | Check whether use depends on reserves that become harder to obtain. |
| Combustion emissions | Check exhaust gases such as carbon dioxide and air pollutants. |
| Extraction impact | Check how mining and drilling affect water, soil, and nearby habitats. |
| Nuclear waste | Check radioactive material storage and reactor safety standards. |

Carbon dioxide is written as <InlineMath math="\text{CO}_2" />. Natural gas consists mainly of methane, written as <InlineMath math="\text{CH}_4" />. [Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/energy-and-the-environment/where-greenhouse-gases-come-from.php).

> Using a non-renewable source draws down a stock that returns very slowly. Whether that choice is appropriate depends on its purpose, effects, and available alternatives.

Assess each source by its stock, conversion path, useful energy, waste, and effects on communities and ecosystems.
