## Writing Numbers with Many Zeros

Physics often uses values that are too large or too small to write comfortably. An area of <InlineMath math="0.000764 \text{ m}^2" /> is still readable. Writing the approximate electron mass in kilograms is harder: after the decimal point, write <InlineMath math="30" /> zeros, then the digits <InlineMath math="911" />. It is easy to lose count.

**Scientific notation** writes a value as a coefficient multiplied by a power of <InlineMath math="10" />.

<BlockMath math="a \times 10^n" />

For positive values, the standard form is:

<BlockMath math="1 \le a < 10 \quad \text{and} \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}" />

The coefficient <InlineMath math="a" /> contains the significant figures. The exponent <InlineMath math="n" /> sets the order of magnitude.

The electron mass above can now be written more compactly:

<BlockMath math="9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}" />

## Moving the Decimal Point without Changing the Value

Move the decimal point until the coefficient is between <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="10" />. The number and direction of the moves determine the exponent.

| Ordinary value | Decimal movement | Scientific notation |
| :------------- | :--------------- | :------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="7\,640\,000 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> places left | <InlineMath math="7.64 \times 10^6 \text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0.000764 \text{ m}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> places right | <InlineMath math="7.64 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0.000000911 \text{ kg}" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> places right | <InlineMath math="9.11 \times 10^{-7} \text{ kg}" /> |

If <Highlight>the decimal point moves left</Highlight>, the exponent is positive. If the decimal point moves right, the exponent is negative.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
7\,640\,000 &= 7.64 \times 10^6 \\
0.000764 &= 7.64 \times 10^{-4}
\end{aligned}" />

Here every value sits at its exponent <InlineMath math="n" /> across and its coefficient <InlineMath math="a" /> up. Values in standard form land between the two gray lines, because <InlineMath math="1 \le a < 10" />. Now find the pink arrow: <InlineMath math="10.0\times10^{-13}" /> from the multiplication at the end of this lesson sits on the top line, outside the band, and one decimal move takes it inside as <InlineMath math="1.0\times10^{-12}" />.

## Significant Figures in the Coefficient

In scientific notation, read the **significant figures from the coefficient**. The power of <InlineMath math="10" /> sets the scale.

| Scientific notation | Significant figures | Reason |
| :------------------ | :------------------ | :----- |
| <InlineMath math="7.64 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | The coefficient is <InlineMath math="7.64" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | The zero after the decimal point in <InlineMath math="1.0" /> is intentional |
| <InlineMath math="1 \times 10^3 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | The coefficient is only <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1.000 \times 10^3 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | The zeros in <InlineMath math="1.000" /> express precision |

Scientific notation records the intended precision of a measurement. The value <InlineMath math="1000 \text{ m}" /> does not state whether its trailing zeros are significant, while <InlineMath math="1.000 \times 10^3 \text{ m}" /> explicitly has <InlineMath math="4" /> significant figures.

## Keep Guard Digits Until the Final Report

Scientific notation should not force an early rounding step. Calculate and convert with guard digits, then **round the final reported value once**.

For example, a bottle cap with diameter <InlineMath math="3.12 \text{ cm}" /> has the following calculated area when the calculator value of <InlineMath math="\pi" /> is retained:

<BlockMath math="A=\frac{1}{4}\pi(3.12 \text{ cm})^2=7.645379881\ldots \text{ cm}^2" />

Use the exact power-of-ten conversion to the International System of Units (SI) before discarding those guard digits.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1 \text{ cm} &= 10^{-2} \text{ m} \\
1 \text{ cm}^2 &= (10^{-2} \text{ m})^2 = 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2 \\
7.645379881\ldots \text{ cm}^2 &= 7.645379881\ldots \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2
\end{aligned}" />

The measured diameter supports <InlineMath math="3" /> significant figures, so the final SI result is <InlineMath math="7.65 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2" />.

> Be careful with powered units. The conversion factor is powered too. From <InlineMath math="\text{cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="\text{m}" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="10^{-2}" />, but from <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^2" /> to <InlineMath math="\text{m}^2" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="10^{-4}" />.

Powered units need their conversion factor powered too. Follow the chain for the cap's area.

## Calculations with Scientific Notation

In scientific notation, **multiply the coefficients and add the exponents** of <InlineMath math="10" /> separately.

Suppose a very small object has length <InlineMath math="2.5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}" /> and width <InlineMath math="4.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A &= (2.5 \times 10^{-6})(4.0 \times 10^{-7}) \text{ m}^2 \\
&= (2.5 \times 4.0) \times 10^{-6+(-7)} \text{ m}^2 \\
&= 10.0 \times 10^{-13} \text{ m}^2 \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-12} \text{ m}^2
\end{aligned}" />

The last step does two jobs. The coefficient <InlineMath math="10.0" /> is not in standard form because it is not less than <InlineMath math="10" />, and both measured lengths carry only <InlineMath math="2" /> significant figures. We therefore report <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 10^{-12}" />.

NIST publishes the official value-writing rules in [SP 811 Chapter 7](https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-chapter-7-rules-and-style-conventions-expressing-values) and the unit-conversion factors in [SP 811 Appendix B](https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-appendix-b-conversion-factors).
