## Variation in Repeated Measurements

When one object is measured several times, the results can differ slightly. That is normal. A hand can press the tool with a different force, an eye can read the scale from a slightly different angle, and the object can shift a little.

**Measurement uncertainty** is a number equal to or greater than zero that shows how widely measured values may be spread, based on the available data and information. For repeated measurements, a Type A evaluation calculates an uncertainty component from the observed results.

The sample standard deviation <InlineMath math="s" /> describes how far individual readings spread around their mean. The standard uncertainty of the mean describes the uncertainty in the mean estimated from those readings.

> Repeated measurements show random variation through small changes between readings. Systematic errors, such as a tool that does not start at zero or a damaged scale, must be checked through inspection or calibration.

## Mean of Repeated Measurements

The diameter of one bottle cap is measured <InlineMath math="5" /> times with a vernier caliper. Every reading uses the same unit, <InlineMath math="\text{cm}" />.

| Data | Diameter |
| :--- | :------- |
| <InlineMath math="d_1" /> | <InlineMath math="3.12 \text{ cm}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="d_2" /> | <InlineMath math="3.14 \text{ cm}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="d_3" /> | <InlineMath math="3.15 \text{ cm}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="d_4" /> | <InlineMath math="3.11 \text{ cm}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="d_5" /> | <InlineMath math="3.14 \text{ cm}" /> |

For repeated data with a roughly symmetric spread and no clear outlier, the mean is an appropriate representative value.

<BlockMath math="\bar d=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i}{N}" />

For the data above:

Add the numerical readings in centimetres first:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} S &amp;=3.12+3.14+3.15 \\ &amp;\quad{}+3.11+3.14 \\ &amp;=15.66 \end{aligned}" />

  <BlockMath math="\bar d=\frac{15.66}{5}\text{ cm}=3.132\text{ cm}" />
</MathContainer>

The mean is not enough. We also need to know <Highlight>whether the data cluster tightly around the mean</Highlight> or spread farther away.

## Data Spread Around the Mean

For repeated data, sample spread can be calculated from the **distance between each reading and the mean**.

<BlockMath math="s_d=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(d_i-\bar d)^2}{N-1}}" />

The denominator <InlineMath math="N-1" /> is used because the sample mean <InlineMath math="\bar d" /> is calculated from the same data. The deviations from the mean must sum to zero, so only <InlineMath math="N-1" /> deviations can vary independently.

Sum the squared numerical deviations first:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} V &amp;=(-0.012)^2+(0.008)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+(0.018)^2+(-0.022)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+(0.008)^2 \\ &amp;=0.00108 \end{aligned}" />

  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;s_d=\sqrt{\frac V4}\text{ cm} \\ &amp;=\sqrt{0.00027}\text{ cm} \\ &amp;\approx0.0164\text{ cm} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

If we report the diameter with the spread of the repeated data, the result becomes:

<BlockMath math="d=(3.13 \pm 0.02)\text{ cm}" />

The value <InlineMath math="3.13 \text{ cm}" /> represents the data. The value <InlineMath math="0.02 \text{ cm}" /> reminds the reader that the repeated readings were not exactly the same.

Here is the whole calculation from raw readings to the two spreads.

## Data Spread and Uncertainty of the Mean

Some repeated-measurement exercises use the compact algebraic form below.

Abbreviate the two sums without changing the formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="S_1=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i,\quad S_2=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2" />

  <BlockMath math="\Delta x=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sqrt{\frac{NS_2-S_1^2}{N-1}}" />
</MathContainer>

That formula is equivalent to the sample standard deviation:

<BlockMath math="\Delta x=s_x=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\bar x)^2}{N-1}}" />

So the <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\sqrt N}" /> in the compact form does not automatically mean the result is the formal uncertainty of the mean.

In formal metrology, NIST and the JCGM GUM describe the standard uncertainty of the mean as:

<BlockMath math="u(\bar x)=\frac{s_x}{\sqrt N}" />

For independent readings with the same spread, the **standard uncertainty of the mean** diameter is smaller than the standard deviation of individual readings:

<BlockMath math="u(\bar d)=\frac{\sqrt{0.00027}}{\sqrt 5}\text{ cm}\approx0.00735\text{ cm}" />

The scene below puts the five readings side by side. Each teal stem is one reading's distance from the mean, drawn <InlineMath math="100" /> times larger. The orange lines at <InlineMath math="\pm s_d" /> show how far single readings spread, while the closer violet lines at <InlineMath math="\pm u(\bar d)" /> show how well the mean itself is known. Try adding the stems above the gray line and the ones below it: they cancel, because deviations from the mean always sum to zero.

<LineEquation
  title="Five Readings around Their Mean"
  description="Each stem is a reading minus the mean, in units of 0.01 cm. Orange lines mark the spread s, violet lines the uncertainty of the mean u."
  data={(() => {
    // Measured diameters in cm. The vertical axis shows d_i - mean in units of 0.01 cm.
    const readings = [3.12, 3.14, 3.15, 3.11, 3.14];
    const count = readings.length;
    const mean = readings.reduce((sum, d) => sum + d, 0) / count;
    const spread = Math.sqrt(readings.reduce((sum, d) => sum + (d - mean) ** 2, 0) / (count - 1));

For a measurement problem, follow the notation defined in its statement. If <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> denotes the compact formula above, then <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> is the sample standard deviation of the individual measurements. Do not confuse this spread with the standard uncertainty of the mean <InlineMath math="u(\bar x)=s_x/\sqrt N" />.

The graph below keeps the same spread <InlineMath math="s_d" /> and asks what happens with more readings. The uncertainty of the mean falls fast at first and then more slowly, because it shrinks with <InlineMath math="\sqrt N" />: four times as many readings only halve it.

## Area Calculated from Diameter Data

Sometimes the measured data is diameter, but the required result is the area of the bottle cap. For a circle, the area is:

<BlockMath math="A=\frac{1}{4}\pi d^2" />

Because area depends on diameter, each diameter reading gives a slightly different area. Use the calculator value of <InlineMath math="\pi" /> and keep the **unrounded area values** during the calculation. The table displays only <InlineMath math="4" /> decimal places for readability.

| Data | Diameter | Area from the diameter |
| :--- | :------- | :--------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="3.12 \text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.6454 \text{ cm}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3.14 \text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.7437 \text{ cm}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="3.15 \text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.7931 \text{ cm}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="3.11 \text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.5964 \text{ cm}^2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="3.14 \text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="7.7437 \text{ cm}^2" /> |

The mean area is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \bar A&=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}A_i \\ &=\frac{38.5223661979\ldots}{5}\text{ cm}^2 \\ &\approx7.7045\text{ cm}^2 \end{aligned}" />

The area spread is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} s_A&=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{5}(A_i-\bar A)^2}{5-1}} \\ &=\sqrt{\frac{0.0260975114\ldots}{4}}\text{ cm}^2 \\ &\approx0.0808\text{ cm}^2 \end{aligned}" />

Using sample spread as the uncertainty convention for this exercise, the bottle-cap area can be written as follows.

<BlockMath math="A=(7.70 \pm 0.08)\text{ cm}^2" />

The displayed table is not the calculation input. Rounding intermediate area values can change the final spread, so the mean and standard deviation above use the unrounded values.

## Relative Spread as a Percentage of the Mean

Relative spread compares the **sample standard deviation** with the magnitude of the mean.

<BlockMath math="\varepsilon_r=\frac{s_x}{|\bar x|}\times 100\,\%" />

For the bottle cap area:

<BlockMath math="\varepsilon_r=\frac{0.0808}{7.7045}\times 100\,\%\approx 1.05\,\%" />

This means the sample spread is about <InlineMath math="1.05\,\%" /> of the mean area. Whether that is adequate depends on the intended use and target uncertainty, so the percentage should not be called "small enough" without a stated requirement.

[Type A evaluation](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-3-type-evaluation-standard-uncertainty) uses statistical analysis of repeated observations to estimate an uncertainty component. A reported measurement result should include enough information to state [how its uncertainty was evaluated](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/2071204/JCGM_100_2008_E.pdf).
