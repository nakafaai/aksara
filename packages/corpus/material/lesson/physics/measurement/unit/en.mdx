## Measurements Need a Common Reference

Imagine reading the quantity value <InlineMath math="12" /> without a unit. Is it <InlineMath math="12 \text{ cm}" />, <InlineMath math="12 \text{ m}" />, <InlineMath math="12 \text{ kg}" />, or <InlineMath math="12 \text{ s}" />? The number is the same, but the meaning can change completely.

A unit is <Highlight>an agreed reference size for a physical quantity</Highlight>. Measurements from different people, tools, and places can be compared only when their units are stated and converted when necessary.

A quantity value is the numerical value multiplied by the unit.

The numerical value answers *how much*. The unit answers *which reference it is compared with*.

Here three friends measure the same <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ m}" /> desk, each with their own pencil. Ayu's pencil is <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> long and fits <InlineMath math="8" /> times, Budi's <InlineMath math="19\text{ cm}" /> pencil about <InlineMath math="6.3" /> times, and Citra's <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" /> pencil about <InlineMath math="5.5" /> times. One desk, three numbers. Pick the metre rule, and everyone gets <InlineMath math="1.20\text{ m}" />.

## From Many Systems to SI

Before one standard became widely used, several unit systems existed side by side. Three names often appear in physics books:

| System | Example base units | How to read them |
| :----- | :----------------- | :--------------- |
| FPS | <InlineMath math="\text{ft},\ \text{lb},\ \text{s}" /> | *foot*, *pound-mass*, and *second* |
| CGS | <InlineMath math="\text{cm},\ \text{g},\ \text{s}" /> | centimeter, gram, and second |
| MKS | <InlineMath math="\text{m},\ \text{kg},\ \text{s}" /> | meter, kilogram, and second |

In this FPS row, <InlineMath math="\text{lb}" /> denotes pound-mass. It must not be confused with pound-force, commonly written <InlineMath math="\text{lbf}" />.

These differences make measurements hard to compare when the unit system is unclear. That is why modern science uses the **International System of Units** or **SI**. SI comes from the French name *Système international d'unités* and is known in English as the *International System of Units*. BIPM, or the *Bureau International des Poids et Mesures*, is the international organization that maintains the SI standard.

[BIPM defines SI](https://www.bipm.org/en/measurement-units) so the units share an international reference.

> SI is an agreement that <InlineMath math="1 \text{ m}" />, <InlineMath math="1 \text{ kg}" />, and <InlineMath math="1 \text{ s}" /> mean the same thing in different schools, laboratories, factories, and countries.

One object makes the point. The bowling ball below is the heaviest the rules allow: <InlineMath math="16.0\text{ lb}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.708\text{ ft}" /> across in FPS. In CGS it is <InlineMath math="7.26\times10^{3}\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="21.6\text{ cm}" />, and in MKS <InlineMath math="7.26\text{ kg}" /> and <InlineMath math="0.216\text{ m}" />. Switch systems: the needle and the ball never move, only the numbers change.

## The Seven SI Base Units

| Base quantity | Common symbol | SI unit | Unit symbol | Dimension |
| :------------ | :------------ | :------ | :---------- | :-------- |
| Length | <InlineMath math="\ell,\ x,\ r" /> | meter | <InlineMath math="\text{m}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{L}]" /> |
| Mass | <InlineMath math="m" /> | **kilogram** | <InlineMath math="\text{kg}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{M}]" /> |
| Time | <InlineMath math="t" /> | second | <InlineMath math="\text{s}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{T}]" /> |
| Electric current | <InlineMath math="I" /> | ampere | <InlineMath math="\text{A}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{I}]" /> |
| Thermodynamic temperature | <InlineMath math="T" /> | kelvin | <InlineMath math="\text{K}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\Theta]" /> |
| Amount of substance | <InlineMath math="n" /> | mole | <InlineMath math="\text{mol}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{N}]" /> |
| Luminous intensity | <InlineMath math="I_v" /> | candela | <InlineMath math="\text{cd}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{J}]" /> |

One detail often causes confusion: the SI base unit for mass is <InlineMath math="\text{kg}" />. Convert grams to kilograms when you need the full SI base unit form.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1 \text{ g} &= 10^{-3} \text{ kg} \\
250 \text{ g} &= 250 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg} = 0.25 \text{ kg}
\end{aligned}" />

## Derived Units Come from Operations on Base Units

A derived unit appears when a derived quantity is formed from base quantities. If the formula multiplies or divides quantities, the **units are multiplied or divided too**.

| Derived quantity | Quantity formula | SI unit | Dimension |
| :--------------- | :--------------- | :------ | :-------- |
| Area | <InlineMath math="A = l \times w" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{m}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{L}]^2" /> |
| Volume | <InlineMath math="V = l \times w \times h" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{m}^3" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{L}]^3" /> |
| Density | <InlineMath math="\rho = \frac{m}{V}" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{kg/m}^3" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L}]^{-3}" /> |
| Speed | <InlineMath math="v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{T}]^{-1}" /> |
| Acceleration | <InlineMath math="a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{T}]^{-2}" /> |
| Force | <InlineMath math="F = m \times a" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{N}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{T}]^{-2}" /> |
| Work | <InlineMath math="W = F \times \Delta s" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{J}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L}]^2[\mathrm{T}]^{-2}" /> |
| Power | <InlineMath math="P = \frac{W}{t}" /> | <InlineMath math="\text{W}" /> | <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L}]^2[\mathrm{T}]^{-3}" /> |

The work row assumes a constant force parallel to the displacement. The speed, acceleration, and power formulas in the table give averages over the chosen interval.

Turn the cube below. Along each edge you count <InlineMath math="10" /> small cubes of <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}" />, so the big cube is <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> on every side. Its volume is <InlineMath math="(10\text{ dm})^3=1000\text{ dm}^3" />, so <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}^3=1000\text{ dm}^3" />, and one small cube holds exactly <InlineMath math="1" /> liter. Notice that the prefix gets cubed together with the unit: <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}=10^{-1}\text{ m}" />, so <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}^3=10^{-3}\text{ m}^3" />.

A derived unit can be broken down into base units. For example, force uses the unit newton, and a newton is built from kilogram, meter, and second.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
F &= m \times a \\
1 \text{ N} &= 1 \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2 \\
&= 1 \text{ kg m s}^{-2}
\end{aligned}" />

Once force is clear, work and power can be read step by step.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
1 \text{ J} &= 1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m}
= 1 \text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2} \\
1 \text{ W} &= \frac{1 \text{ J}}{1 \text{ s}}
= 1 \text{ kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-3}
\end{aligned}" />

Each derived unit is a product of powers of kg, m and s. Rotate the bars: the joule is a newton times a meter, so only the power of m grows, and the watt is a joule per second, so the power of s drops to <InlineMath math="-3" />.

## Reading Prefixes in Calculations

An SI prefix can be attached to a **base unit or a derived unit**. For example, kilometer uses meter, while kilowatt uses watt. For example, <InlineMath math="\text{km}" /> means one thousand meters, so it remains a unit of length based on the meter.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
7.5 \text{ km}
&= 7.5 \times 10^3 \text{ m} \\
&= 7500 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

For small sizes, a prefix replaces a string of decimal zeros with a named power of <InlineMath math="10" />. A diameter of <InlineMath math="0.10 \ \mu\text{m}" /> is equal to:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
0.10 \ \mu\text{m}
&= 0.10 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m} \\
&= 1.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

Scientific notation separates the electron mass into a coefficient and its scale:

<BlockMath math="9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}" />

For a unit such as <InlineMath math="\text{cm}" />, <InlineMath math="\text{mg}" />, or <InlineMath math="\mu\text{m}" />, first separate the prefix from the base unit. Then replace the prefix with its power of <InlineMath math="10" /> and continue the calculation.

Each prefix is a power of ten, so moving between prefixes is a step along one exponent line. From kilometers to millimeters is <InlineMath math="3-(-3)=6" /> steps, so <InlineMath math="1\text{ km}=10^{6}\text{ mm}" />. Zoom along the line and count the steps.
