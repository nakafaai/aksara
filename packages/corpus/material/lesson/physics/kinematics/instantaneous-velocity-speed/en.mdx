## A Reading at One Instant

Instantaneous speed is the speed of an object at a particular time or at a particular point on its path. A common example is the number shown by a vehicle speedometer when the driver looks at it.

Instantaneous velocity is also read at a particular time, but it still includes direction. If the vehicle is moving right, the velocity is positive; if it is moving left, the velocity is negative.

> An instantaneous value answers, "How fast right now?" An average value covers a longer part of the trip.

<BlockMath math="\text{instantaneous speed}=\lvert\vec{v}_{\text{instant}}\rvert" />

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity.

If position is a differentiable function of time, instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocity as the sampling interval shrinks to zero.

<BlockMath math="v_x(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=\frac{dx}{dt}" />

Real instruments estimate this value over a short but finite sampling interval. A shorter interval can better approximate the value at that instant when the motion varies smoothly.

## Reading Instant Motion

The measuring point in the simulation represents the place where motion is read at one instant. The speed gives the magnitude of the reading, while the car's direction as it passes the measuring point sets the sign of its velocity.

At <InlineMath math="t=6 \text{ s}" />, the car moves rightward with an instantaneous speed of <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m/s}" />. Because rightward is chosen as the positive direction, the instantaneous velocity is <InlineMath math="+20 \text{ m/s}" />.

At <InlineMath math="t=9 \text{ s}" />, the car moves leftward with an instantaneous speed of <InlineMath math="8 \text{ m/s}" />. Because leftward is opposite the positive direction, the instantaneous velocity is <InlineMath math="-8 \text{ m/s}" />.

| Time | Speed reading | Direction of motion | Instantaneous velocity |
| :--- | :------------ | :------------------ | :--------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="6 \text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m/s}" /> | rightward | <InlineMath math="+20 \text{ m/s}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="9 \text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="8 \text{ m/s}" /> | leftward | <InlineMath math="-8 \text{ m/s}" /> |

So instantaneous speed is always written as an unsigned magnitude, while instantaneous velocity uses a sign to show direction.

## Not the Trip Average

Instantaneous speed is different from average speed. Average speed uses the whole distance and the whole time, while instantaneous speed reads the state at one time.

If a speedometer shows <InlineMath math="15 \text{ m/s}" /> right now, it does not mean the vehicle moved at <InlineMath math="15 \text{ m/s}" /> for the whole trip.

For example, a vehicle may stop at a red light and then move quickly afterward. Its average speed can be small because the whole trip time is counted, while its instantaneous speed while moving can be much larger.

## Speedometer Reads One Moment

A posted limit of <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km/h}" /> applies to the vehicle at every moment. A roadside detector therefore reads the instantaneous speed as the vehicle passes.

That is why a detector does not need to wait for the vehicle to reach its destination. It checks the speed at the moment the vehicle passes the measuring point.

The word "instantaneous" therefore means that we read the motion at one particular time.
