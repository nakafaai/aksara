## Measuring Speed at a Single Instant

An instantaneous value captures motion at **one exact moment** instead of averaging over a long stretch. As a sampling interval shrinks toward zero, the average slope settles onto the instantaneous velocity.

### Measuring Speed Over a Shrinking Time Interval

Instantaneous speed is the speed of an object at a particular time or at a particular point on its path. A common example is the number shown by a vehicle speedometer when the driver looks at it.

Instantaneous velocity is also measured at a particular time, but it includes direction. When **rightward is chosen as positive**, rightward motion gives a positive velocity and leftward motion gives a negative velocity.

> An instantaneous value answers, "How fast right now?" An average value covers a longer part of the trip.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{instantaneous speed}=\lvert\vec{v}_{\text{instant}}\rvert \end{aligned}" />

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity.

If position is a differentiable function of time, instantaneous velocity is <Highlight>the limit of average velocity</Highlight> as the sampling interval shrinks to zero.

<BlockMath math="v_x(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=\frac{dx}{dt}" />

Real instruments estimate this value over a short but finite sampling interval. A shorter interval can better approximate the value at that instant when the motion varies smoothly.

## Direction Changes Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity always pairs **how fast** with **which way**. Both reversing along a line and curving around a circle change the velocity even when the speed stays untouched.

### Direction Sets the Sign of Instantaneous Velocity

The measuring point in the simulation records the car at one instant. Speed gives the magnitude of its motion. The **direction of travel** sets the sign of its velocity.

At <InlineMath math="t=6 \text{ s}" />, the car moves rightward with an instantaneous speed of <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m/s}" />. Because rightward is chosen as the positive direction, the instantaneous velocity is <InlineMath math="+20 \text{ m/s}" />.

At <InlineMath math="t=9 \text{ s}" />, the car moves leftward with an instantaneous speed of <InlineMath math="8 \text{ m/s}" />. Because leftward is opposite the positive direction, the instantaneous velocity is <InlineMath math="-8 \text{ m/s}" />.

| Time | Speed reading | Direction of motion | Instantaneous velocity |
| :--- | :------------ | :------------------ | :--------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="6 \text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m/s}" /> | rightward | <InlineMath math="+20 \text{ m/s}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="9 \text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="8 \text{ m/s}" /> | leftward | <InlineMath math="-8 \text{ m/s}" /> |

Instantaneous speed has no direction sign. Instantaneous velocity uses a positive or negative sign to show direction along the chosen axis.

A speedometer gives only the size. Add the direction as a sign: rightward is positive and leftward is negative, so the same kind of reading becomes two different velocities. Zoom in on both readings.

## Contrasting Instantaneous and Average Speed

Average speed condenses a whole trip into one number, whereas instantaneous speed **can rise and fall every second**. During stops or free fall, the two rarely match.

### Difference from Average Speed

Average speed summarizes the whole trip by dividing total distance by total time. Instantaneous speed gives the speed at **one chosen time**.

If a speedometer shows <InlineMath math="15 \text{ m/s}" /> right now, it does not mean the vehicle moved at <InlineMath math="15 \text{ m/s}" /> for the whole trip.

For example, a vehicle may stop at a red light and then move quickly afterward. Its average speed can be small because the whole trip time is counted, while its instantaneous speed while moving can be much larger.

### A Falling Ball Never Keeps One Speed

A ball dropped from <InlineMath math="1.8\text{ m}" /> falls for <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ s}" />, giving an average speed of:

<BlockMath math="\bar{v}=\frac{1.8\text{ m}}{0.6\text{ s}}=3\text{ m/s}" />

Its **instantaneous speed never stays at that average**, because it grows by <InlineMath math="1\text{ m/s}" /> every tenth of a second. Slide the drop time <InlineMath math="d" /> to compare the rising line with the flat average.

## Reading Speed on Roads and Wheels

Speedometers and bike computers turn real motion into **instantaneous speed readings**. What matters is how fast a vehicle passes one detector or how quickly a wheel completes one turn.

### A Speedometer Measures the Current Speed

A posted limit of <InlineMath math="80 \text{ km/h}" /> applies to the vehicle at **every moment**. A roadside detector therefore reads the instantaneous speed as the vehicle passes.

A vehicle passing at <InlineMath math="90 \text{ km/h}" /> exceeds that limit even if its average speed for the whole trip is only <InlineMath math="60 \text{ km/h}" />. Time spent stopped at a red light lowers the trip average but does not change the reading at the detector.

### How a Bike Computer Reads Speed

A bike computer counts how often a magnet on the wheel passes a sensor. The speed of a point on the wheel is **its circle divided by the time for one turn**. The valve sits on the rim at <InlineMath math="0.311\text{ m}" />, while a spoke reflector sits halfway out at <InlineMath math="0.155\text{ m}" />. Adjust the turn period <InlineMath math="p" /> to compare both speeds.
