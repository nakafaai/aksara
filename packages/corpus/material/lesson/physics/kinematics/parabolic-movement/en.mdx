## Two Motions in One Path

Projectile motion is the motion of an object thrown at an angle or horizontally, so that its path curves. The curve comes from **two motions happening at the same time**: horizontal motion at constant velocity and vertical motion accelerated downward by gravity. With air resistance ignored, the two motions follow these equations:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x(t)=x_0+v_0\cos\theta\ t" />
  <BlockMath math="y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\ t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2" />
  <BlockMath math="v_x=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="v_0" /> is the initial speed, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> the launch angle above the horizontal, <InlineMath math="g" /> the gravitational acceleration, and <InlineMath math="t" /> the time since launch. The horizontal launch direction and upward are taken as positive. The ball path simulation keeps the same initial speed for several angles, so you can compare their ranges and times of flight.

## Gravity Changes Only the Vertical Motion

Gravity pulls downward, so only the vertical velocity changes. The horizontal velocity <InlineMath math="v_x" /> **stays the same along the whole path**, because there is no horizontal acceleration.

| Part of the motion | What happens | Acceleration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays constant | Zero |
| Vertical while rising | <InlineMath math="v_y" /> positive and shrinking | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |
| At the top | <InlineMath math="v_y = 0" />, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> unchanged | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |
| Vertical while falling | <InlineMath math="v_y" /> negative and growing in size | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |

The object does not stop at the top. Its vertical component is zero, but it is still moving horizontally at <InlineMath math="v_x" />. That vertical component behaves exactly like an object thrown upward in [vertical motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/vertical-movement), and the horizontal component like [uniform linear motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/uniform-linear-motion).

## The Range Depends on the Time in the Air

The range <InlineMath math="R" /> is the horizontal distance from the launch point to the landing point. Because the horizontal motion has constant velocity, the range is **the horizontal velocity times the time in the air**:

<BlockMath math="R = v_x \, t_{\text{air}}" />

The time in the air comes from the vertical motion alone. Of two balls with the same <InlineMath math="v_x" />, the one that stays in the air longer lands farther away. Of two balls that stay in the air equally long, the one with the larger <InlineMath math="v_x" /> lands farther away.

## A Ball Rolling Off a Table

An object launched horizontally has zero initial vertical velocity, so its vertical motion is **free fall**. Suppose a ball rolls horizontally off the edge of a table <InlineMath math="1.25\text{ m}" /> high with <InlineMath math="v_x = 4\text{ m/s}" />. With <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />, the fall time comes from the vertical motion:

<BlockMath math="1.25 = \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 = 0.25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 0.5\text{ s}" />

The range comes from the horizontal motion during that same time:

<BlockMath math="R = v_x t = 4(0.5) = 2\text{ m}" />

Check with the vertical equation: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}(10)(0.5)^2 = 1.25\text{ m}" />, the height of the table. A ball dropped from rest at the same table edge also reaches the floor after <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ s}" />, because the horizontal velocity does not change how long the fall takes.

## Launch Angle and Range

For an object launched from the ground that lands on the ground, the launch angle splits the initial speed into a horizontal part and a vertical part. A small angle gives a large <InlineMath math="v_x" /> but a short time in the air. A large angle gives a long time in the air but a small <InlineMath math="v_x" />. The longest range comes at **an angle of** <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, where the two parts are equal.

With <InlineMath math="v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />, throws at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> land at the same point, while a <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> throw goes farther:

| Angle | <InlineMath math="v_x" /> | Time in the air | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2}\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{3}\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" /> |

For the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> throw, every quantity comes from the two components:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 20\cos30^\circ = 10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y} = 20\sin30^\circ = 10\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="T = \frac{2 \times 10}{10} = 2\text{ s},\qquad R = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> throw: <InlineMath math="v_x = 10\text{ m/s}" /> and <InlineMath math="T = 2\sqrt{3}\text{ s}" />, so <InlineMath math="R = 10 \times 2\sqrt{3} = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />, the same range.

Each time in the air is twice the rise time <InlineMath math="\frac{v_0 \sin\theta}{g}" />. The <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> throw stays up longer but moves sideways more slowly, so its range equals that of the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> throw. All three ranges also come straight from the formula <InlineMath math="R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}" /> used in [projectile motion formulas](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/parabolic-movement-analysis). The largest value of <InlineMath math="\sin 2\theta" />, which is <InlineMath math="1" />, comes at <InlineMath math="\theta = 45^\circ" />.

## Common Mistakes

Before you calculate, **separate the horizontal and vertical motions**, and link them only through time.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thinking the velocity is zero at the top | Only <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is zero, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays | The speed at the top equals <InlineMath math="v_x" /> |
| Putting gravity into the horizontal motion | Gravity acts only downward | The horizontal acceleration must be zero |
| Finding the time from the horizontal motion | The time in the air comes from the vertical motion | The time must fit the <InlineMath math="y" /> equation |
| Thinking a ball thrown horizontally falls more slowly | The horizontal velocity does not change the vertical motion | Compare with a ball dropped from the same height |

The first mistake shows when you write a speed of zero at the top. The object is still moving sideways there, so its speed equals <InlineMath math="v_x" />.

## Practice on Projectile Motion

Use <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />. For each problem, first find **the time in the air from the vertical motion**.

1. A ball rolls off a table <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> high with a horizontal velocity of <InlineMath math="3\text{ m/s}" />. Find the fall time and how far it lands from the point on the floor directly below the table edge.

2. A stone is thrown horizontally at <InlineMath math="15\text{ m/s}" /> from a cliff <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> high. Find the horizontal distance it travels before reaching the bottom and its speed at that moment.

3. Two balls are launched from the ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />, one at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and one at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Which ball stays in the air longer, and which lands farther away?

### Answers

**Problem 1**. The vertical motion is **free fall** from a height of <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="0.8 = \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 = 0.16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 0.4\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = 3(0.4) = 1.2\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="5(0.4)^2 = 0.8\text{ m}" />, the height of the table.

**Problem 2**. The fall time from <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{\frac{2 \times 20}{10}} = 2\text{ s}" />. The horizontal distance and **both velocity components** at the bottom are:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R = 15(2) = 30\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 15\text{ m/s},\qquad v_y = -10(2) = -20\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{15^2 + 20^2} = 25\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the third vertical equation: <InlineMath math="v_y^2 = 2(10)(20) = 400" />, so <InlineMath math="\lvert v_y \rvert = 20\text{ m/s}" />.

**Problem 3**. The time in the air is **twice the rise time**, <InlineMath math="T = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="T_{30^\circ} = \frac{2(20)(0.5)}{10} = 2\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="T_{60^\circ} = \frac{2(20)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)}{10} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46\text{ s}" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> ball stays in the air longer. Its range is <InlineMath math="10 \times 2\sqrt{3} = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />, the same as the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> ball's range of <InlineMath math="10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />. So both balls land at the same point.
