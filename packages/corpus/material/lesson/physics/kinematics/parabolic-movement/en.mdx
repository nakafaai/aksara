## Two Motions Combined in One Curved Path

Projectile motion is the motion of an object thrown at an angle or horizontally, so that its path curves. The curve comes from **two motions happening at the same time**: horizontal motion at constant velocity and vertical motion accelerated downward by gravity. With air resistance ignored, the two motions follow these equations:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x(t)=x_0+v_0\cos\theta\ t" />
  <BlockMath math="y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\ t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2" />
  <BlockMath math="v_x=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt" />
</MathContainer>

> Horizontal motion and vertical motion unfold independently and connect only through the shared time in the air.

Here <InlineMath math="v_0" /> is the initial speed, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> the launch angle above the horizontal, <InlineMath math="g" /> the gravitational acceleration, and <InlineMath math="t" /> the time since launch. The horizontal launch direction and upward are taken as positive. The ball path simulation keeps the same initial speed for several angles, so you can compare their ranges and times of flight.

## Gravity Changes Only the Vertical Motion

Gravity pulls downward, so only the vertical velocity changes. The horizontal velocity <InlineMath math="v_x" /> **stays the same along the whole path**, because there is no horizontal acceleration.

| Part of the motion | What happens | Acceleration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays constant | Zero |
| Vertical while rising | <InlineMath math="v_y" /> positive and shrinking | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |
| At the top | <InlineMath math="v_y = 0" />, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> unchanged | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |
| Vertical while falling | <InlineMath math="v_y" /> negative and growing in size | <InlineMath math="-g" /> |

The object does not stop at the top. Its vertical component is zero, but it is still moving horizontally at <InlineMath math="v_x" />. That vertical component behaves exactly like an object thrown upward in [vertical motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/vertical-movement), and the horizontal component like [uniform linear motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/uniform-linear-motion).

Here is a demonstration of that idea. A launcher aims straight at a can, and the can is released the moment the ball leaves. **Without gravity the ball would hit the can where it hung**, and gravity lowers both by the same amount.

Before you calculate, **separate the horizontal and vertical motions**, and link them only through time.

| Misconception | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thinking velocity is zero at the top | Only <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is zero, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays | The speed at the top equals <InlineMath math="v_x" /> |
| Putting gravity into horizontal motion | Gravity acts only downward | Horizontal acceleration must be zero |
| Finding time from horizontal motion | Time in the air comes from vertical motion | Time must fit the <InlineMath math="y" /> equation |
| Thinking a horizontal throw falls slower | Horizontal velocity does not alter falling | Compare with a ball dropped from the same height |

You catch the first error right away whenever you write a speed of zero at the peak. The object is still gliding sideways there, so its speed equals <InlineMath math="v_x" />.

## How Time in the Air Controls Range

Range balances two competing effects: **steady horizontal velocity** and **airtime set by vertical motion**. Multiplying those two numbers determines how far the projectile lands.

### Linking Components Through Flight Time

The range <InlineMath math="R" /> is the horizontal distance from the launch point to the landing point. Because the horizontal motion has constant velocity, the range is **the horizontal velocity times the time in the air**:

<BlockMath math="R = v_x \, t_{\text{air}}" />

The time in the air comes from the vertical motion alone. Of two balls with the same <InlineMath math="v_x" />, the one that stays in the air longer lands farther away. Of two balls that stay in the air equally long, the one with the larger <InlineMath math="v_x" /> lands farther away.

/>

Once the vertical equation fixes the flight time <InlineMath math="T" />, one multiplication by <InlineMath math="v_x" /> delivers the full horizontal range.

## Horizontal Launches and Launch Angles

A ball rolling off a table and a ball launched upward from level ground obey the exact same rules. One starts with **zero vertical speed**, while the other splits initial speed by angle.

### A Ball Rolling Off a Table Matches Free Fall

An object launched horizontally has zero initial vertical velocity, so its vertical motion is **free fall**. Suppose a ball rolls horizontally off the edge of a table <InlineMath math="1.25\text{ m}" /> high with <InlineMath math="v_x = 4\text{ m/s}" />. With <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />, the fall time and range are:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="1.25 = \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 = 0.25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 0.5\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = v_x t = 4(0.5) = 2\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking with the vertical equation recovers the exact height of the table:

<BlockMath math="h = \frac{1}{2}(10)(0.5)^2 = 1.25\text{ m}" />

A ball dropped from rest at the same table edge also reaches the floor after <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ s}" />. Slide time <InlineMath math="d" /> below to verify that both balls match height at every instant.

### Complementary Angles and Maximum Range

For an object launched from the ground that lands on the ground, the launch angle splits the initial speed into a horizontal part and a vertical part. A small angle gives a large <InlineMath math="v_x" /> but a short time in the air. A large angle gives a long time in the air but a small <InlineMath math="v_x" />. The longest range comes at **an angle of** <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, where the two parts are equal.

With <InlineMath math="v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />, throws at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> land at the same point, while a <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> throw goes farther:

| Angle | <InlineMath math="v_x" /> | Time in the air | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2}\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{3}\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" /> |

Working out the <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> launches confirms their matching range:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 20\cos30^\circ = 10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y} = 20\sin30^\circ = 10\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="T_{30^\circ} = \frac{2 \times 10}{10} = 2\text{ s},\qquad R_{30^\circ} = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="T_{60^\circ} = 2\sqrt{3}\text{ s},\qquad R_{60^\circ} = 10 \times 2\sqrt{3} = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Each time in the air is twice the rise time, and all three ranges come straight from the [projectile motion formulas](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/parabolic-movement-analysis):

<BlockMath math="T = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g},\qquad R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}" />

The largest value of <InlineMath math="\sin 2\theta" />, which is <InlineMath math="1" />, occurs at <InlineMath math="\theta = 45^\circ" />.

## Worked Projectile Scenarios

These worked examples follow the **same two-step order**: find the time in the air from the vertical equation using <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />, then calculate horizontal reach and impact speed.

### Horizontal Drops from Tables and Cliffs

Suppose a ball rolls off a table <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> high with horizontal velocity <InlineMath math="3\text{ m/s}" />. Its vertical motion is **free fall**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="0.8 = \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 = 0.16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 0.4\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = 3(0.4) = 1.2\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking <InlineMath math="5(0.4)^2" /> confirms the exact <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ m}" /> table height.

Next, a stone is thrown horizontally at <InlineMath math="15\text{ m/s}" /> from a cliff <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> high. Computing fall time, horizontal reach, and **both velocity components** at impact gives:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 20}{10}} = 2\text{ s},\qquad R = 15(2) = 30\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 15\text{ m/s},\qquad v_y = -10(2) = -20\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{15^2 + 20^2} = 25\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking with the third vertical equation confirms the vertical speed at impact:

<BlockMath math="v_y^2 = 2(10)(20) = 400 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lvert v_y \rvert = 20\text{ m/s}" />

### Comparing Thirty and Sixty Degree Launches

Two balls are launched from level ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />, one at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and one at <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Each time in the air is **twice the rise time**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="T = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}" />
  <BlockMath math="T_{30^\circ} = \frac{2(20)(0.5)}{10} = 2\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="T_{60^\circ} = \frac{2(20)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)}{10} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46\text{ s}" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> ball stays in the air longer, yet comparing both ranges shows they land together:

<BlockMath math="R_{60^\circ} = 10 \times 2\sqrt{3} = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m},\qquad R_{30^\circ} = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />

Both balls therefore strike the ground at the exact same distance of about <InlineMath math="34.6\text{ m}" />.
