## One Ball with Two Motions at Once

Projectile motion is motion along a curved path because an object moves horizontally while also moving up and down vertically. In this lesson, air resistance is ignored, so the only acceleration is gravity downward.

Analyze the motion by separating its horizontal and vertical components. The horizontal component follows uniform linear motion because its velocity stays constant. The vertical component follows uniformly accelerated motion because gravity keeps changing its velocity.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x(t)=x_0+v_0\cos\theta\ t" />
  <BlockMath math="y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\ t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2" />
  <BlockMath math="v_x=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt" />
</MathContainer>

Here, <InlineMath math="v_0" /> is the initial speed, <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the launch angle from the horizontal, <InlineMath math="g" /> is the magnitude of gravitational acceleration, and <InlineMath math="t" /> is the time since launch. The coordinates <InlineMath math="x_0" /> and <InlineMath math="y_0" /> are the launch position. Setting both to zero gives the shorter formulas often used in examples.

## Gravity Changes Only the Vertical Part

Imagine a ball leaving a ramp at an angle. Its horizontal part keeps moving forward at the same velocity, while its vertical part slows down on the way up and speeds up on the way down.

> In projectile motion without air resistance, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays constant, but <InlineMath math="v_y" /> changes every moment.

This sentence prevents a common mistake. The object does not lose its horizontal motion at the top. Only the vertical velocity becomes zero there.

## The Ball Still Moves at the Peak

At the top of the path, <InlineMath math="v_y=0" /> because the ball stops for an instant in its up-and-down motion. However, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> is still present, so the ball still moves forward.

That is why the path does not stop at the top. After the top, gravity makes <InlineMath math="v_y" /> negative, which means the ball starts moving downward. At the same time, <InlineMath math="v_x" /> still carries the ball forward.

| Part of the motion | What happens | Motion type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays constant | Zero horizontal acceleration |
| Vertical while rising | Positive <InlineMath math="v_y" /> decreases to zero | Constant acceleration <InlineMath math="-g" /> |
| Vertical while falling | <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is negative and its magnitude grows | Constant acceleration <InlineMath math="-g" /> |

## Range Comes from Time in the Air

Horizontal range, usually written as <InlineMath math="R" />, is the horizontal distance from launch point to landing point. Because the horizontal motion has constant velocity, range is horizontal velocity multiplied by time in the air.

<BlockMath math="R=v_x t_{\text{air}}" />

If two balls have the same <InlineMath math="v_x" />, the one that stays in the air longer lands farther away. If the air time is the same, the ball with the larger <InlineMath math="v_x" /> has the larger range.

## A Ball Leaves a Table Horizontally

Suppose a ball leaves the edge of a table horizontally with <InlineMath math="v_x=4\text{ m/s}" /> and the table height is <InlineMath math="1.25\text{ m}" />. With <InlineMath math="g=10\text{ m/s}^2" />, the fall time comes from the vertical motion:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
h&=\frac{1}{2}gt^2\\
1.25&=\frac{1}{2}(10)t^2\\
t^2&=0.25\\
t&=0.5\text{ s}
\end{aligned}" />

The horizontal range is:

<BlockMath math="R=v_x t=4(0.5)=2\text{ m}" />

So the ball lands <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> from the foot of the table. Notice that fall time is determined by the vertical motion, while horizontal distance is determined by the horizontal motion during that time.
