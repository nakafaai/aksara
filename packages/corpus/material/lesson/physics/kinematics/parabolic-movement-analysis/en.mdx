## Projectile Formulas from Two Components

Analyzing projectile motion starts by **splitting the initial velocity** <InlineMath math="v_0" />, launched at an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> above the horizontal, into two components. For an object launched from the ground that lands on the ground, every important quantity follows from those components:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g},\qquad h_{\text{max}}=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}" />
  <BlockMath math="T=2t_{\text{top}},\qquad R=v_{0x}T=\frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" />
</MathContainer>

Air resistance is ignored and gravity is constant. Right and up are positive, and the examples on this page use <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />. The cannonball simulation shows the constant horizontal component and the changing vertical component for several shots.

## Time to the Top and Maximum Height

At the top, the object is still moving horizontally, but **its vertical velocity is zero**. Setting <InlineMath math="v_y = v_{0y} - gt = 0" /> gives the time to the top, and the third vertical equation gives the maximum height:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g}" />
  <BlockMath math="0 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g\,h_{\text{max}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_{\text{max}}=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}" />
</MathContainer>

Both formulas use only the vertical component. That component moves like an object thrown upward at <InlineMath math="v_{0y}" /> in [vertical motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/vertical-movement).

Keep the launch speed and change only the angle. **A steeper launch spends more time rising, so it climbs higher**, because the vertical component <InlineMath math="v_0\sin\theta" /> grows.

## Time of Flight and Range

If the object lands at **the same height** it was launched from, the time down equals the time up, so the total time of flight is <InlineMath math="T = 2t_{\text{top}}" />. The range is the horizontal component times the time of flight:

<BlockMath math="R = v_0\cos\theta \cdot \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \cdot 2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" />

The last step uses the identity <InlineMath math="2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta" />. Because <InlineMath math="\sin 2\theta" /> is at most <InlineMath math="1" />, the longest range comes at <InlineMath math="\theta = 45^\circ" />. Two angles that add up to <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, such as <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />, give the same range because <InlineMath math="\sin 60^\circ = \sin 120^\circ" />. With <InlineMath math="v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />, both land <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" /> away, the same result that time of flight and horizontal velocity give in [projectile motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/parabolic-movement). This formula does not hold when the landing point is higher or lower than the launch point.

## Velocity Along the Path

For the velocity at time <InlineMath math="t" />, find **both components at that time**, then combine them with the Pythagorean theorem:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = v_{0x},\qquad v_y = v_{0y} - gt" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}" />
</MathContainer>

The direction of the velocity above the horizontal is <InlineMath math="\alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(v_y, v_x)" />. For <InlineMath math="v_x > 0" />, this is the same as <InlineMath math="\tan\alpha = \frac{v_y}{v_x}" />. A positive <InlineMath math="v_y" /> means the object is moving up at a slant, and a negative one means it is moving down.

## A Sixty Degree Cannonball

A ball is fired at <InlineMath math="40\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Because <InlineMath math="\sin 60^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ" /> have **exact values**, every result can be written without rounding until the last step.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x}=40\cos60^\circ=20\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y}=40\sin60^\circ=20\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{20\sqrt{3}}{10}=2\sqrt{3}\approx 3.46\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}}=\frac{(20\sqrt{3})^2}{2 \times 10}=\frac{1200}{20}=60\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R=20 \times 4\sqrt{3}=80\sqrt{3}\approx 138.6\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check the range with the direct formula: <InlineMath math="\frac{40^2\sin 120^\circ}{10} = \frac{1600 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}{10} = 80\sqrt{3}" />.

The velocity at <InlineMath math="t = 2\text{ s}" /> uses the vertical component at that time:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_y = 20\sqrt{3} - 10(2) = 20\sqrt{3} - 20 \approx 14.64\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{20^2 + (20\sqrt{3} - 20)^2} = \sqrt{2000 - 800\sqrt{3}} \approx 24.8\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(20\sqrt{3} - 20, 20) \approx 36.2^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check the speed: <InlineMath math="v^2 = 20^2 + 14.64^2 \approx 614.4" />, and <InlineMath math="\sqrt{614.4} \approx 24.8" />.

At <InlineMath math="t = 2\text{ s}" /> the ball is still rising because <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is positive, and that time is indeed earlier than the time to the top, <InlineMath math="3.46\text{ s}" />.

## A Forty Five Degree Kick

A ball is kicked from the ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />. At this angle **the two components are equal**, because <InlineMath math="\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x} = v_{0y} = 20 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}} = \frac{10\sqrt{2}}{10} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\text{ s},\qquad h_{\text{max}} = \frac{(10\sqrt{2})^2}{20} = 10\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = 10\sqrt{2} \times 2\sqrt{2} = 40\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the direct formula: <InlineMath math="\frac{20^2 \sin 90^\circ}{10} = 40\text{ m}" />. This range is longer than a <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> or <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> throw at the same speed, which both land at <InlineMath math="20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" />.

## Common Mistakes

Before using the short formulas, check **whether the landing point is as high as the launch point**.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the whole <InlineMath math="v_0" /> for the time to the top | The time to the top depends only on <InlineMath math="v_{0y}" /> | The time formula must contain <InlineMath math="\sin\theta" /> |
| Swapping sine and cosine | The horizontal component uses cosine, the vertical uses sine | At a small angle, <InlineMath math="v_{0x}" /> must be larger |
| Using <InlineMath math="R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" /> for a cliff or a roof | That formula holds only when the landing point is as high as the launch point | Compare the starting and ending heights |
| Finding the direction with <InlineMath math="\tan^{-1}" /> while ignoring the sign | A downward direction gives a negative angle | The sign of the angle must match the sign of <InlineMath math="v_y" /> |

The sine and cosine mistake is easiest to catch with a <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> angle. A low throw must have a larger horizontal component than vertical component.

## Practice on Projectile Formulas

Use <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />. For each problem, first write **both components of the initial velocity**.

1. A ball is launched from the ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />. Find the time to the top, the maximum height, the time of flight, and the range.

2. A projectile is fired from the ground at <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle whose sine is <InlineMath math="0.8" /> and whose cosine is <InlineMath math="0.6" />. Find its maximum height and range.

3. For the projectile in Problem 2, find its speed and the direction of its velocity at <InlineMath math="t = 1\text{ s}" />.

4. A ball is kicked at <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> and lands <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> away on level ground. Find its initial speed.

### Answers

**Problem 1**. The components are <InlineMath math="v_{0x} = 20\cos30^\circ = 10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}" /> and **the vertical component** <InlineMath math="v_{0y} = 20\sin30^\circ = 10\text{ m/s}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}} = \frac{10}{10} = 1\text{ s},\qquad h_{\text{max}} = \frac{10^2}{20} = 5\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="T = 2\text{ s},\qquad R = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{20^2 \sin 60^\circ}{10} = 20\sqrt{3}" />.

**Problem 2**. The components are <InlineMath math="v_{0x} = 30(0.6) = 18\text{ m/s}" /> and <InlineMath math="v_{0y} = 30(0.8) = 24\text{ m/s}" />. The time to the top is <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ s}" />, so **the time of flight** is <InlineMath math="4.8\text{ s}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}} = \frac{24^2}{20} = 28.8\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = 18 \times 4.8 = 86.4\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the direct formula: <InlineMath math="\sin 2\theta = 2(0.8)(0.6) = 0.96" />, and <InlineMath math="\frac{30^2 \times 0.96}{10} = 86.4\text{ m}" />.

**Problem 3**. At <InlineMath math="t = 1\text{ s}" />, the horizontal component is unchanged and **the vertical component drops** by <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 18\text{ m/s},\qquad v_y = 24 - 10(1) = 14\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{18^2 + 14^2} = \sqrt{520} \approx 22.8\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \tan^{-1}\frac{14}{18} \approx 37.9^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

The angle is positive and smaller than the launch angle, because the projectile is still rising, but <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is shrinking while <InlineMath math="v_x" /> stays the same, so its path keeps flattening.

**Problem 4**. At <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, <InlineMath math="\sin 90^\circ = 1" />, so the range formula becomes **very simple**:

<BlockMath math="40 = \frac{v_0^2 \times 1}{10} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_0^2 = 400 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />

A speed cannot be negative, so <InlineMath math="v_0 = -20" /> is rejected. Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{20^2}{10} = 40\text{ m}" />.
