## Projectile Formulas from Two Components

Analyzing projectile motion starts by **splitting the initial velocity** <InlineMath math="v_0" />, launched at an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> above the horizontal, into two components. For an object launched from the ground that lands on the ground, every important quantity follows from those components:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta,\qquad v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g},\qquad h_{\text{max}}=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}" />
  <BlockMath math="T=2t_{\text{top}},\qquad R=v_{0x}T=\frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" />
</MathContainer>

> Every projectile formula grows out of just two independent velocity components linked by one shared flight time.

Air resistance is ignored and gravity is constant. Right and up are positive, and the examples on this page use <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />. The cannonball simulation shows the constant horizontal component and the changing vertical component for several shots.

## Peak Height and Level Ground Range

On level ground, **vertical rise time** and **horizontal velocity** determine every headline quantity of the flight. Before applying the shortcut range formula, always confirm that launch and landing sit at the same height.

### Time to the Top and Maximum Height

At the top, the object is still moving horizontally, but **its vertical velocity is zero**. Setting the vertical velocity to zero gives the rise time, and the third vertical equation gives the maximum height:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_y = v_{0y} - gt = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g}" />
  <BlockMath math="0 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g\,h_{\text{max}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_{\text{max}}=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}" />
</MathContainer>

Both formulas use only the vertical component. That component moves like an object thrown upward at <InlineMath math="v_{0y}" /> in [vertical motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/vertical-movement).

Hold launch speed fixed at <InlineMath math="22\text{ m/s}" /> and vary only launch angle <InlineMath math="a" />. **A steeper launch spends more time rising, so it climbs higher**, because <InlineMath math="v_0\sin a" /> grows. Slide <InlineMath math="a" /> below to track peak height and rise time.

Both peak time and peak height depend strictly on <InlineMath math="v_0\sin\theta" />:

<BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g},\qquad h_{\max}=\frac{(v_0\sin\theta)^2}{2g}" />

That is why the steepest launch rises highest and stays airborne longest.

### Time of Flight and Level Ground Range

If the object lands at **the same height** it was launched from, the time down equals the time up, so the total time of flight is <InlineMath math="T = 2t_{\text{top}}" />. The range is the horizontal component times the time of flight:

<BlockMath math="R = v_0\cos\theta \cdot \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \cdot 2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" />

The last step uses the double-angle identity:

<BlockMath math="2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta" />

Because <InlineMath math="\sin 2\theta" /> is at most <InlineMath math="1" />, the longest range comes at <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />. Two angles adding to <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, such as <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />, share the same double-angle sine and land at about <InlineMath math="34.6\text{ m}" /> when launched at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />, matching [projectile motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/parabolic-movement). This shortcut does not hold when launch and landing heights differ.

### Verifying Formula Conditions Before Calculating

Before using the short formulas, check **whether the landing point is as high as the launch point**.

| Slip | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using full <InlineMath math="v_0" /> for peak time | Peak time depends only on <InlineMath math="v_{0y}" /> | The time formula must contain <InlineMath math="\sin\theta" /> |
| Swapping sine and cosine | Horizontal uses cosine, vertical uses sine | At a small angle, <InlineMath math="v_{0x}" /> must be larger |
| Using level-ground range for a cliff | That formula requires equal start and end height | Compare launch and landing heights first |
| Dropping the sign on <InlineMath math="\tan^{-1}" /> | Downward motion gives a negative angle | The angle sign must match the sign of <InlineMath math="v_y" /> |

The sine and cosine slip is easiest to catch at <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />. A low throw must always have a larger horizontal component than vertical component.

### Instantaneous Speed and Angle Along a Trajectory

For the velocity at time <InlineMath math="t" />, find **both components at that time**, then combine them with the Pythagorean theorem:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = v_{0x},\qquad v_y = v_{0y} - gt" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2},\qquad \alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(v_y, v_x)" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="v_x > 0" />, the direction angle matches the inverse tangent of <InlineMath math="v_y" /> over <InlineMath math="v_x" />. A positive <InlineMath math="v_y" /> means the object is moving up at a slant, and a negative one means it is moving down.

## Worked Trajectories at Sixty and Forty Five Degrees

Two classic launch angles put every component formula to work side by side: <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> preserves **exact radicals** through rise time, peak height, and instantaneous velocity, while <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> balances both components for maximum level-ground range.

### A Sixty Degree Cannonball

A ball is fired at <InlineMath math="40\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Because <InlineMath math="\sin 60^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ" /> have **exact values**, every result can be written without rounding until the last step.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x}=40\cos60^\circ=20\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y}=40\sin60^\circ=20\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}}=\frac{20\sqrt{3}}{10}=2\sqrt{3}\approx 3.46\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}}=\frac{(20\sqrt{3})^2}{2 \times 10}=\frac{1200}{20}=60\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R=20 \times 4\sqrt{3}=80\sqrt{3}\approx 138.6\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking the range with the direct formula confirms the exact radical result:

<BlockMath math="R = \frac{40^2\sin 120^\circ}{10} = \frac{1600 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}{10} = 80\sqrt{3}\text{ m}" />

The velocity at <InlineMath math="t = 2\text{ s}" /> uses the vertical component at that time:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_y = 20\sqrt{3} - 10(2) = 20\sqrt{3} - 20 \approx 14.64\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{20^2 + (20\sqrt{3} - 20)^2} = \sqrt{2000 - 800\sqrt{3}} \approx 24.8\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(20\sqrt{3} - 20, 20) \approx 36.2^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Checking the speed from squared components confirms the value:

<BlockMath math="v^2 = 20^2 + 14.64^2 \approx 614.4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v \approx 24.8\text{ m/s}" />

At <InlineMath math="t = 2\text{ s}" /> the ball is still rising because <InlineMath math="v_y" /> is positive, and that time is earlier than the time to the top, <InlineMath math="3.46\text{ s}" />.

### Maximum Range at Forty Five Degrees

A ball is kicked from the ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />. At this angle **the two components are equal**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x} = v_{0y} = 20 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}} = \frac{10\sqrt{2}}{10} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\text{ s},\qquad h_{\text{max}} = \frac{(10\sqrt{2})^2}{20} = 10\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="R = 10\sqrt{2} \times 2\sqrt{2} = 40\text{ m},\qquad \frac{20^2 \sin 90^\circ}{10} = 40\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

This range beats a <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> or <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> throw at the same speed, both of which land near <InlineMath math="34.6\text{ m}" />.

## Applied Projectile Formula Calculations

For each calculation using <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" />, write **both components of the initial velocity** first. A ball launched from the ground at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> and <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> gives these **components, flight time, and range**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x} = 20\cos30^\circ = 10\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y} = 20\sin30^\circ = 10\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{top}} = \frac{10}{10} = 1\text{ s},\qquad h_{\text{max}} = \frac{10^2}{20} = 5\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="T = 2\text{ s},\qquad R = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Next, a projectile fires from the ground at <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" /> with <InlineMath math="\sin\theta = 0.8" /> and <InlineMath math="\cos\theta = 0.6" />. Its peak time is <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ s}" />, so **the time of flight** is <InlineMath math="4.8\text{ s}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_{0x} = 30(0.6) = 18\text{ m/s},\qquad v_{0y} = 30(0.8) = 24\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}} = \frac{24^2}{20} = 28.8\text{ m},\qquad R = 18 \times 4.8 = 86.4\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="\sin 2\theta = 2(0.8)(0.6) = 0.96 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = \frac{30^2 \times 0.96}{10} = 86.4\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

For that same projectile with <InlineMath math="v_{0x}=18\text{ m/s}" /> and <InlineMath math="v_{0y}=24\text{ m/s}" />, at <InlineMath math="t = 1\text{ s}" /> the horizontal component is unchanged while **the vertical component drops**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_x = 18\text{ m/s},\qquad v_y = 24 - 10(1) = 14\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{18^2 + 14^2} = \sqrt{520} \approx 22.8\text{ m/s}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \tan^{-1}\frac{14}{18} \approx 37.9^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

The angle is positive and smaller than the launch angle because the projectile is still rising while <InlineMath math="v_y" /> shrinks and flattens the path.

Finally, if a ball kicked at <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> lands <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> away on level ground, <InlineMath math="\sin 90^\circ = 1" /> simplifies the range equation directly:

<BlockMath math="40 = \frac{v_0^2 \times 1}{10} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_0^2 = 400 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />

A speed cannot be negative, so the negative root is rejected, and substituting back reproduces the exact <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> range.
