## Position Requires a Reference Point

If you say, "I am <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> from the store," your friend can still ask, "which direction?" If you say, "I am east," your friend can still ask, "how far?"

In physics, **position** is an object's location in a chosen **frame of reference**. The frame provides a coordinate system and a positive direction. A store, house, flagpole, or start line can serve as the **reference point**, or origin, inside that frame.

> A complete position statement names the reference point, value, unit, and direction.

If motion happens along one straight line, we can choose one direction as positive. In the street example, east is positive and west is negative.

<BlockMath math="x'=x_{\text{object}}-x_{\text{origin}}" />

This equation keeps the object in place and shifts the coordinate origin while the axis and positive direction stay fixed.

## Position from Different References

Take one object at coordinate <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> when the store is used as zero. If the origin is shifted to Rani's house at coordinate <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m}" />, the object's new coordinate becomes <InlineMath math="+10 \text{ m}" />.

The **object does not move** when the origin changes. Only its coordinate changes. Shifting the origin to the right can turn a large positive coordinate into a smaller positive coordinate, zero, or a negative coordinate.

Each arrow starts at a different origin and ends at the object, so its label is the object's coordinate measured from that origin. ## Reading Sign and Direction

The sign of a position tells us <Highlight>which side of the reference point</Highlight> the object is on. In the street example, east is chosen as positive. So a positive value means the object is east of the reference point, while a negative value means the object is west of it.

| Origin in the same reference frame | Object coordinate on the street axis | Coordinate relative to the origin |
| :-------------- | :--------------------------------- | :----------------------- |
| Store at <InlineMath math="0 \text{ m}" /> | Object at <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="+30 \text{ m}" /> east |
| Rani's house at <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m}" /> | Object at <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="+10 \text{ m}" /> east |
| Naya's house at <InlineMath math="50 \text{ m}" /> | Object at <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="-20 \text{ m}" /> or <InlineMath math="20 \text{ m}" /> west |

The value <InlineMath math="-20 \text{ m}" /> does not mean the distance is negative. The negative sign only tells the direction relative to the reference point.

## A Complete Position Statement

A position sentence needs four parts. If one part is missing, another person can read the object's location from the wrong reference point.

<Mermaid
  title="Building a Position Statement"
  description="Follow this order so the object's position is not read from the wrong reference point."
  chart={`flowchart LR
    A["Observed object"] --> B["Origin and positive direction"]
    B --> C["Coordinate value and unit"]
    C --> D["Position relative to the origin"]`}
/>

A complete statement is: "The object is <InlineMath math="10 \text{ m}" /> east of Rani's house." It specifies the object, reference point, coordinate magnitude, unit, and direction.

The sentence "the object is near the house" names the house as a reference, but leaves the exact point on the house, numerical distance, unit, and direction unspecified. A **precise origin** lets two observers measure the object's coordinate from the same place.

In two dimensions a position statement needs an origin and two numbers. From <InlineMath math="O" /> the point <InlineMath math="P" /> sits at <InlineMath math="(3,2)" />, but from a second origin <InlineMath math="O'" /> at <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> it sits at <InlineMath math="\vec r\,'=\vec r-\vec r_0" />. ## A Position Sentence from the Store

A marker is at coordinate <InlineMath math="40 \text{ m}" /> when the store is the origin. Dimas's house is at coordinate <InlineMath math="30 \text{ m}" /> in the same frame. If the **origin is shifted** to Dimas's house, the marker's coordinate is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x'
&=x_{\text{marker}}-x_{\text{Dimas}} \\
&=40 \text{ m}-30 \text{ m} \\
&=+10 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

So, the marker is <InlineMath math="10 \text{ m}" /> east of Dimas's house. With the store as origin, its coordinate is <InlineMath math="40 \text{ m}" />. With Dimas's house as origin, its coordinate is <InlineMath math="+10 \text{ m}" />. Both statements are correct because they use different origins in a clearly defined reference frame.

Both arrows below end at the same marker. Only their starting point, the origin, changes, and with it the number in the position sentence.
