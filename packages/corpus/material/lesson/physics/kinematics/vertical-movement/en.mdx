## Vertical Motion and Gravitational Acceleration

Vertical motion is the motion of an object moving up or down along **a single vertical line**, with gravity as the only thing changing its velocity. Its acceleration is constant, with size <InlineMath math="g" /> and direction always downward. If up is chosen as positive, the position <InlineMath math="y" /> and velocity <InlineMath math="v_y" /> at time <InlineMath math="t" /> follow three equations:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="y=y_0+v_0t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2" />
  <BlockMath math="v_y=v_0-gt" />
  <BlockMath math="v_y^2=v_0^2-2g(y-y_0)" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="y_0" /> is the starting position and <InlineMath math="v_0" /> the initial velocity. Vertical motion is [motion with constant acceleration](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/non-uniform-linear-motion) where the acceleration is <InlineMath math="-g" />, so these are the same equations.

> These equations hold near Earth's surface with constant <InlineMath math="g" /> and no air resistance. The [standard value of gravitational acceleration](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/pdf/sp811.pdf) is <InlineMath math="g_n=9.80665\text{ m/s}^2" />. The calculations on this page use <InlineMath math="g=10\text{ m/s}^2" /> so the numbers come out round.

## Three Ways an Object Moves Vertically

All three kinds of vertical motion use the same equations. Only **the sign and size of the initial velocity** <InlineMath math="v_0" /> differ.

| Kind of motion | Initial velocity | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thrown upward | <InlineMath math="v_0 > 0" /> | A ball thrown straight up |
| Free fall | <InlineMath math="v_0 = 0" /> | A fruit falling from its stem |
| Thrown downward | <InlineMath math="v_0 < 0" /> | A stone thrown down from a bridge |

In all three, the acceleration stays <InlineMath math="-g" /> for the whole motion, including at the highest point. The minus sign comes from choosing up as positive, not from gravity changing direction.

## An Object Thrown Upward

When an object is thrown upward, its velocity starts positive and **keeps decreasing because of gravity** until it reaches zero at the highest point. Setting <InlineMath math="v_y = 0" /> gives the rise time and the maximum height above the launch point:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{rise}} = \frac{v_0}{g}" />
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}} = \frac{v_0^2}{2g}" />
</MathContainer>

Suppose a ball is thrown upward with <InlineMath math="v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}" />. Its rise time is <InlineMath math="\frac{20}{10} = 2\text{ s}" />, and its maximum height is <InlineMath math="\frac{20^2}{2 \times 10} = 20\text{ m}" />. The position equation gives the same result:

<BlockMath math="y = 20(2) - \frac{1}{2}(10)(2^2) = 40 - 20 = 20\text{ m}" />

At the highest point the ball's velocity is momentarily zero, but its acceleration is still <InlineMath math="-g" />. Substitute a few values of <InlineMath math="t" /> into <InlineMath math="y = 20t - 5t^2" /> and <InlineMath math="v_y = 20 - 10t" /> to follow the way up and down:

| Time | Height | Velocity | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="0\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="0\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> | Rising |
| <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> | Rising |
| <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="0\text{ m/s}" /> | Stopped for an instant |
| <InlineMath math="3\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="-10\text{ m/s}" /> | Falling |
| <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="0\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="-20\text{ m/s}" /> | Back at the launch point |

The motion is symmetric. The ball is at a height of <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> at <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> and at <InlineMath math="3\text{ s}" />, with the same speed but opposite directions, and it returns to the launch point with its starting speed.

## An Object in Free Fall

In school physics, free fall means an object released **with no initial velocity**, so <InlineMath math="v_0 = 0" />. Some books call any motion under gravity alone free fall, throws included, but this page uses the first meaning. For an object falling through a height <InlineMath math="h" />, the fall time and the speed on reaching the ground are:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{2gh}" />
</MathContainer>

A ball dropped from a height of <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> reaches the ground after <InlineMath math="t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 20}{10}} = 2\text{ s}" />. Its velocity at that moment is:

<BlockMath math="v_y = 0 - 10(2) = -20\text{ m/s}" />

The minus sign means the ball is moving downward. Check its speed with the second formula: <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 20} = 20\text{ m/s}" />. This is the same speed a ball thrown up at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> has when it gets back to its launch point, because both fall <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> from a moment of rest.

## An Object Thrown Downward

An object thrown downward already has a downward initial velocity, so **its initial velocity is negative**. Gravity adds to its speed from the start, and it reaches the ground sooner than an object dropped from the same height.

Suppose a stone is thrown downward at <InlineMath math="5\text{ m/s}" /> from a height of <InlineMath math="30\text{ m}" />. With <InlineMath math="y_0 = 30" /> and <InlineMath math="v_0 = -5" />, the stone reaches the ground when <InlineMath math="y = 0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="0 = 30 - 5t - 5t^2" />
  <BlockMath math="t^2 + t - 6 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t + 3)(t - 2) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="v_y = -5 - 10(2) = -25\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

The value <InlineMath math="t = -3" /> would mean three seconds before the stone was thrown, so it is rejected, and the stone lands after <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" />. Check with the third equation: <InlineMath math="v_y^2 = (-5)^2 + 2(10)(30) = 625" />, so the speed is <InlineMath math="25\text{ m/s}" />.

## Vertical Motion Inside Projectile Motion

Without air resistance, the same equations describe the vertical part of a ball thrown at an angle. In [projectile motion](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/parabolic-movement), **the horizontal velocity stays constant**, while the vertical velocity changes exactly as for an object thrown upward.

That is why the time a projectile spends in the air comes from its vertical motion alone. A ball kicked at an angle with an initial vertical velocity of <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> still rises for <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" /> and lands after <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" /> on level ground, just like a ball thrown straight up at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />.

## Common Mistakes

Before you calculate, fix **the positive direction** and use it throughout.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thinking the acceleration is zero at the highest point | Only the velocity is zero, gravity still acts | The acceleration is <InlineMath math="-g" /> throughout |
| Using <InlineMath math="h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2" /> for an object thrown upward | That formula holds only for free fall with <InlineMath math="v_0 = 0" /> | Check whether there is an initial velocity |
| Writing a positive initial velocity for a downward throw | Down is negative when up is chosen as positive | The sign of <InlineMath math="v_0" /> must match the direction of the throw |
| Mixing up velocity and speed | Velocity has a sign, speed is always positive | The velocity while falling must be negative |

Sign mistakes are easiest to catch at the end. An object moving down must have a negative <InlineMath math="v_y" />, and a negative time is always rejected.

## Practice on Vertical Motion

Use <InlineMath math="g = 10\text{ m/s}^2" /> and take up as positive. For each problem, first decide **the sign of the initial velocity** and the starting position.

1. A ball is thrown straight up at <InlineMath math="15\text{ m/s}" />. Find its rise time and maximum height.

2. A coconut falls from a tree <InlineMath math="45\text{ m}" /> high. Find the fall time and its speed when it hits the ground.

3. A ball is thrown upward at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> from the roof of a building <InlineMath math="25\text{ m}" /> high. When does it hit the ground, and what is its velocity then?

4. A stone is thrown downward at <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> from a bridge <InlineMath math="15\text{ m}" /> high. Find the time to reach the water and its speed.

### Answers

**Problem 1**. At the highest point **the velocity is zero**, so:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t_{\text{rise}} = \frac{15}{10} = 1.5\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="h_{\text{max}} = \frac{15^2}{2 \times 10} = 11.25\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the position equation: <InlineMath math="15(1.5) - 5(1.5)^2 = 22.5 - 11.25 = 11.25\text{ m}" />.

**Problem 2**. The coconut falls **with no initial velocity**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 45}{10}} = 3\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 45} = 30\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="v_y = 0 - 10(3) = -30\text{ m/s}" />, so the speed is <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" />.

**Problem 3**. Take the roof as <InlineMath math="y_0 = 25" />, so the ground is <InlineMath math="y = 0" />. The position equation gives **a quadratic in** <InlineMath math="t" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="0 = 25 + 20t - 5t^2" />
  <BlockMath math="t^2 - 4t - 5 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t - 5)(t + 1) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="v_y = 20 - 10(5) = -30\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

The negative time is rejected, so the ball hits the ground after <InlineMath math="5\text{ s}" /> at <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" /> downward. Check: <InlineMath math="v_y^2 = 20^2 + 2(10)(25) = 900" />.

**Problem 4**. The initial velocity is **negative**, <InlineMath math="v_0 = -10" />, and <InlineMath math="y_0 = 15" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="0 = 15 - 10t - 5t^2" />
  <BlockMath math="t^2 + 2t - 3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t + 3)(t - 1) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="v_y = -10 - 10(1) = -20\text{ m/s}" />
</MathContainer>

The stone reaches the water after <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> at a speed of <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />. Check: <InlineMath math="(-10)^2 + 2(10)(15) = 400 = 20^2" />.
