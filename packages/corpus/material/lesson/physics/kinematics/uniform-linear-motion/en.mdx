## Motion Without Changing Velocity

Uniform linear motion is motion along a straight path with <Highlight>constant velocity</Highlight>. Constant means the speed stays the same and the direction also does not change.

Picture a small rail cart moving on a straight track. If, during each <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> interval, it moves <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> and then another <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> during the next second, the motion is uniform linear motion.

> In uniform linear motion, the object may keep moving. What stays unchanged is its velocity.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\Delta x &= v\Delta t \\
x(t) &= x(t_0)+v(t-t_0)
\end{aligned}" />

The symbols <InlineMath math="x(t_0)" /> and <InlineMath math="x(t)" /> are the positions at the initial and final times, <InlineMath math="v" /> is the constant velocity, <InlineMath math="\Delta t=t-t_0" /> is elapsed time, and <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> is displacement.

## Position Marks With Equal Spacing

If an object's position is recorded at **equal time intervals**, uniform linear motion produces equally spaced marks. This is like a ticker tape: marks are made regularly, so the gaps between marks show how far the object moves during each time interval.

Suppose the initial position is <InlineMath math="x_0=1\text{ m}" /> and the velocity is <InlineMath math="+2\text{ m/s}" />. That means the position increases by <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> every second.

| Time | Position |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="0\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="7\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="9\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5\text{ s}" /> | <InlineMath math="11\text{ m}" /> |

The position value changes, while the difference between consecutive positions stays equal. From <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="3" />, from <InlineMath math="3" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />, and so on, the difference is always <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" />.

## Calculating Final Position

Suppose an object starts from <InlineMath math="x_0=1\text{ m}" /> and moves right with constant velocity <InlineMath math="v=2\text{ m/s}" /> for <InlineMath math="5\text{ s}" />. Its displacement is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\Delta x &= v\Delta t \\
&= 2(5) \\
&= 10\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

Its final position is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
x_t &= x_0+\Delta x \\
&= 1+10 \\
&= 11\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

This calculation works because the **velocity does not change**. If the velocity changes, we cannot use one fixed value of <InlineMath math="v" /> for the whole motion.

## Recognizing Uniform Motion on Graphs

Two graphs identify uniform linear motion. The velocity-time graph must be horizontal, while the position-time graph must be a straight line with **constant slope**.

| Graph | Shape in uniform linear motion | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Velocity against time | Horizontal line | Velocity is constant |
| Position against time | Slanted straight line | Position changes regularly |

The signed area between a velocity-time graph and the time axis equals displacement. For constant velocity, that area is a rectangle, so <InlineMath math="\Delta x=v\Delta t" />. An area below the time axis contributes negative displacement.

On a position-time graph, the slope of the line represents velocity. The sign of the slope gives direction, while a larger absolute steepness means a larger speed. For uniform linear motion, the line stays straight because the slope does not change from start to finish.

On a velocity-time graph the same motion is a flat line at <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" />. The area under it is the displacement, <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" />, which added to <InlineMath math="x_0=1\text{ m}" /> gives the final position <InlineMath math="11\text{ m}" />.

## Direction Distinguishes Constant Speed from Constant Velocity

Uniform linear motion needs constant velocity. Both the size and direction of motion must stay fixed. Constant speed only fixes the size.

A car moving with constant speed on a circular road is not in uniform linear motion, <Highlight>because the direction of its velocity keeps changing</Highlight>. A rail cart moving straight with constant speed and direction is in uniform linear motion.

### Constant Speed on a Curved Track

A runner who keeps <InlineMath math="8\text{ m/s}" /> around an athletics track has a constant speed, but **the velocity changes direction on every bend**, so the motion is not uniform linear motion.
