## Motion Depends on the Observer

Motion is always described <Highlight>relative to a [frame of reference](/en/subjects/physics/kinematics/reference-frame-position)</Highlight>. An object moving quickly past an observer beside the road can have a smaller, zero, or oppositely directed velocity relative to another moving observer.

For ordinary vehicle speeds and parallel, nonrotating axes, we use the Galilean velocity rule. If both velocities are first measured relative to the same ground frame <InlineMath math="G" />, the velocity of object <InlineMath math="X" /> relative to observer <InlineMath math="Y" /> is:

<BlockMath math="\vec{v}_{X/Y}=\vec{v}_{X/G}-\vec{v}_{Y/G}" />

The positive and negative signs record direction along the chosen axis.

> The reference frame changes the measured relative velocity. The physical event remains the same.

## Observer Velocity Changes Relative Velocity

An object's velocity according to an observer changes when the observer is also moving. The calculation must compare the **object's motion with the observer's motion**.

Suppose rightward is chosen as positive. A car moves at <InlineMath math="+12 \text{ m/s}" /> and a taxi moves at <InlineMath math="+14 \text{ m/s}" />. To a passenger in the taxi, the car appears to move backward:

<BlockMath math="v_{\text{car/taxi}}=12-14=-2 \text{ m/s}" />

The negative value means the relative motion points left.

When two vehicles move in the same direction, the faster observer can see the other vehicle as moving backward. The car still moves forward relative to the road, but its velocity relative to the taxi is negative.

## When the Observer Is Still

If the observer is stationary relative to the ground, relative velocity is the same as velocity relative to the ground.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;v_{\text{object/observer}} = v_{\text{object/ground}}-0 \\ &amp;= v_{\text{object/ground}} \end{aligned}" />

That is why, in many simple problems, a velocity with no extra frame stated is usually assumed to be relative to the ground.

An object can also be **at rest relative to a moving observer**. If the car and taxi both move right at <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" />, their velocity difference is zero:

<BlockMath math="v_{\text{car/taxi}}=12-12=0\text{ m/s}" />

## When Both Are Moving

If two objects move in **opposite directions**, their relative speed can be greater than either object's speed relative to the ground. For example, a car moves right at <InlineMath math="+12 \text{ m/s}" />, while a truck moves left at <InlineMath math="-8 \text{ m/s}" />.

To the truck driver, the car's velocity is:

<BlockMath math="v_{\text{car/truck}}=12-(-8)=+20 \text{ m/s}" />

The value <InlineMath math="+20 \text{ m/s}" /> means the car's position relative to the truck changes rightward at a relative speed of <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />. Whether the vehicles are approaching or separating also depends on which one is initially to the left.

Different observers assign different relative velocities to the same physical motion.

## Relative Velocity Is a Difference

| Situation | Calculation | Meaning of the **relative motion** |
| :-------- | :---------- | :----------------------------- |
| Observer is still | <InlineMath math="v_Y=0" /> | <InlineMath math="v_{X/Y}=v_X" /> |
| Observer is faster in the same direction | <InlineMath math="12-14=-2 \text{ m/s}" /> | The object appears to move in the negative direction |
| Observer moves in the opposite direction | <InlineMath math="12-(-8)=+20 \text{ m/s}" /> | The object appears to move in the positive direction |

So, before saying an object is fast, slow, still, or moving backward, always check the frame of reference.

The car, the taxi and the truck all come down to the same subtraction with a different observer.
