## Stopping Distance Includes Reaction and Braking Distance

Stopping distance is the total distance a moving object needs until it fully stops. For a vehicle, it <Highlight>starts before the brakes act</Highlight>.

Before braking begins, the driver still needs reaction time. During that time, the vehicle keeps moving forward with its initial speed.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{stop}}=d_{\text{reaction}}+d_{\text{brake}}" />
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{reaction}}=vt_r" />
</MathContainer>

So stopping distance has two stages. The first stage happens when the driver notices danger but the vehicle has not started braking. The second stage happens after the brakes act and the vehicle slows to a stop.

| Part | What Happens | What Determines It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reaction distance | The vehicle still moves with its initial speed | Speed and reaction time |
| Braking distance | The vehicle slows until it stops | Initial speed and deceleration magnitude |

## Braking Distance Uses Constant Deceleration

While the brakes act, the vehicle slows down. If deceleration is **treated as constant** and the final velocity is zero, a uniformly accelerated motion equation can be used to find the braking distance.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_t^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x" />
  <BlockMath math="0=v^2-2b\,d_{\text{brake}}" />
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{brake}}=\frac{v^2}{2b}" />
</MathContainer>

The quantity <InlineMath math="b" /> is the magnitude of the deceleration, so it is written as a positive value.

This formula uses a simple assumption: the path is straight, braking deceleration is treated as constant, and the vehicle does not skid. If the road condition changes, the value of <InlineMath math="b" /> can change too.

### The Road Surface Changes the Deceleration

The path stays straight, but if a car without ABS brakes as hard as it can, its wheels lock and the tyres slide straight along the road. Then the only force slowing the car is friction between tyre and road. Its size is <InlineMath math="\mu mg" />, where <InlineMath math="\mu" /> is the coefficient of friction. The car's mass appears on both sides, so the deceleration **depends only on the road**.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="mb=\mu mg" />
  <BlockMath math="b=\mu g" />
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{brake}}=\frac{v^2}{2\mu g}" />
</MathContainer>

The sliding friction coefficients in this table come from [Soica and Gheorghe's 2025 review of tyre and road interaction](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1702/13/11/1005). For wet asphalt the review gives a range from <InlineMath math="0.45" /> to <InlineMath math="0.6" />, and the table uses its lower end. The last column is worked out for <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> with <InlineMath math="g=9.8\text{ m/s}^2" />.

| Surface | Friction coefficient | Deceleration | Braking distance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dry asphalt | <InlineMath math="0.75" /> | <InlineMath math="7.35\text{ m/s}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="27.2\text{ m}" /> |
| Wet asphalt | <InlineMath math="0.45" /> | <InlineMath math="4.41\text{ m/s}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="45.4\text{ m}" /> |
| Gravel | <InlineMath math="0.55" /> | <InlineMath math="5.39\text{ m/s}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="37.1\text{ m}" /> |
| Packed snow | <InlineMath math="0.15" /> | <InlineMath math="1.47\text{ m/s}^2" /> | <InlineMath math="136.1\text{ m}" /> |

## Braking Distance Grows with the Square of Speed

Reaction distance is directly proportional to speed. If the speed doubles, the reaction distance also doubles for the same reaction time.

Braking distance is more sensitive because it contains <InlineMath math="v^2" />. For a fixed vehicle mass, kinetic energy <InlineMath math="K=\frac{1}{2}mv^2" /> also scales with the **square of speed**. The brakes and tire-road forces must remove that energy while bringing the vehicle to rest.

Suppose the reaction time is <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> and the braking deceleration magnitude is <InlineMath math="5\text{ m/s}^2" />.

| Speed | Reaction Distance | Braking Distance | Stopping Distance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" /> | <InlineMath math="30\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="90\text{ m}" /> | <InlineMath math="120\text{ m}" /> |

When speed doubles, reaction distance doubles from <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> to <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" />, while braking distance quadruples from <InlineMath math="10\text{ m}" /> to <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" />. Their sum therefore rises from <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> to <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" />.

The graph below adds the two parts for every speed up to <InlineMath math="30\text{ m/s}" />. The teal reaction distance grows in a straight line. The orange total bends upward because the braking part grows with <InlineMath math="v^2" />. At <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> the total is <InlineMath math="20+40=60\text{ m}" />.

### Motion Turns into Heat in the Brakes

Why does braking distance follow <InlineMath math="v^2" />? The brakes have to use up the car's kinetic energy, <InlineMath math="K=\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2" />. Friction between the pads and the disc turns that energy into **heat**, so the disc warms up. With a constant braking force, the energy used up equals force times distance, so braking distance scales with <InlineMath math="v^2" /> as well.

The specific heat of iron is <InlineMath math="449\text{ J/(kg K)}" /> from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics, and the discs start at <InlineMath math="30^\circ\text{C}" />.

## A Safe Gap Behind Another Vehicle

When the car ahead brakes hard, you only need to stop before you hit it, and that car keeps moving while it brakes too. If both cars brake equally hard, their braking distances are equal. The only difference is your reaction distance: for <InlineMath math="t_r" /> you're still at full speed while the car ahead is already slowing down.

Say you keep a time gap <InlineMath math="h" /> behind that car. At speed <InlineMath math="v" /> the starting gap is <InlineMath math="vh" />, and once both have stopped, what's left is

<BlockMath math="d_{\text{left}}=vh-vt_r=v\,(h-t_r)" />

So the gap runs out exactly when <InlineMath math="h<t_r" />, **whatever the speed**. That's why [the UK Highway Code in rule 126](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/general-rules-techniques-and-advice-for-all-drivers-and-riders-103-to-158) asks for at least a two-second gap on high-speed roads, at least double that on wet roads, and up to ten times as much on icy roads.

## Calculating Total Stopping Distance

A vehicle moves at <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" />. The driver's reaction time is <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" /> and the magnitude of the braking deceleration is <InlineMath math="5\text{ m/s}^2" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{reaction}}=20(1)=20\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{brake}}=\frac{(20\text{ m/s})^2}{2(5\text{ m/s}^2)}=\frac{400}{10}\text{ m}=40\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="d_{\text{stop}}=20+40=60\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Under those conditions, the model gives a stopping distance of <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" />.

That <InlineMath math="60\text{ m}" /> includes **reaction distance and braking distance**. The first <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> happens before braking starts, and the next <InlineMath math="40\text{ m}" /> happens while the vehicle is braking.

> This calculation shows how the formula works. A real stopping distance changes with perception-reaction time, road surface, tires, weather, grade, and braking performance. A [2004 FHWA stopping sight distance example](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/04091/03.cfm) uses a <InlineMath math="2.5\text{ s}" /> perception-reaction time and <InlineMath math="3.4\text{ m/s}^2" /> deceleration.
