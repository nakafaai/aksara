## Acceleration Measures Change in Velocity

When a car starts moving, your body may feel pushed backward. When the car brakes, your body may feel pushed forward. In both cases, the car's velocity changes.

In physics, average acceleration describes the <Highlight>change in velocity</Highlight> during a time interval. Since velocity has magnitude and direction, acceleration must also be read together with the positive direction we choose.

> A vehicle accelerates when it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction because each case changes its velocity.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\vec{a}_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t} \\
&= \frac{\vec{v}_t-\vec{v}_0}{t_t-t_0}
\end{aligned}" />

The unit of acceleration is <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}^2" />. If acceleration is constant at <InlineMath math="a=2\text{ m/s}^2" />, the velocity increases by <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" /> every <InlineMath math="1\text{ s}" />. In that special case, the average acceleration over any interval equals the acceleration at each instant.

## Observing Acceleration in Traces and Ramps

Motion traces, velocity graphs, and inclined ramps all reveal the same **velocity change each second**. Comparing growing gaps in space with slopes on a graph shows what constant acceleration looks like.

### From Ticker Tape to Graph Slope

A ticker timer marks a paper tape at **equal time intervals**. For motion along one straight line without reversing direction, wider gaps mean a larger speed, equal gaps mean constant speed, and narrower gaps mean a smaller speed. The gaps alone do not show the sign of velocity, so the chosen positive direction still matters.

## Reading Positive and Negative Acceleration

The sign of acceleration never tells direction of motion by itself. Instead, it tells **which way velocity changes**, so you always pair it with the sign of velocity to see whether speed grows or shrinks.

### Reading the Sign of Acceleration

The sign of acceleration cannot be read by itself. It always depends on the positive direction we choose. In this one-dimensional example, let the rightward direction be positive.

The table assumes **constant acceleration throughout the observed interval**.

| Acceleration sign | Velocity change | Reading with right as positive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="a>0" /> | <InlineMath math="v_t>v_0" /> | Velocity increases in the positive direction |
| <InlineMath math="a=0" /> | <InlineMath math="v_t=v_0" /> | Velocity stays constant |
| <InlineMath math="a<0" /> | <InlineMath math="v_t<v_0" /> | Velocity decreases if the object is still moving in the positive direction |

The last row is where many students trip up. Negative acceleration does not automatically mean the object is moving left. It means <Highlight>the velocity change points toward the negative side</Highlight> of the chosen axis.

Read the signs together. With <InlineMath math="v>0" />, an acceleration of the same sign makes the speed grow, the opposite sign makes it shrink, and <InlineMath math="a=0" /> keeps it the same. Zoom in on the three lines and compare their slopes.

## Calculating Constant and Average Acceleration

When acceleration stays steady, one number describes every instant. On a real commute with braking and stops, **the secant slope between two endpoints** captures the average acceleration across the interval.

### Calculating Velocity Change Each Second

Suppose a car changes velocity from <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" /> to <InlineMath math="18\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" />. Its average acceleration is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{v_t-v_0}{\Delta t} \\
&= \frac{18-2}{4} \\
&= 4\text{ m/s}^2
\end{aligned}" />

If acceleration is **constant throughout those four seconds**, velocity increases by <InlineMath math="4\text{ m/s}" /> each second. The sequence becomes <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" />, <InlineMath math="6\text{ m/s}" />, <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" />, and so on if the acceleration remains constant.

Because this example assumes constant acceleration, the same value applies at each instant. Now compare it with a car whose velocity changes from <InlineMath math="18\text{ m/s}" /> to <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
a_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{2-18}{4} \\
&= -4\text{ m/s}^2
\end{aligned}" />

The value <InlineMath math="-4\text{ m/s}^2" /> means the velocity decreases by <InlineMath math="4\text{ m/s}" /> each second if the car is still moving in the positive direction.

## Zero Acceleration Still Needs Interpretation

If acceleration is zero **throughout the observed interval**, the object's velocity does not change. That does not always mean the object is at rest. The object may still move with a constant velocity, such as <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> to the right.

A quick check is to look at both velocity and acceleration. If <InlineMath math="a=0" /> and <InlineMath math="v=0" />, the object is at rest. If <InlineMath math="a=0" /> but <InlineMath math="v\neq 0" />, the object moves in a straight line with constant velocity.
