## Equal Velocity Changes in Equal Time Intervals

Uniformly accelerated linear motion is motion along a straight path with constant acceleration. Constant acceleration means velocity <Highlight>changes by the same amount</Highlight> during each equal time interval.

If a car starts at <InlineMath math="0\text{ m/s}" /> and gains <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}" /> every second, its velocity becomes <InlineMath math="2" />, <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, and so on. The same increase repeats every second.

> In uniformly accelerated motion, acceleration stays constant while the velocity changes at a steady rate.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v_t=v_0+at" />
  <BlockMath math="\Delta x=v_0t+\frac{1}{2}at^2" />
  <BlockMath math="v_t^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x" />
</MathContainer>

Here, <InlineMath math="v_0" /> is initial velocity, <InlineMath math="v_t" /> is velocity after time <InlineMath math="t" />, <InlineMath math="a" /> is acceleration, and <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> is displacement.

## Position Marks No Longer Stay Evenly Spaced

In uniform linear motion, the position marks each second are equally spaced. In uniformly accelerated motion, those gaps can change. While an object keeps moving in the **same direction**, the gaps grow when its speed increases and shrink when its speed decreases. If the object reverses direction, the order and side of the marks must also be examined.

Look at the "From Rest" motion. At the first instant the car is not moving yet, then the gaps between marks become longer. That is the sign that velocity is increasing. The graph rises in a straight line because the acceleration is constant.

## Calculating Displacement from Graph Area

A velocity-time graph does more than show velocity. The **signed area** between the graph and the time axis represents displacement. In uniformly accelerated motion above the time axis, that area can be a triangle or a trapezoid. An area below the axis contributes negative displacement.

| Situation | Area shape on the graph | Displacement formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starts from rest | Triangle | <InlineMath math="\Delta x=\frac{1}{2}at^2" /> |
| Initial and final velocities are positive | Trapezoid | <InlineMath math="\Delta x=\frac{1}{2}(v_0+v_t)t" /> |
| Uses acceleration directly | Rectangle plus triangle | <InlineMath math="\Delta x=v_0t+\frac{1}{2}at^2" /> |

These formulas express the same relation: displacement is the signed area between the velocity-time graph and the time axis.

## Car Starts from Rest

A toy car starts from rest, then has constant acceleration <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}^2" /> for <InlineMath math="5\text{ s}" />.

Its final velocity is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
v_t &= v_0+at \\
&= 0+2(5) \\
&= 10\text{ m/s}
\end{aligned}" />

Its displacement is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\Delta x &= v_0t+\frac{1}{2}at^2 \\
&= 0(5)+\frac{1}{2}(2)(5^2) \\
&= 25\text{ m}
\end{aligned}" />

So after <InlineMath math="5\text{ s}" />, the car is moving at <InlineMath math="10\text{ m/s}" /> and has moved <InlineMath math="25\text{ m}" /> from its starting point.

The two results answer different questions. Final velocity gives the car's speed and direction at the end, while displacement gives its net change in position during the whole interval.

You can check the displacement using the **triangular area under the velocity-time graph**. Its base is the elapsed time and its height is the final velocity.

<BlockMath math="\Delta x=\frac{1}{2}(5\text{ s})(10\text{ m/s})=25\text{ m}" />

## Acceleration Sign Depends on the Positive Direction

In one-dimensional motion, positive and negative signs depend on the **positive direction we choose**. If rightward is positive, positive acceleration means the velocity change points rightward.

Negative acceleration does not automatically mean the object moves left. A car can still move right while having negative acceleration if it is braking. Its velocity points right, but its value gets smaller.
