## The Law of Universal Gravitation

**Newton's law of universal gravitation** says that the gravitational force between two bodies is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres:

<BlockMath math="F = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}" />

Here <InlineMath math="G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}" /> is the **universal gravitational constant**, <InlineMath math="m_1" /> and <InlineMath math="m_2" /> are the masses in kilograms, and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the distance between the centres in metres. The same force pulls an apple to the Earth and keeps the Moon in orbit. In these notes, you change the distance, climb from the Earth's centre out into space, and compare real satellite orbits.

The law applies to **every** body with mass, including you and your book. The force between two small bodies is extremely weak because <InlineMath math="G" /> is so small. Gravity only becomes noticeable when at least one body is huge, like a planet or a star.

### Example Force between the Earth and the Moon

The mass of the Earth is <InlineMath math="5.97 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}" />, the mass of the Moon is <InlineMath math="7.35 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}" />, and the distance between their centres is <InlineMath math="3.84 \times 10^{8}\text{ m}" />. Find **the gravitational force between the Earth and the Moon**.

<BlockMath math="F = \frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{(3.84 \times 10^{8})^2} = 1.98 \times 10^{20}\text{ N}" />

Check the order of magnitude: the numerator is about <InlineMath math="2.9 \times 10^{37}" /> and the denominator about <InlineMath math="1.5 \times 10^{17}" />, so the result is around <InlineMath math="2 \times 10^{20}" />. The force is the same size on the Earth and on the Moon, as Newton's third law says.

### Example Tripling the Distance

Two bodies attract each other with a force of <InlineMath math="36\text{ N}" />. The distance between their centres is made **three times as large**. The new force is:

<BlockMath math="F' = \frac{36}{3^2} = 4\text{ N}" />

Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="r = 3" />, the ratio <InlineMath math="F / F_0" /> is <InlineMath math="1/9" />, and <InlineMath math="36 \div 9 = 4" />.

## Gravitational Acceleration at and above the Surface

The gravitational force on a body of mass <InlineMath math="m" /> is its weight, <InlineMath math="mg" />. Set it equal to the law of gravitation, and <InlineMath math="m" /> cancels:

<BlockMath math="g = \frac{GM}{r^2}" />

So **gravitational acceleration doesn't depend on the mass of the falling body**. At the Earth's surface, <InlineMath math="r = R = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}" /> and <InlineMath math="g = 9.81\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. This model shows how <InlineMath math="g" /> changes from the Earth's centre to far out in space, treating the Earth as a sphere of uniform density. Inside the Earth, <InlineMath math="g" /> rises linearly, and outside, <InlineMath math="g" /> falls with the inverse square.

The International Space Station orbits about <InlineMath math="400\text{ km}" /> above the surface, at <InlineMath math="r = 6.77 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}" />:

<BlockMath math="g = \frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{(6.77 \times 10^{6})^2} = 8.69\text{ m s}^{-2}" />

## Satellites and the Laws of Kepler

A satellite stays in a circular orbit because gravity acts as the **centripetal force**. Set <InlineMath math="\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}" /> equal to <InlineMath math="\dfrac{mv^2}{r}" />, and the satellite's linear speed is:

<BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}" />

A satellite further out moves more slowly and takes longer for each orbit. Pick the three real satellites in this model and compare their radius, speed, period and acceleration.

**Kepler's third law** says that the square of the orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the orbital radius, <InlineMath math="T^2 \propto r^3" />. It follows from the satellite speed: <InlineMath math="T = 2\pi r / v" />, so <InlineMath math="T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM} r^3" />. Real planet data obey this law. With distances in astronomical units and periods in Earth years, <InlineMath math="T = r^{3/2}" />.

Kepler's first law says that every planet orbits the Sun in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. His second law says that the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so a planet moves faster when it's close to the Sun.

Now put the law to work. A **geostationary** satellite orbits once every <InlineMath math="24" /> hours, matching the Earth's rotation, so it seems fixed in the sky. Find its orbital radius.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{(3.98 \times 10^{14})(86{,}400)^2}{4\pi^2}" />
<BlockMath math="r = 4.22 \times 10^{7}\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

That's about <InlineMath math="36{,}000\text{ km}" /> above the surface. Check it with the orbit model: pick the geostationary satellite and read the same radius.

## Escape Velocity

**Escape velocity** is the minimum velocity a body at a planet's surface needs to escape the planet's gravity without any further thrust:

<BlockMath math="v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}" />

Escape velocity is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" /> times the orbital speed at the same distance. At the Earth's surface, the orbital speed is <InlineMath math="7.91\text{ km s}^{-1}" /> and the escape velocity is <InlineMath math="11.2\text{ km s}^{-1}" />. ## Common Mistakes about Gravitation

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Doubling the distance halves the force. | The force is inversely proportional to <InlineMath math="r^2" />, so it becomes a quarter. | Square the distance factor. |
| <InlineMath math="r" /> is the height above the surface. | <InlineMath math="r" /> is measured from the Earth's centre, so <InlineMath math="r = R + h" />. | Add the Earth's radius to the height. |
| Astronauts float because there's no gravity in orbit. | <InlineMath math="g" /> at the space station's orbit is still <InlineMath math="8.69\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. They're in free fall. | Calculate <InlineMath math="g" /> at the orbital distance. |
| A heavier satellite needs a higher orbital speed. | The satellite's mass cancels in <InlineMath math="v = \sqrt{GM/r}" />. | Check whether <InlineMath math="m" /> appears in the final formula. |

The second mistake is the most common one in calculation questions. **Always measure from the centre**, not from the surface.

## Practice on Gravitation

These questions start with a simple ratio and finish with a satellite. **Write <InlineMath math="r" /> in metres** before you substitute.

1. The gravitational force between two bodies is <InlineMath math="F" />. The distance between their centres is halved. What is the new force?

2. The mass of Mars is <InlineMath math="6.42 \times 10^{23}\text{ kg}" /> and its radius is <InlineMath math="3.39 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}" />. Calculate the gravitational acceleration at the surface of Mars.

3. The mass of the Moon is <InlineMath math="7.35 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}" /> and its radius is <InlineMath math="1.74 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}" />. Calculate the escape velocity from the Moon's surface.

4. A satellite orbits the Earth at a distance of <InlineMath math="2R" /> from the Earth's centre. Calculate its linear speed and its orbital period in hours.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The force is inversely proportional to <InlineMath math="r^2" />. If <InlineMath math="r" /> becomes <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2}r" />:

<BlockMath math="F' = \frac{F}{(1/2)^2} = 4F" />

Halving the distance makes the force **four times as large**.

**Solution 2**: Use <InlineMath math="g = GM/R^2" />:

<BlockMath math="g = \frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(6.42 \times 10^{23})}{(3.39 \times 10^{6})^2} = 3.73\text{ m s}^{-2}" />

Check: that's about <InlineMath math="38\%" /> of <InlineMath math="9.81\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, reasonable for a planet much smaller than the Earth.

**Solution 3**: Use the escape velocity formula:

<BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{1.74 \times 10^{6}}} = 2.37 \times 10^{3}\text{ m s}^{-1}" />

The Moon's escape velocity is <InlineMath math="2.37\text{ km s}^{-1}" />, about a fifth of the Earth's.

**Solution 4**: At <InlineMath math="r = 2R" />, the orbital speed is the surface value divided by <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v = \frac{7.91}{\sqrt{2}} = 5.59\text{ km s}^{-1}" />
<BlockMath math="T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi (1.274 \times 10^{7})}{5{,}590} = 1.43 \times 10^{4}\text{ s} = 3.98\text{ hours}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the speed model: at <InlineMath math="n = 2" />, you read an orbital speed of <InlineMath math="5.59\text{ km s}^{-1}" />.
