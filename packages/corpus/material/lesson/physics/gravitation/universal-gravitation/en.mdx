## Universal Gravitation

Drop a stone and look up at the Moon on the same evening. Both follow **Newton's law of universal gravitation**: every mass in the universe attracts every other mass along the line joining their centres.

<BlockMath math="F = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}" />

Here <InlineMath math="G" /> equals <InlineMath math="6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}" />, <InlineMath math="m_1" /> and <InlineMath math="m_2" /> are the two masses in kilograms, and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the centre-to-centre separation in metres. Because <InlineMath math="G" /> is tiny, gravity feels noticeable only when at least one object is planetary in scale.

> Every gravitational distance starts at the centre of each body, never at its outer surface.

Doubling the separation cuts the pull to one quarter rather than one half, because the denominator squares <InlineMath math="r" />. Slide the distance control below to track how steeply the inverse-square curve drops.

At <InlineMath math="r = 3" />, the ratio reads one ninth, so a <InlineMath math="36\text{ N}" /> pull falls to <InlineMath math="4\text{ N}" />. By Newton's third law, the Earth pulls the Moon with the exact same force size that the Moon exerts on the Earth.

## Planetary Gravity and Orbits

Gravity sets both how strongly a planet pulls objects at its surface and how fast a **natural or artificial satellite** must travel to stay aloft without firing engines.

### Surface Gravity

Setting an object's weight equal to Newton's gravitational force cancels the falling object's own mass <InlineMath math="m" />, defining **gravitational acceleration** <InlineMath math="g" />.

<BlockMath math="g = \frac{GM}{r^2}" />

Gravitational acceleration depends only on the planet's mass <InlineMath math="M" /> and distance <InlineMath math="r" /> from its centre. At the Earth's surface, <InlineMath math="R" /> is <InlineMath math="6.37 \times 10^{6}\text{ m}" /> and <InlineMath math="g" /> is <InlineMath math="9.81\text{ m s}^{-2}" />.

On Planet Z, doubling both <InlineMath math="M" /> and <InlineMath math="R" /> cuts <InlineMath math="g" /> in half because radius is squared in the denominator. Meanwhile, astronauts aboard the International Space Station at <InlineMath math="400\text{ km}" /> altitude still experience <InlineMath math="8.69\text{ m s}^{-2}" />, nearly ninety percent of surface gravity. They float because they and the station fall around the Earth together.

### Satellite Orbits

A satellite stays in a circular orbit when gravity provides the exact **centripetal force** needed to bend its path continuously around the planet.

<BlockMath math="v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}" />

The satellite's own mass cancels out, so a heavy telescope and a tiny cubesat travel at the same speed in the same orbit. A larger orbital radius means a weaker pull, a slower linear speed, and a much longer orbital period.

Always add the planet's radius <InlineMath math="R" /> to any altitude <InlineMath math="h" /> above the ground before substituting into an orbital formula. Compare two satellites orbiting above the Earth's surface at heights of <InlineMath math="3R" /> and <InlineMath math="R" /> below.

## Kepler Laws of Motion

Johannes Kepler discovered three geometric rules that describe every planetary and satellite orbit before Newton explained why they work:

1. Every planet moves in an **ellipse** with the Sun at one focus.
2. The line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps **equal areas in equal times**, so a planet speeds up near perihelion and slows down near aphelion.
3. The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the mean orbital radius.

<BlockMath math="T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} r^3" />

> A planet moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion because gravity pulls forward on approach and backward as it climbs away.

## Escape Speed from Planets

**Escape velocity** is the minimum launch speed needed from distance <InlineMath math="r" /> to leave a planet's gravitational well without any further thrust.

<BlockMath math="v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}" />

At any distance, escape velocity is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2}" /> times the circular orbital speed. At Earth's surface, circular orbit requires <InlineMath math="7.91\text{ km s}^{-1}" />, whereas escape requires <InlineMath math="11.2\text{ km s}^{-1}" />.
