## Plants Need Seventeen Nutrient Elements

A green plant makes its own food by photosynthesis, but it still needs **inorganic nutrient elements** from the air, water and soil. The elements it needs in large amounts are the **macronutrients**: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur. The elements it needs in tiny amounts are the **micronutrients**: iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, chlorine and nickel. Root hairs take in water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport. A shortage of just one element is enough to stunt growth.

## Where the Dry Mass of a Plant Comes From

About <InlineMath math="80\%" /> to <InlineMath math="90\%" /> of a living plant's mass is water. Once the water is dried off, almost all the mass that's left is carbon, oxygen and hydrogen. Those three elements come from **carbon dioxide in the air and from water**, not from the soil. The chart shows each element's share of the dry mass.

The mineral bar is small, but without it a plant can't build proteins, chlorophyll or nucleic acids. That's why farmers add fertiliser even though **mineral elements make up less than a twentieth of the dry mass**.

## What Each Element Does and How a Shortage Shows

Each macronutrient has a particular job in the cell. When one element runs short, the plant shows signs you can recognise on its leaves, stem or roots.

| Macronutrient | Main function | Deficiency sign |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nitrogen | Builds proteins, nucleic acids and chlorophyll | Older leaves turn yellow (chlorosis), stunted growth |
| Phosphorus | Builds nucleic acids, ATP and phospholipids | Older leaves turn purplish, poor root growth |
| Potassium | Activates enzymes, controls stomatal opening | Edges of older leaves turn yellow then brown, weak stems |
| Calcium | Builds the middle lamella of the cell wall | Young leaves curled, shoot tips die |
| Magnesium | Part of the chlorophyll molecule | Yellowing between the veins of older leaves |
| Sulphur | Builds some amino acids and proteins | Young leaves turn yellow |

Micronutrients are needed in very small amounts, but each one is still essential.

| Micronutrient | Main function | Deficiency sign |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Iron | Needed to make chlorophyll | Young leaves turn yellow |
| Manganese and chlorine | Help split water in photosynthesis | Yellow and brown spots on leaves |
| Boron | Helps pollen tubes grow and calcium uptake | Shoot tips die, flowers drop |
| Zinc | Needed to make auxin | Small leaves, short internodes |
| Copper | Part of enzymes in photosynthesis | Tips of young leaves die |
| Molybdenum | Needed to use nitrate | Signs like nitrogen deficiency |
| Nickel | Part of the enzyme urease | Leaf tips die |

Check **which leaves show the sign first**. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium can be moved from old leaves to young ones, so the old leaves suffer first. Calcium, sulphur and iron stay where they are, so the young leaves suffer first.

## The Scarcest Element Limits Growth

Plenty of nitrogen fertiliser won't help if the soil is short of phosphorus. Growth is limited by **the element in shortest supply compared with what the plant needs**, an idea called the law of the minimum. ## A Culture Solution Experiment on Nutrient Deficiency

To test what a shortage of nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium does, you grow seedlings of the same size in a **culture solution**, which is distilled water with mineral salts dissolved in it and no soil. A complete Knop's solution is the control. Every other set lacks just one element, so any difference in growth can be traced to that element.

| Variable | In this experiment |
| :--- | :--- |
| Manipulated | Type of culture solution: complete, no nitrogen, no phosphorus, no potassium |
| Responding | Leaf colour, height and dry mass of the seedlings |
| Fixed | Type and size of seedlings, volume of solution, light, temperature and duration |

Wrap each container in black paper so algae don't grow in the solution. Bubble air through the solution so the roots get oxygen for respiration. After a few weeks, the seedlings in the complete solution are the healthiest. The set without nitrogen has yellow older leaves and dwarfed plants, the set without phosphorus has purplish leaves and short roots, and the set without potassium has brown leaf edges.

## Root Hairs Take In Water and Mineral Salts

Water and mineral ions enter the plant through **root hair cells**, which are epidermal cells of the root that grow out into fine projections. Three features suit them to the job: the projection that adds surface area, a thin cell wall, and cell sap that's more concentrated than the soil solution. Each feature gets its own model below.

### The Surface Area of Root Hairs

The surface area of that piece of root is the area of the root cylinder plus the area of all the root hairs:

<BlockMath math="A = 2\pi(0.25)(1) + n \cdot 2\pi(0.005)\,l = \pi(0.5 + 0.01\,l\,n)\text{ mm}^2" />

### Water Enters by Osmosis

The cell sap of a root hair holds dissolved sugars and ions, so it's more concentrated than the soil solution. The soil solution is **hypotonic** to the cell sap, so water moves in by osmosis. For dilute solutions at <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" />, the osmotic pressure difference pushing water in is about <InlineMath math="2.48\text{ MPa}" /> for every <InlineMath math="1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> of concentration difference.

### Worked Example on Too Much Fertiliser

Take the cell sap of a root hair to be equivalent to a <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution. Ordinary soil water has a concentration of <InlineMath math="0.02\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. After too much fertiliser is scattered around the roots, it becomes <InlineMath math="0.45\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Work out **which way water moves** in each case.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2.48(0.30 - 0.02) = 0.69\text{ MPa}" />
<BlockMath math="2.48(0.30 - 0.45) = -0.37\text{ MPa}" />
</MathContainer>

The positive value in ordinary soil water means water moves into the root. The negative value after too much fertiliser means the soil solution is now hypertonic, so water leaves the root and the plant wilts. Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="c = 0.45" /> and <InlineMath math="s = 0.30" />, the marker sits below the horizontal axis.

### Ions Enter by Active Transport

The concentration of ions such as potassium and nitrate in the cell sap is usually higher than in the soil. So the ions must be taken in **against the concentration gradient** by carrier proteins, using ATP from respiration. Root hair cells have many mitochondria for this. This simple model takes the maximum uptake rate to be proportional to the oxygen in the soil, because aerobic respiration needs oxygen.

## Plants with Special Ways of Feeding

Most plants get all their minerals through their roots. Some live where nutrients are scarce or grow on other plants, so they feed differently.

| Type | Examples | How it gets nutrients |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Carnivorous | Pitcher plant, Venus flytrap, *Drosera* | Photosynthesises, but traps insects and digests them with enzymes to get nitrogen and other minerals |
| Parasitic | *Rafflesia*, dodder (*Cuscuta*) | Has no chlorophyll, takes water, minerals and food straight from the host's vascular tissue |
| Semi-parasitic | Mistletoe (*Loranthus*) | Photosynthesises, but takes water and minerals from the host's xylem |
| Epiphytic | Staghorn fern, bird's nest fern, orchids | Grows on tree branches to reach light, takes in water from the air and humus without taking anything from the tree |

Carnivorous plants usually live on acidic, boggy soil that's **poor in nitrogen**. The digested insects supply the nitrogen they can't get from that soil.

## Common Mistakes about Plant Nutrition

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Micronutrients can be ignored because so little is needed. | A shortage of any required element still stunts growth. | Use the law of the minimum: the scarcest element limits growth. |
| Plants get their carbon from the soil. | Carbon comes from carbon dioxide through photosynthesis. | Dry mass is almost all carbon, oxygen and hydrogen from air and water. |
| Mineral ions always enter by diffusion. | Ion concentration in the cell sap is often higher, so active transport is needed. | Compare the concentrations inside and outside the cell. |
| More fertiliser is always better. | A soil solution that's too concentrated becomes hypertonic and draws water out of the roots. | Compare the soil solution concentration with the cell sap. |

The fourth mistake shows up most often in application questions. Too much fertiliser **reverses the direction of osmosis**, so the plant wilts even though the soil is wet.

## Practice on Nutrition in Plants

The questions move from naming elements to calculations. **Show your working** in every calculation.

1. A field supplies <InlineMath math="80\%" /> of the nitrogen, <InlineMath math="100\%" /> of the phosphorus and <InlineMath math="50\%" /> of the potassium a crop needs. Calculate the relative growth and name the fertiliser to add first.

2. A seedling's root has <InlineMath math="50" /> root hairs per millimetre, each <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ mm}" /> long. Using the surface area model, work out how many times larger the surface area is than for a root without root hairs.

3. The cell sap of a root hair is equivalent to a <InlineMath math="0.30\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution and the soil solution is <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Calculate the osmotic pressure difference and state which way water moves.

4. A green plant on nitrogen-poor peat has pitcher-shaped leaves holding fluid. Name its type of nutrition and explain how it gets nitrogen.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Find the lowest supply:

<BlockMath math="\min(80, 100, 50, 100) = 50" />

Relative growth is <InlineMath math="50\%" />, and potassium is the limiting element. Add **potassium fertiliser** first. Check: raising potassium to <InlineMath math="80\%" /> lifts growth to <InlineMath math="80\%" />, and then nitrogen becomes the limit.

**Solution 2**: Use the area ratio from the model:

<BlockMath math="1 + 0.02 \times 0.5 \times 50 = 1.5" />

The surface area is <InlineMath math="1.5" /> times as large. Check with the model: set <InlineMath math="n = 50" /> and <InlineMath math="l = 0.5" />, and the ratio reading is <InlineMath math="1.5" />.

**Solution 3**: Multiply the concentration difference by <InlineMath math="2.48" />:

<BlockMath math="2.48(0.30 - 0.10) = 0.50\text{ MPa}" />

The positive value means the soil solution is hypotonic, so **water moves into** the root hair by osmosis. Check: the soil concentration is lower than the cell sap's, so the direction must be inwards.

**Solution 4**: This is a **carnivorous plant**, such as a pitcher plant. It still photosynthesises to make food. Insects that slip into the pitcher are digested by enzymes in the fluid, and the leaf absorbs the nitrogen ions released. Check: green leaves show it isn't a parasite.
