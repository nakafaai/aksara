## Glucose Pays for Cell Work

Picture glucose as a large banknote. A tiny machine inside a living cell cannot feed a big note straight into its slot, so the cell first changes it into small coins called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate). Breaking down glucose to load those ATP coins is **cellular respiration**.

There are two ways to break glucose down. With oxygen, the cell takes glucose apart completely, all the way to carbon dioxide and water. That's **aerobic respiration**.

Without oxygen, the cell splits glucose only partly and makes far fewer ATP coins. That's **fermentation**. In the mitochondrion model ahead, you switch the oxygen off and see for yourself which steps stop.

> Oxygen unlocks the full energy store of glucose inside mitochondria for about <InlineMath math="36" /> ATP, while without oxygen the cell stops in the cytoplasm at just <InlineMath math="2" /> ATP.

Why move the energy into ATP first? The energy in glucose sits locked inside its chemical bonds, and from there it can't push a muscle or pump an ion. So the cell breaks ATP down, a little at a time, right where a job needs doing:

| Cell job | Example | What ATP does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Contracting | Skeletal muscle fibre | Gives the muscle the energy to contract |
| Pumping ions | Nerve cell | Pumps sodium ions out and potassium ions in |
| Building proteins | Any cell with ribosomes | Joins amino acids into protein chains |
| Starting glycolysis | Every cell | Two ATP are spent first so glucose can be split |

Pumping ions is uphill work. The sodium-potassium pump pushes ions from where there are few to where there are many, against their concentration gradient. It's one example of [active transport across the plasma membrane](/en/subjects/biology/membrane-transport/diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport), and **each cycle of the pump breaks down one ATP molecule**.

Building proteins is also one of the largest energy costs of a growing cell. Every amino acid link requires ready-to-spend ATP coins.

People often mix up respiration and breathing. **Breathing** is air moving in and out of your lungs, the [mechanism of breathing and gas exchange](/en/subjects/biology/respiratory-system/breathing-and-gas-exchange) that brings oxygen into your blood. **Cellular respiration** is the chemical reaction inside each cell that uses oxygen to break down glucose.

A tree has no lungs, yet its leaf cells have mitochondria. At night there's no light, so photosynthesis stops, but the leaf cells keep taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide.

To tell the two apart, ask one simple question. **Is glucose being broken down to make ATP**? If it is, that's cellular respiration.

## Cytoplasm to Mitochondrion

The breaking down starts in the **cytoplasm**, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. There, one six-carbon glucose molecule is split in half into two three-carbon molecules of **pyruvic acid**.

This splitting step is called glycolysis. It doesn't need oxygen, and it gives a net gain of **<InlineMath math="2" /> ATP per glucose**.

If oxygen is there, the pyruvic acid moves into a [mitochondrion, the organelle wrapped in two membranes](/en/subjects/biology/cell-and-organisation/cell-structure-and-specialised-cells). In the space inside, the matrix, the carbon atoms of pyruvic acid come off as carbon dioxide. By the end, all six carbon atoms of the glucose have left as six <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> molecules.

The hydrogen pulled off along the way is carried to the folded inner membrane, the cristae. There, **oxygen takes up the hydrogen and forms water**. This last step makes about **<InlineMath math="90\%" /> of all the ATP** from aerobic respiration.

Without oxygen, nothing takes the hydrogen at the end of the inner membrane, so the steps in the mitochondrion stop. Pyruvic acid stays in the cytoplasm.

A muscle cell turns it into lactic acid, while a yeast cell turns it into ethanol and carbon dioxide. That change frees the hydrogen carriers again so glycolysis can keep going, but **ATP stays at <InlineMath math="2" /> per glucose**.

One kind of cell in your body never carries out aerobic respiration. **A mature red blood cell has no mitochondria**, so all its ATP comes from glycolysis and it makes lactic acid.

Because it has no mitochondria, a red blood cell carries oxygen around your whole body without using any of it itself. All the oxygen it picks up arrives intact at the tissues.

## Gas Balance of Respiration

For each glucose, aerobic respiration takes in a fixed amount of oxygen and gives out a fixed amount of carbon dioxide. The equation tells you the exact ratio: **six oxygen for six carbon dioxide**.

You'll use that ratio twice. First to work out the mass of the products, then to predict how the coloured liquid moves in a respirometer, a sealed tube that measures how much oxygen living things take in.

### Atoms and Kilojoules

In words, glucose reacts with oxygen:

<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} + \text{energy}" />

The balanced chemical equation says the same thing with numbers. It gives the number of molecules of each substance and the energy from one mole of glucose:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 6\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\mathrm{H_2O} + 2{,}898\text{ kJ}" />

Count each kind of atom on both sides to verify that none is lost:

<BlockMath math="\text{C}: 6 = 6, \quad \text{H}: 12 = 12, \quad \text{O}: 6 + 12 = 12 + 6 = 18" />

Of the <InlineMath math="2{,}898\text{ kJ}" />, **less than half ends up stored in ATP**. The rest leaves as heat that keeps your body warm.

Books also give different numbers of ATP per glucose: <InlineMath math="38" /> as the theoretical maximum, <InlineMath math="36" /> in many school books, and fewer in real cells because the number of hydrogen ions pumped varies. We'll use <InlineMath math="36" /> in every calculation.

The cells of a leg muscle break down <InlineMath math="45\text{ g}" /> of glucose aerobically with oxygen. How much carbon dioxide comes out?

Change the mass of glucose into moles, use the **<InlineMath math="1 : 6" /> ratio in the equation**, then change back to a mass. The molar masses are glucose <InlineMath math="180" />, oxygen <InlineMath math="32" />, carbon dioxide <InlineMath math="44" /> and water <InlineMath math="18\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Glucose} = \frac{45}{180} = 0.25\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CO_2} = 6 \times 0.25 = 1.5\text{ mol} = 1.5 \times 44 = 66\text{ g}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Energy} = 0.25 \times 2{,}898 = 724.5\text{ kJ}" />
</MathContainer>

Check the working with the conservation of mass. Because no atoms appear or vanish, total reactant mass must match total product mass:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{O_2} = 1.5 \times 32 = 48\text{ g}, \quad \mathrm{H_2O} = 1.5 \times 18 = 27\text{ g}" />
<BlockMath math="45 + 48 = 93\text{ g} = 66 + 27" />
</MathContainer>

Both sides total exactly <InlineMath math="93\text{ g}" />, confirming that the carbon dioxide mass matches the balanced equation.

### Reading a Respirometer

Oxygen is invisible, but a coloured liquid moves as soon as gas disappears from a sealed tube. A respirometer uses that movement to measure the rate of aerobic respiration through the **volume of oxygen taken in**.

The model shows two sealed boiling tubes side by side in one water bath, both holding germinating seeds on wire gauze. Under the gauze in the right tube, **potassium hydroxide** (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{KOH}" />) solution soaks up all the carbon dioxide the seeds give off.

Because the carbon dioxide is soaked up right away, the gas in the right tube shrinks only by the oxygen taken in. Its red drop in the capillary creeps steadily towards the seeds.

Under the gauze in the left tube is water, which does not soak up carbon dioxide. So its red drop moves only by the difference between the oxygen taken in and the carbon dioxide given off.

Both tubes sit in one water bath so warm gas can't expand and push the drops falsely. A real experiment adds a control tube with no living seeds to catch any temperature or pressure drift.

Make a prediction before you play the model or move the time slider. In <InlineMath math="20" /> minutes, seeds oxidising glucose move the drop in the potassium hydroxide tube <InlineMath math="30\text{ mm}" />. **Where is the drop in the water tube** at minute <InlineMath math="20" />?

The drop in the water tube **doesn't move**. For glucose, every six oxygen molecules taken in are swapped for six carbon dioxide molecules given off.

Equal numbers of gas molecules at the same temperature and pressure take up equal volumes, so the gas lost and the gas gained cancel out:

<BlockMath math="\Delta d = 30 - 30 = 0\text{ mm}" />

Carbon dioxide given off divided by oxygen taken in is the **respiratory quotient** (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{RQ}" />). The readout in the model shows <InlineMath math="1.0" />, the typical value for carbohydrate.

Now turn the potassium hydroxide reading into a rate. The missing gas fills the capillary like a thin rod, forming a **cylinder with the radius of the capillary bore**.

The bore is <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm}" /> across, so its radius is <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ mm}" />. The cylinder is <InlineMath math="30\text{ mm}" /> long, the distance the drop moved:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="V = \pi r^2 h = \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 30 = 7.5\pi \approx 23.6\text{ mm}^3" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Rate} = \frac{7.5\pi\text{ mm}^3}{20\text{ min}} = 0.375\pi \approx 1.18\text{ mm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check both the units and the final number before moving on:

<BlockMath math="\text{mm}^2 \times \text{mm} = \text{mm}^3, \quad 0.375\pi \times 20 = 7.5\pi" />

A volume divided by a time gives <InlineMath math="\text{mm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" />, and multiplying back recovers the exact starting volume.

Another common school respirometer uses the same idea with a small living animal, such as a cockroach, sitting on wire gauze above soda lime in a sealed boiling tube. A capillary leads out and dips straight down into coloured eosin dye.

Because soda lime also soaks up carbon dioxide, **the eosin rises up the capillary** as the animal takes in oxygen. A second tube with soda lime and no animal acts as the control in the same water bath.

## Sprinting Short of Oxygen

When you sprint, your muscle fibres use ATP faster than your blood can deliver oxygen. So the fibres switch to making ATP without oxygen through **lactic acid fermentation** in the cytoplasm.

One glucose becomes two molecules of lactic acid, and no carbon dioxide comes out:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{lactic acid} + \text{energy } (150\text{ kJ})" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{C_3H_6O_3}" />
</MathContainer>

That's far less energy. One mole of glucose releases only about <InlineMath math="150\text{ kJ}" /> and gives <InlineMath math="2" /> ATP per glucose, so glycolysis alone can sustain hard muscle work for only about a minute.

As lactic acid builds up, the inside of the fibre turns more acidic and **the pH inside the fibre drops**. A low pH slows down enzymes, so lactic acid contributes to muscle fatigue.

When you stop running, you keep breathing fast and deeply for a while. That extra oxygen pays back what the sprint ran short of, known as the **oxygen debt**.

Your blood carries the lactic acid to the liver. There, part of it is turned into glucose or glycogen, while the rest is turned back into pyruvic acid and oxidised to carbon dioxide and water.

> Heavy breathing after a sprint is your body paying back its oxygen debt, not lactic acid lingering into the next day.

Sore muscles a day or two after hard exercise often get blamed on lactic acid. The timing doesn't fit: **lactic acid in the blood returns to its pre-exercise level within about an hour**, whereas soreness peaks <InlineMath math="24" /> to <InlineMath math="72" /> hours later.

That late soreness comes from tiny tears in muscle fibres and the inflammation that follows, especially when a muscle stretches while contracting such as running downhill. When your legs ache on day two, the lactic acid was cleared long ago.

## Fermentation beyond Muscle

Yeast, rice roots and *Lactobacillus* bacteria ferment too when oxygen is short or missing. Yeast and rice roots make **ethanol and carbon dioxide**, while *Lactobacillus* makes lactic acid just as your muscle fibres do.

Each one runs short of oxygen in a different place: yeast in a tube sealed with oil, rice roots in waterlogged mud, and *Lactobacillus* in milk.

### Yeast in a Sealed Tube

Yeast is a tiny fungus, the one that makes bread dough rise. Without oxygen, it carries out **alcoholic fermentation**, turning one glucose into two molecules of ethanol and two of carbon dioxide:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{energy } (210\text{ kJ})" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{C_2H_5OH} + 2\mathrm{CO_2}" />
</MathContainer>

A common school experiment tests for these products with two boiling tubes. Tube A holds <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="5\%" /> glucose solution that was boiled and cooled, <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="5\%" /> yeast suspension, and a **layer of paraffin oil** on top.

A delivery tube carries the gas from tube A into limewater. Tube B, the control, is set up the same way without the yeast suspension:

| Step | Reason |
| :--- | :--- |
| Boil the glucose solution | Drives out dissolved oxygen, so there's no oxygen left |
| Cool it before adding yeast | Heat denatures yeast enzymes and kills the yeast |
| Pour on paraffin oil | Stops oxygen from the air dissolving back in |
| Bubble the gas into limewater | Limewater turns cloudy with carbon dioxide |

At the end, tube A's limewater turns cloudy, the mixture gets warmer, and it smells of alcohol, while tube B hasn't changed. Before you read on, decide **which observation proves fermentation** and what tube B rules out.

Cloudy limewater shows carbon dioxide, but aerobic respiration gives off carbon dioxide too, so that alone isn't enough. The smell of alcohol shows ethanol, which only fermentation makes, so **the ethanol smell is the proof of fermentation**.

The rise in temperature shows that some energy escapes as heat. Tube B's limewater stays clear, proving the carbon dioxide in tube A came from the yeast, not from the air or the glucose solution.

### Rice Roots under Water

Plants ferment too. Roots normally take their oxygen from the air in tiny spaces between soil grains.

In a waterlogged paddy field, water fills those spaces and **oxygen diffuses through water about <InlineMath math="10{,}000" /> times more slowly** than through air. Mud oxygen runs out quickly, so root cells switch to alcoholic fermentation and make ethanol and carbon dioxide.

How does rice survive in a paddy? Its roots have **aerenchyma**, large air spaces in the cortex of the root and stem that work like air pipes from the shoot all the way to the root tip.

Rice roots form aerenchyma even in drained soil and build more when waterlogged. Older upper parts of rice roots also build a barrier layer that stops oxygen leaking out into the mud.

Dryland crops such as maize, wheat and barley don't build that barrier, so waterlogging damages them. When fermentation lasts too long, roots get too little ATP and toxic ethanol builds up until root cells die.

### Fermented Food and Fuel

*Lactobacillus* bacteria carry out lactic acid fermentation just like oxygen-starved muscle fibres. They turn milk sugar into lactic acid.

The milk turns sour and thick because **the acid makes milk proteins set**, which is how yogurt and cheese are made. Other fermentation products end up in food and fuel too:

| Product | Organism | Fermentation product used |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Yogurt and cheese | *Lactobacillus* | Lactic acid sets the milk proteins |
| Bread | Yeast | Carbon dioxide makes the dough rise |
| Tapai | Moulds and yeasts in ragi | Moulds turn starch into sugar, yeasts make a little alcohol |
| Bioethanol | Yeast | Ethanol from sugarcane or maize starch becomes fuel |

## One Glucose on Three Paths

All three paths start the same way, with glycolysis in the cytoplasm. They split over one question: **what happens to pyruvic acid next decides how much energy is released**.

| Feature | Aerobic respiration | Lactic acid fermentation | Alcoholic fermentation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Oxygen | Needed | Not needed | Not needed |
| Where it happens | Cytoplasm and mitochondria | Cytoplasm | Cytoplasm |
| Products | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> and water | Lactic acid | Ethanol and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> |
| Energy per mole of glucose | <InlineMath math="2{,}898\text{ kJ}" /> | <InlineMath math="150\text{ kJ}" /> | <InlineMath math="210\text{ kJ}" /> |
| ATP per glucose | About <InlineMath math="36" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |
| Examples | Most cells | Muscle cells, *Lactobacillus* | Yeast, rice roots |

Fermentation leaves most of glucose's energy still trapped inside lactic acid or ethanol. Ethanol proves it because it still burns as bioethanol fuel.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Aerobic respiration} = \frac{72}{36} = 2\text{ glucose}" />

<BlockMath math="2 \times 36 = 72, \quad 36 \times 2 = 72, \quad \frac{36}{2} = 18" />

Fermentation uses **<InlineMath math="18" /> times as much glucose**, which is why a muscle's glucose store runs down so fast in a sprint. Set the slider to <InlineMath math="72" />, and the graph shows the same numbers.

<BlockMath math="\text{Glucose} = \frac{0.6}{2} = 0.3\text{ mol} = 0.3 \times 180 = 54\text{ g}" />

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="\text{Yeast: ethanol} = 0.6 \times 46 = 27.6\text{ g}, \quad \mathrm{CO_2} = 0.6 \times 44 = 26.4\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

Check the conservation of mass and compare with the aerobic glucose requirement:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="27.6 + 26.4 = 54\text{ g}" />
