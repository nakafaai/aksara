## Two Gametes Become One Zygote

Every baby starts as just one cell, formed when a father's **sperm** joins a mother's **ovum** (egg cell). Both are **gametes**, the new cell is a **zygote**, and making offspring this way is **sexual reproduction**.

Each human gamete carries <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes, **half of the <InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes in your body cells**. So the zygote gets <InlineMath math="23 + 23 = 46" /> chromosomes, just like its parents. The zygote divides into an embryo, settles into the wall of the uterus, develops into a foetus fed through the placenta, and is born and keeps growing.

You'll move through each of those steps yourself in six models: gametes forming in the testes and ovaries, hormones running the menstrual cycle, an embryo travelling to the uterus, the mother's and the foetus's blood swapping substances, twins forming, and insects growing in steps.

## Making Sperm and Eggs

Why does a gamete carry only half? Imagine a sperm and an ovum each carrying <InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes: the zygote would get <InlineMath math="92" />, the next generation <InlineMath math="184" />, and the count would double every generation. So the body makes gametes by **meiosis**, a process called **gametogenesis**.

> Meiosis halves the chromosome number from <InlineMath math="46" /> to <InlineMath math="23" /> so each zygote returns to <InlineMath math="46" /> and the count never doubles from generation to generation.

Meiosis does one more thing. During meiosis, the chromosomes you inherited from your father and your mother swap pieces and then get shuffled at random into the gametes. So every gamete carries a slightly different mix of genes, and that's why brothers and sisters don't look exactly alike.

Gametogenesis happens in the **gonads**: the testes in males and the ovaries in females. The other organs of the reproductive system store, carry or receive the gametes:

| Organ | System | Its job |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Testis | Male | Makes sperm and the hormone testosterone |
| Scrotum | Male | Holds the testes outside the body, <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4^\circ\text{C}" /> cooler than body temperature |
| Epididymis | Male | Stores sperm while they mature |
| Sperm duct | Male | Carries sperm to the urethra |
| Seminal vesicle, prostate gland, Cowper's gland | Male | Add fluids that carry and feed the sperm, and the mixture is semen |
| Urethra and penis | Male | Carry semen out of the body |
| Ovary | Female | Makes ova and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone |
| Fallopian tube | Female | Carries the ovum to the uterus, and is usually where fertilisation happens |
| Uterus | Female | Where the foetus grows, with an inner lining called the endometrium |
| Cervix | Female | The narrow neck of the uterus, opening into the vagina |
| Vagina | Female | Receives sperm, and is the birth canal |

In this model, pick male or female and go in one step at a time: from the reproductive system, into the gonad, to the dividing cells, all the way to a finished gamete. The testes and the ovaries use the same meiosis, but **they end up with very different results**, and that difference is what you're looking for.

### Sperm in the Testes

A testis is about <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" /> long, packed with coiled **seminiferous tubules** whose walls produce sperm. This process, **spermatogenesis**, begins at puberty and runs daily.

The testes make about <InlineMath math="100" /> to <InlineMath math="300" /> million sperm a day, and one round from stem cell to finished sperm takes about <InlineMath math="64" /> days.

Follow one cell from the wall of the tubule into the space in its middle:

1. A **spermatogonium** (<InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes) divides by mitosis. Some of its daughter cells grow into **primary spermatocytes** (<InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes).
2. A primary spermatocyte goes through **meiosis I** and becomes two **secondary spermatocytes**, each with <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes.
3. Each secondary spermatocyte goes through **meiosis II** and becomes two **spermatids**. One primary spermatocyte has now become four spermatids.
4. Each spermatid changes shape into a **sperm** with a tail.

The pituitary gland at the base of the brain controls this with two hormones. **LH stimulates the interstitial cells**, the cells between the tubules, to release **testosterone**. FSH acts on the cells in the tubule wall that look after the developing sperm. FSH and testosterone together keep spermatogenesis going.

A finished sperm has three parts, and each part has a job:

| Part | What's inside | Its job |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Head, only <InlineMath math="5\ \mu\text{m}" /> long | The nucleus with <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes, under a cap called the **acrosome** | The acrosome's enzymes digest the outer layer of the oocyte |
| Middle piece | Packed with mitochondria | Supplies ATP |
| Tail | A long, thin whip | Beats back and forth using that ATP, so the sperm can swim |

Make a prediction before you open the dividing cells level in the model. How many chromosomes are in one secondary spermatocyte, and in one spermatid?

The answer is **<InlineMath math="23" /> for both**. Meiosis I pulls each pair of chromosomes apart into two cells, so that's where the number halves, from <InlineMath math="46" /> to <InlineMath math="23" />. Meiosis II only pulls apart the two chromatids of each chromosome, so each spermatid still gets <InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes.

An easy check: <InlineMath math="23" /> is odd, so it can't be halved again into a whole number. If you got <InlineMath math="11" /> or <InlineMath math="12" />, you halved at both divisions.

### Eggs in the Ovaries

**Oogenesis** starts before a baby girl is born. In the ovaries of a female foetus, **oogonia** (<InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes) divide by mitosis and then grow into **primary oocytes** (<InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes). Each primary oocyte starts meiosis I, then **stops partway, in prophase I**, and waits inside a small follicle, some of them for decades.

A baby girl's ovaries hold <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="2" /> million primary oocytes. By puberty about <InlineMath math="400{,}000" /> are left, and by the end of menopause there are none.

From puberty, in every menstrual cycle, **FSH makes a few follicles grow**, and usually just one becomes a mature **Graafian follicle**.

Just before ovulation, a surge of LH makes its primary oocyte finish meiosis I **unequally**. One cell gets almost all the cytoplasm as a **secondary oocyte** (<InlineMath math="23" /> chromosomes), while the tiny other cell is the **first polar body**.

Ovulation releases the secondary oocyte, so the ovum isn't finished yet. Meiosis II finishes only if a sperm enters, producing the **ovum** and one more polar body.

The first polar body may or may not divide, so one primary oocyte gives one ovum and one to three polar bodies that all break down.

Why would the ovary waste cells like that? The ovum keeps almost all the cytoplasm, food and organelles for the embryo's first days, with a sperm's volume <InlineMath math="85{,}000" /> times smaller than an oocyte's.

In a Graafian follicle, **follicular cells** around the secondary oocyte release oestrogen beside a large space filled with follicular fluid.

Now compare the two processes. What they share: both happen in the gonads, both use meiosis to turn <InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes into <InlineMath math="23" />, and both are controlled by FSH and LH from the pituitary. The differences are in this table:

| Feature | Spermatogenesis | Oogenesis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Where | Seminiferous tubules in the testes | Follicles in the ovaries |
| When it starts | At puberty | Before birth |
| Pace | Every day, nonstop | One secondary oocyte per cycle, until menopause |
| From one starting cell | Four sperm | One ovum and one to three polar bodies |
| Cytoplasm | Shared equally | Nearly all to one cell |
| Gamete | Small and swims | Large and can't move by itself |
| Meiosis II finishes | Inside the testis | Only after a sperm gets in |
| Hormones | FSH, LH, testosterone | FSH, LH, oestrogen, progesterone |

## Menstrual Cycle Hormones

About once a month, a woman's body prepares one oocyte and builds a thick, soft uterus lining for an embryo. If no embryo arrives, the lining breaks down as blood and the cycle starts again.

This **menstrual cycle** averages <InlineMath math="28" /> days, usually ranging from <InlineMath math="21" /> to <InlineMath math="32" /> days, with the first day of the period counting as day one.

Four hormones run every step. Two come from the pituitary gland and two from the ovary itself:

| Hormone | Released by | What it does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| FSH | Pituitary gland | Makes follicles grow |
| Oestrogen | The growing follicle | Repairs and thickens the endometrium, and at high levels sets off the LH surge |
| LH | Pituitary gland | Its surge bursts the follicle and turns the empty follicle into a corpus luteum |
| Progesterone | Corpus luteum | Keeps the endometrium thick, and with oestrogen holds back FSH and LH |

In a <InlineMath math="28" />-day cycle, five events happen in this order:

1. **Menstruation**, about days <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />: progesterone and oestrogen are low, so the endometrium comes away. FSH starts to rise.
2. **Follicle development**: FSH makes a follicle grow, and that follicle releases more and more oestrogen.
3. **Thickening of the endometrium**: oestrogen repairs the endometrium and builds it up again.
4. **Ovulation**, around day <InlineMath math="14" />: high oestrogen sets off the LH surge, and the Graafian follicle bursts and releases the secondary oocyte.
5. **Corpus luteum formation**: LH turns the empty follicle into a corpus luteum, which releases progesterone so the endometrium stays thick and full of blood vessels.

If there's no fertilisation, the corpus luteum breaks down after <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="12" /> days. Progesterone drops, **the endometrium comes away**, and FSH is free to rise again for the next cycle.

Plenty of cycles aren't <InlineMath math="28" /> days long. Change the cycle length in the model and watch which part stretches: the time from ovulation to the next period stays near two weeks because of the corpus luteum's lifespan.

What stretches or shrinks is the time before ovulation, so **count ovulation back from the end of the cycle**:

<BlockMath math="\text{ovulation day} = \text{cycle length} - 14" />

For example, in a <InlineMath math="31" />-day cycle:

<BlockMath math="\text{ovulation day} = 31 - 14 = 17" />

Check: after day <InlineMath math="17" /> come days <InlineMath math="18" /> to <InlineMath math="31" />, which is <InlineMath math="31 - 17 = 14" /> days before the next period. So ovulation isn't always on day <InlineMath math="14" />. That's only true for a <InlineMath math="28" />-day cycle. Even the <InlineMath math="14" /> is an estimate: in one study of more than <InlineMath math="600{,}000" /> real cycles, the average time after ovulation was <InlineMath math="12.4" /> days.

Try this one before you read the working. A woman's cycle length varies between <InlineMath math="25" /> and <InlineMath math="31" /> days. Sperm can live in the Fallopian tube for up to <InlineMath math="5" /> days, and the oocyte for about <InlineMath math="1" /> day after ovulation. On which days could fertilisation happen?

Start with the earliest and the latest ovulation. Then take the sperm's lifespan away from the earliest day, and add the oocyte's lifespan to the latest day:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{earliest ovulation} = 25 - 14 = 11, \quad \text{latest} = 31 - 14 = 17" />
<BlockMath math="\text{first day} = 11 - 5 = 6, \quad \text{last day} = 17 + 1 = 18" />
</MathContainer>

So fertilisation could happen **from day <InlineMath math="6" /> to day <InlineMath math="18" />**:

<BlockMath math="18 - 6 + 1 = 13\text{ days}" />

Check: sperm arriving on day <InlineMath math="6" /> are still alive on day <InlineMath math="11" />, and an oocyte released on day <InlineMath math="17" /> is still alive on day <InlineMath math="18" />. For an irregular cycle, a calendar count covers almost half the cycle, so it's only a rough guess.

Now pick **Pregnant** in the model. About a week after fertilisation, the embryo settles into the endometrium, and its outer layer starts releasing **HCG** (human chorionic gonadotrophin). HCG tells the corpus luteum to stay alive, grow and keep releasing progesterone and oestrogen, so the endometrium stays, no period comes, and no new follicle matures.

A home pregnancy test detects HCG in urine a few days after implantation. Between weeks <InlineMath math="12" /> and <InlineMath math="17" /> of pregnancy, the placenta takes over making those hormones.

Now pick **Miscarriage**. The endometrium only lasts while progesterone stays high. So if progesterone drops early in pregnancy, for example because the corpus luteum breaks down before the placenta is ready, the endometrium breaks down and the embryo is lost with it. A pregnancy lost on its own before week <InlineMath math="20" /> is called a **miscarriage**.

## Fertilisation to Foetus

After fertilisation, one zygote develops into a whole baby inside the uterus. For the first eight weeks it's called an **embryo**, and from week nine until birth it's called a **foetus**.

Three big questions cover that journey: how the embryo reaches and settles in the uterus, how the placenta feeds it without mixing blood, and how one pregnancy sometimes produces twins.

### Journey to the Uterus

After sexual intercourse, millions of sperm swim through the cervix and uterus into the Fallopian tubes, where fertilisation usually occurs in the wide **ampulla**. Acrosome enzymes from several sperm digest the secondary oocyte's outer layer.

Once one sperm enters, that outer layer changes to block all others. The oocyte finishes meiosis II, and the sperm and ovum nuclei fuse into a **zygote** with <InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes.

The zygote keeps dividing by mitosis while the cilia and muscles of the tube gently push it towards the uterus. These early divisions are called **cleavage**. The zygote becomes a **two-celled embryo**, then four cells, eight cells, and so on. About three days after fertilisation, a solid ball of <InlineMath math="16" /> cells called a **morula** reaches the uterus.

Inside the uterus, the embryo divides to about <InlineMath math="100" /> cells with a fluid-filled space, forming a **blastocyst**. Its outer **trophoblast** helps build the placenta, while the **inner cell mass** becomes the baby.

At the end of the first week, the blastocyst touches the endometrium and sinks into it. This is **implantation**, finishing by the middle of the second week.

One thing in the model is surprising: the number of cells goes up, but **the embryo doesn't grow during cleavage**. Each division only splits the cytoplasm that's already there, so every cell gets smaller and smaller. For example, after four cleavage divisions:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{number of cells} = 2^4 = 16" />
<BlockMath math="\text{volume of one cell} = \frac{1}{16} \times \text{volume of the zygote}" />
</MathContainer>

Check the total volume of all those cells together:

<BlockMath math="16 \times \frac{1}{16} = 1" />

Together they still match the zygote's original size. The embryo only grows fast after implantation, once it starts receiving food from the mother.

Try to answer this before you read on. A woman takes a home pregnancy test four days after fertilisation, and it's negative. Does that mean she isn't pregnant?

**Not necessarily.** The test detects HCG in urine, and the trophoblast only releases HCG after the blastocyst implants at the end of the first week, turning a home test positive a few days later.

On day four, the embryo is still an unimplanted morula or blastocyst, so there's no HCG yet. Check the model's timeline: implantation hasn't started on day four, so she'd need to test again days later.

### Exchange in the Placenta

Once the embryo has implanted, it needs oxygen and food every minute, and it has to get rid of carbon dioxide and urea. It can't breathe or eat by itself, so it relies on the mother's blood through the **placenta**.

The placenta forms from two tissues: the **chorionic villi** from the embryo and the endometrium from the mother. Chorionic villi are fine, finger-like bumps full of the foetus's blood capillaries, sitting soaked in pools of the mother's blood for a huge exchange surface.

The **umbilical cord** connects the foetus to the placenta. It holds three blood vessels. Two **umbilical arteries** carry deoxygenated blood, along with carbon dioxide and urea, from the foetus to the placenta. One **umbilical vein** carries oxygenated blood and food from the placenta back to the foetus.

Lots of students expect the umbilical arteries to carry oxygenated blood, because body arteries usually do. Arteries and veins are named by flow direction: an artery leaves the heart, here the foetus's heart, so **the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood**.

So the mother's and foetus's blood **never mix**, even as substances cross. Antibodies give the foetus [natural passive immunity from its mother](/en/subjects/biology/immunity/body-defence-and-vaccination), yet alcohol, nicotine, some medicines and germs still cross, so pregnant women should avoid smoking and alcohol.

> The mother's blood and the foetus's blood never mix in the placenta, so the villus wall shields delicate foetal vessels from higher pressure and blocks blood cells and immune cells from crossing.

Why do the mother's and the foetus's blood systems have to stay apart? Think of two water pipes, one thick and one thin. Join the thick pipe with its strong flow straight onto the thin one, and the thin pipe can burst.

Near the end of pregnancy, **the mother's arterial pressure is about <InlineMath math="100\text{ mmHg}" />**, while the foetus's is only about <InlineMath math="55\text{ mmHg}" />. If the two systems joined, the mother's blood would push in and could damage the foetus's delicate vessels.

The second reason is blood groups and immune cells. The foetus inherits half its genes from the father, so its blood group may differ from the mother's, just as the [ABO and Rhesus blood groups](/en/subjects/biology/circulatory-system/heart-blood-and-vessels) of two adults can differ.

If foetal red blood cells entered the mother's blood, her body could make antibodies against them, or her immune cells could attack the foetus. The villus wall **stops blood cells crossing**, keeping foetal blood cells in their vessels and maternal immune cells out.

### How Twins Form

Most pregnancies give one baby. Twins form in two different ways, and the way they form decides how alike they are.

**Fraternal twins** start when the ovaries release two secondary oocytes in one cycle and two different sperm fertilise them. Each embryo implants with its own placenta and amniotic sac, and their genes differ like ordinary siblings, so they can be a boy and a girl.

**Identical twins** start from one zygote that splits into two early in development, so **both get the exact same genes** and are always the same sex.

That split can happen as early as two cells, but most often at the early blastocyst stage of <InlineMath math="70" /> to <InlineMath math="100" /> cells. The day the embryo splits decides what the twins share:

| Embryo splits | Placenta | Amniotic sac | Share of identical twins |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Before day <InlineMath math="4" /> | Two, sometimes fused | Two | <InlineMath math="18\%" /> to <InlineMath math="36\%" /> |
| Days <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="8" /> | One, shared | Two | About <InlineMath math="70\%" /> |
| Days <InlineMath math="8" /> to <InlineMath math="13" /> | One, shared | One, shared | About <InlineMath math="1\%" /> to <InlineMath math="3\%" /> |
| After day <InlineMath math="13" /> | One, shared | One, shared | Conjoined twins, very rare |

**Conjoined twins** form when the embryo splits late, after about day <InlineMath math="13" />, and the split doesn't finish. The embryonic disc only splits partway, so the two bodies stay joined, for example at the chest, the belly or the head, and they may share organs. Conjoined twins happen in about one in every <InlineMath math="50{,}000" /> to <InlineMath math="100{,}000" /> pregnancies.

Think about this one first: a pair of twins are a boy and a girl. Are they identical or fraternal?

They're **fraternal twins**. Sex is set at fertilisation by the zygote's sex chromosomes, XX for a girl and XY for a boy.

Identical twins come from one zygote, so both share the same sex chromosomes and sex. A boy-and-girl pair must come from two zygotes, matching the sex row in the table.

## Reproductive Health Problems

Reproductive hormones and organs don't always work smoothly. Some problems come every month, some come with age, and some stop a couple from having a baby.

Many women feel unwell for a few days before their period. **Premenstrual syndrome** (PMS) is a group of physical and emotional symptoms that start one to two weeks before a period, such as bloating, sore breasts, irritability and mood swings. They go away within a few days after the period starts, as hormone levels begin rising again.

Between the mid-forties and mid-fifties, the ovaries stop responding to FSH and LH, so periods become rare and stop. This is **menopause**, confirmed after a full year without a period.

Oestrogen and progesterone drop sharply, and that lack of oestrogen causes **menopausal syndrome**, including sudden hot flushes and trouble sleeping.

Men don't go through a sudden change like that. Their testosterone peaks at about age <InlineMath math="30" /> and then falls slowly, by about <InlineMath math="1\%" /> a year. This gradual change is called **andropause**.

Another problem is **infertility**: a couple can't have a baby even after trying for at least a year. The cause can be in the man, the woman or both. Each cause blocks one of the steps you've already followed in the models:

| Step that's blocked | Cause in the man | Cause in the woman |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Making gametes | The testes make no sperm, or too few or abnormal sperm | The ovaries don't release a secondary oocyte |
| Gametes meeting | A blocked sperm duct, or impotence | A blocked Fallopian tube |
| Hormone control | Hormone imbalance | Hormone imbalance |
| Implantation | None | An abnormal uterus, or a growth in the uterus |

**Impotence**, also called erectile dysfunction, is when a man can't get or keep an erection firm enough for sex. Erection depends on blood filling spongy tissue, so anything damaging blood vessels or nerves can cause it.

Causes include diabetes, cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, nerve damage, low testosterone, some medicines, smoking, heavy alcohol use, drugs, anxiety, stress and depression.

Some books use the word impotency for this whole topic. Strictly, impotence is only one cause of infertility. An impotent man may still make healthy sperm, but the sperm don't reach the oocyte.

Treatment for infertility depends on its cause: hormone treatment, surgery to open a blocked tube or duct, or **in vitro fertilisation** (IVF). In IVF, the ovum and sperm are joined in a lab dish. The embryo that forms is then placed in the uterus to implant, just like the blastocyst in the model earlier.

## Growth Curves of Animals

You're taller now than when you were five, and you'll never shrink back. **Growth** is a permanent, irreversible increase in cell number, size, volume and body mass, while cells also **differentiate and specialise** into types like muscle and nerve cells.

The word **permanent** matters. If you drink a litre of water, your mass goes up by about a kilogram for a while, but that isn't growth, because the water leaves again. So to measure growth, pick something you can measure again and again. That's a **growth parameter**:

| Parameter | Strength | Weakness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Height or length | Easy to measure without harming the organism | Doesn't show an increase in width or mass |
| Fresh mass | Easy to weigh on a living organism | Changes with the water drunk and the food in the gut |
| Dry mass | The most accurate, because all the water has been removed | The organism has to be dried, so it dies |

For people and living animals, we measure height and fresh mass. If you plot an animal's mass against its age over its whole life, you get an S-shaped curve called a **sigmoid growth curve**. In this graph, move the age and check the growth rate, which tells you how fast the mass is changing.

Each part of the curve is a phase, and the growth rate tells you which phase is happening:

| Phase | Shape of the curve | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Lag phase | Rises slowly | The body is still small, so few cells are dividing |
| Rapid growth phase | Steepest | Many cells divide quickly |
| Slow growth phase | Levels off | The growth rate falls as the body nears adult size |
| Stationary phase | Flat | About as many new cells are made as cells die |
| Senescence | Falls | Cells die faster than they're replaced, so mass drops |
| Death phase | The curve ends | The organism dies |

Insects grow in a completely different way. An insect's body is wrapped in a hard **exoskeleton** made of chitin. The exoskeleton can't stretch, like a suit of armour that can't be let out. So the insect has to take it off. Shedding the exoskeleton is called **ecdysis**:

1. A new, soft exoskeleton forms under the old one.
2. The insect squeezes its muscles and takes in air so its body swells, and the old exoskeleton splits.
3. The insect climbs out, and its body keeps swelling while the new exoskeleton is still soft.
4. Within a few hours, the new exoskeleton hardens at a bigger size.

The stage between two moults is called an **instar**. Body length jumps at each ecdysis, then stays almost the same through the instar. So a graph of body length against time looks like a flight of stairs, and it's called a **staircase growth curve**.

Insects also change shape as they grow, which is **metamorphosis**. A grasshopper goes through **incomplete metamorphosis**: the egg hatches into a wingless nymph that moults five or six times before becoming a winged adult.

A butterfly goes through **complete metamorphosis**: egg, larva or caterpillar, pupa, then adult. A monarch caterpillar goes through five instars and grows from about <InlineMath math="2\text{ mm}" /> to <InlineMath math="45\text{ mm}" /> long.
