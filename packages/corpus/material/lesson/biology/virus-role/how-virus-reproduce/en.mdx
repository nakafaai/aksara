## Viruses Replicate inside Host Cells

Cells can increase their number by dividing. Viruses use a different process: their genetic material enters a suitable host cell and directs the cell to build viral parts.

### Viral Replication Uses Cell Machinery

Bacteria can divide from one cell into two cells. A virus delivers its genome into a host cell and redirects that cell's machinery to make new viral components.

Once the viral genome enters, the host cell can be redirected to make capsid proteins, copy the viral genome, assemble new virions, and release them.

For many viruses, infection can proceed only after a viral surface protein binds to a compatible receptor on the host cell. [OpenStax Biology 2e](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/21-2-virus-infections-and-hosts) explains this relationship between a virus, its host, and infection.

Entry and genome-copying mechanisms vary among viruses. A bacteriophage may inject only its genome, while many animal viruses enter as a complete particle. The enzymes used to copy the genome may come from the host cell, the virus, or both. Every mechanism still requires a suitable host cell.

### The Bacteriophage Lytic or Lysogenic Path Starts with the Genome

Replication order is also used in microbiology to distinguish virulent phages from temperate phages. A temperate phage is a bacteriophage that may enter the host chromosome before destroying the cell. [OpenStax Microbiology](https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/6-2-the-viral-life-cycle) explains that its genome can be copied with the host chromosome before becoming active again. During lysogeny, the viral genome remains present and can later return to the lytic cycle.

The lytic and lysogenic cycles in this lesson describe bacteriophages. Animal viruses use a wider range of replication strategies, and latency or persistent infection should not be renamed lysogeny.

## The Lytic Cycle Starts with Attachment

The lytic cycle follows an ordered sequence from cell recognition to the release of newly assembled virions. Each step describes a different event.

### Adsorption Decides Which Cell Fits

The lytic cycle begins with **adsorption**, attachment of the virus to the host cell. Attachment depends on a match between viral proteins and cell receptors. Without that molecular match, replication is unlikely to begin.

After attachment comes **penetration**, when the virus delivers its genome or another part needed for replication into the cell. The next stage is **synthesis**, when the host cell is directed to copy viral genomes and make capsid proteins. When the materials are ready, **assembly** builds new virions. In many lytic examples, the cell then undergoes **lysis**, the breaking of the cell so new virions can exit.

### Synthesis Produces Parts and Assembly Builds Virions

Each stage describes a different event. Attachment determines which cell the virus can bind to. During synthesis, the host cell copies viral genomes and produces viral proteins. Assembly joins those parts into virions, and release carries the virions out of the cell.

| Phage lytic stage | What happens | Easy confusion to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adsorption | The virus attaches to a matching host receptor | Attachment is not yet reproduction |
| Penetration | Viral genome or material needed for replication enters | The whole outside shell may not enter in every virus |
| Synthesis | Viral genomes and proteins are made | The cell is producing the parts needed for new viruses |
| Assembly and release | New virions form and leave | The parts join to form new viruses, which then leave the cell |

> Viruses do not divide like bacteria. The host cell first makes viral parts, then those parts are assembled into new virions.

## A Prophage Can Remain in the Host Genome

In the lysogenic cycle, the viral genome does not immediately command the cell to make many virions. The genome can integrate or be carried with the host genetic material. When the host cell divides, viral information can be copied too.

During lysogeny, the viral genome remains inside the cell and is copied with the host genetic material. Certain conditions can activate it and shift the virus into the lytic cycle.

An integrated phage genome is called a **prophage**, and its return to the lytic cycle is called **induction**. Animal viruses can also form long-lasting or inactive infections, but terms such as latency and persistent infection describe different biological processes. They should not be treated as exact synonyms for a prophage integrated into a bacterial chromosome.

## Viral Parts Are Made before They Are Assembled

The confusing part is often separating synthesis from assembly. During synthesis, the host cell is making materials because genomes are copied and capsid proteins are produced. During assembly, those materials are being put together into new virions.

In viral replication, separate parts are made first and then assembled. This sequence differs from cell division and explains how many new virions can form after a virus redirects host-cell machinery.
