## Biology and Working Safely in the Lab

**Biology** is the study of living things: their structure, function, growth, origin and links with their surroundings. This chapter answers four questions. What fields and careers does biology hold? How do you work **safely in a biology lab**? How do you report data in tables, graphs and drawings? And how do you plan a fair scientific investigation?

In these notes you'll zoom from a cell down to a virus, read the human insulin gene, watch one bacterium become a colony, move a lamp in front of Hydrilla and rotate a body model to learn the body planes.

## Fields of Biology and Their Careers

Biology is far too wide for one scientist, so it splits into **fields of study**. Each field opens particular careers:

| Field | Study of | Example careers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Botany | Plants | Botanist, agricultural officer |
| Zoology | Animals | Zoologist, wildlife officer |
| Microbiology | Microorganisms | Microbiologist, medical lab technologist |
| Anatomy | Body structure of organisms | Doctor, surgeon |
| Physiology | How body parts work | Physiologist, physiotherapist |
| Genetics | Genes and inheritance | Geneticist, forensic scientist |
| Biochemistry | Chemical reactions in organisms | Biochemist, pharmacist |
| Ecology | Links between organisms and their surroundings | Environmental officer |
| Biotechnology | Using organisms to make products | Biotechnology engineer |
| Cytology | Cells | Cytologist |
| Histology | Tissues | Histopathology technologist |

These fields also differ in **the size of what they study**. A cytologist looks at animal cells about <InlineMath math="20\,\mu\text{m}" /> across, a microbiologist studies bacteria <InlineMath math="2\,\mu\text{m}" /> long, and a virologist studies virions <InlineMath math="0.1\,\mu\text{m}" /> across. ## The Human Insulin Gene inside Bacteria

Progress in biology often comes from combining fields. A well-known example is **human insulin**. People with type <InlineMath math="1" /> diabetes don't make enough insulin. Since 1978, biotechnologists have put the human insulin gene into the bacterium Escherichia coli. The bacteria multiply quickly, and every cell reads the gene to make human insulin in large amounts.

A gene is a sequence of DNA bases. The model below builds the first <InlineMath math="30" /> base pairs of the human insulin gene, exactly as in the [record NM_000207.3 in the NCBI database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NM_000207.3). Every three bases form one **codon**, and the first codon, ATG, is the start codon.

## Personal Safety in the Biology Lab

A biology lab holds two kinds of hazard: chemicals and **biological materials** such as microorganisms, blood and tissue. Lab rules protect you from both, and they also protect the people who use the lab after you.

### Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment puts a barrier between your body and a hazardous material. Each item guards a particular part of the body:

| Equipment | Function |
| :--- | :--- |
| Lab coat | Protects skin and clothing from spills |
| Gloves | Keep chemicals and microorganisms off your hands |
| Safety goggles | Protect your eyes from splashes |
| Face mask | Stops you breathing in particles, fumes or microorganisms |
| Closed shoes | Protect your feet from spills and broken glass |

Work that gives off harmful fumes goes in a **fume chamber**, and work with microorganisms goes in a biological safety cabinet. Daily habits matter too: don't eat or drink in the lab, tie back long hair, never pipette by mouth, and wash your hands with soap before you leave.

### What May and May Not Go Down the Sink

A sink drains straight into the sewer, so only substances that won't pollute the water or damage the pipes can go down it, with plenty of water. **Non-toxic water-based solutions**, such as dilute salt and sugar solutions, and dilute acids or alkalis that have been neutralised, can go down the sink.

Substances that must not go down the sink include organic solvents, flammable substances, heavy metal compounds such as mercury, oils, concentrated acids and alkalis, and solids such as agar and filter paper. These go into a labelled waste container. **Microbial cultures never go straight down the sink**, because living microorganisms can spread.

## Managing Biological Waste and Accidents

Biological waste is anything that contains, or has touched, living material, such as used culture plates, blood and animal carcasses. Why does one culture plate need such careful treatment? Two hours hold <InlineMath math="120 \div 20 = 6" /> divisions, so one cell becomes <InlineMath math="2^6 = 64" /> cells. After ten hours it could become <InlineMath math="2^{30} \approx 1.07 \times 10^9" /> cells if food lasts. Any cell left on waste keeps multiplying, so biological waste must be **decontaminated before disposal**.

| Type of waste | How to manage it |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sharps: needles, scalpel blades, broken glass slides | Put into a puncture-proof sharps container |
| Contaminated solids: culture plates, gloves | Put into a biohazard bag, then autoclave |
| Biological liquids: liquid cultures | Autoclave or treat with disinfectant before disposal |
| Animal carcasses and tissue | Wrap in a plastic bag, keep in a freezer, then burn in an incinerator |

An **autoclave** heats waste with pressurised steam at <InlineMath math="121^\circ\text{C}" />. That temperature kills microorganisms and their spores, which survive boiling at <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" />.

If an accident happens, tell your teacher straight away. Rinse a chemical off your skin or eyes under plenty of running water for at least <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes. Sprinkle sulfur powder over a mercury spill so the mercury turns into mercury sulfide, which doesn't evaporate. Sweep up broken glass with a brush and dustpan, never with your hands. Cover a culture spill with paper towels and pour disinfectant over it before you clean it up.

## Planning a Scientific Investigation

A scientific investigation starts with a **problem** you can test. The steps are: identify the problem, form a hypothesis, plan the experiment, carry it out and collect data, interpret the data, draw a conclusion, and write a report.

Take one problem: does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis? The water plant Hydrilla releases oxygen bubbles while it photosynthesises, so the number of bubbles per minute measures the rate of photosynthesis. A closer lamp gives brighter light. Light intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance:

<BlockMath math="I \propto \frac{1}{d^2}" />

### Example Naming the Variables and the Hypothesis

For the Hydrilla investigation, state the variables and one testable hypothesis.

- **Manipulated variable**: the distance of the lamp from Hydrilla, which sets the light intensity.
- **Responding variable**: the number of oxygen bubbles per minute.
- **Fixed variables**: water temperature, the type and length of the Hydrilla, and the concentration of sodium hydrogen carbonate.
- **Hypothesis**: the closer the lamp is to Hydrilla, the higher the rate at which oxygen bubbles are released.

Check it against the data: as the distance falls from <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />, the rate rises from <InlineMath math="8" /> to <InlineMath math="42" /> bubbles per minute. The hypothesis is accepted.

### Example Comparing Light Intensities

The lamp moves from <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" />. How many times brighter is the light?

<BlockMath math="\frac{I_{20}}{I_{40}} = \left(\frac{40}{20}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4" />

The intensity becomes **four times greater**, but the bubble rate only rises from <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="30" />, which is <InlineMath math="2.5" /> times. The rate doesn't rise as much as the intensity because other factors, such as carbon dioxide, also limit photosynthesis. Check with the model: the relative intensity is <InlineMath math="6.25" /> at <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="1.5625" /> at <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}" />, and <InlineMath math="6.25 \div 1.5625 = 4" />.

## Data Tables and Graphs and Biological Drawings

Investigation data goes into a **table** first. The first column holds the manipulated variable, the next column the responding variable, and every column heading carries its unit:

| Lamp distance (cm) | Bubbles per minute |
| :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="42" /> |
| <InlineMath math="20" /> | <InlineMath math="30" /> |
| <InlineMath math="30" /> | <InlineMath math="19" /> |
| <InlineMath math="40" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="50" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> |

Then you plot the data. **The manipulated variable goes on the horizontal axis** and the responding variable on the vertical axis. Both axes carry a label with a unit and an even scale. Distance is continuous data, so a line graph fits. Data in separate categories, such as the number of students in each blood group, goes in a bar chart.

A **biological drawing** records the specimen you observed. Use a sharp pencil, draw smooth, unbroken lines with no shading or colour, and keep the parts in their true proportions. Draw label lines straight with a ruler, never crossing and without arrowheads. Write a title and the magnification of the drawing:

<BlockMath math="\text{Magnification} = \frac{\text{drawing size}}{\text{actual size}}" />

Try an example. A cheek cell <InlineMath math="60\,\mu\text{m}" /> long is drawn <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> long. Find the magnification of the drawing. Put **both sizes in the same unit** first: <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm} = 60\text{ mm}" /> and <InlineMath math="60\,\mu\text{m} = 0.06\text{ mm}" />.

<BlockMath math="\text{Magnification} = \frac{60\text{ mm}}{0.06\text{ mm}} = 1000" />

Write the magnification as <InlineMath math="\times 1000" />. Check: <InlineMath math="0.06\text{ mm} \times 1000 = 60\text{ mm}" />, the length of the drawing.

## Body Planes and Direction Terms

Anatomists cut and describe the body with fixed terms so everyone means the same part. The terms refer to a person standing upright and facing forward. There are three **body planes**:

- **The sagittal plane** divides the body into left and right parts.
- **The frontal plane** divides the body into front and back parts.
- **The transverse plane** divides the body into upper and lower parts.

Each direction term has an opposite partner. **Superior** means towards the head and inferior towards the feet. Anterior, or ventral, means towards the front of the body, while posterior, or dorsal, means towards the back. Lateral means away from the midline of the body and medial means towards it. On a limb, proximal means close to where it joins the body and distal means far from it.

Specimens such as plant stems are cut in two ways. **A cross section** is cut across the long axis and shows the tissues arranged in a ring. A longitudinal section is cut along the long axis and shows the tissues running from end to end.

## Common Mistakes in the Biology Introduction Chapter

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Pouring a bacterial culture down the sink after rinsing it. | Living bacteria keep multiplying and spread through the sewer. | Autoclave the culture first. |
| Putting the responding variable on the horizontal axis. | The horizontal axis belongs to the factor you change. | Ask: which factor did I set before measuring? |
| Calculating magnification with sizes in different units. | Centimetres and micrometres differ by <InlineMath math="10^4" />, so the answer is off by that factor. | Convert both sizes to millimetres first. |
| Shading a biological drawing and labelling with arrows. | Shading hides structures, and arrows don't point exactly at a part. | Label lines are straight and end exactly on the part. |

The first mistake is the most dangerous. **Decontaminate biological waste first**, and only then throw it away.

## Practice on Introduction to Biology

These questions move from lab rules to calculations. **Show your steps and units** in every answer.

1. State how to dispose of each of these: a used dilute sodium chloride solution, a Petri dish with bacterial colonies, and a broken glass slide with blood on it.

2. A student studies the effect of temperature on the growth rate of yeast in sugar solution. State the manipulated variable, the responding variable, two fixed variables and one hypothesis.

3. An onion cell is drawn <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm}" /> long at a magnification of <InlineMath math="\times 400" />. Calculate the actual length of the cell in micrometres.

4. A frog is cut into a left half and a right half. Name that plane. Then a balsam stem is cut across to look at its vascular bundles. Name that section.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Dilute sodium chloride solution is non-toxic and dissolves in water, so it **can go down the sink** with plenty of running water. The Petri dish with cultures goes into a biohazard bag and is autoclaved at <InlineMath math="121^\circ\text{C}" /> before disposal. A broken slide with blood on it is a contaminated sharp, so it goes into a sharps container.

**Solution 2**: The manipulated variable is the temperature of the solution, because the student sets it. The responding variable is the growth rate of the yeast, for example the volume of gas released in a set time. Fixed variables include the concentration and volume of the sugar solution and the mass of yeast. Hypothesis: **the higher the temperature**, the higher the growth rate of the yeast, up to the optimum temperature.

**Solution 3**: Rearrange the magnification formula to find the actual size, then convert the unit.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Actual size} = \frac{\text{drawing size}}{\text{magnification}} = \frac{90\text{ mm}}{400} = 0.225\text{ mm}" />
<BlockMath math="0.225\text{ mm} \times 1000 = 225\,\mu\text{m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="0.225\text{ mm} \times 400 = 90\text{ mm} = 9\text{ cm}" />, the length of the drawing.

**Solution 4**: A cut that separates left from right is the **sagittal plane**. A stem cut across its long axis gives a cross section, which shows the vascular bundles arranged in a ring.
