## Careers Built on Living Things

**Biology** is the study of living things, from bacteria too small to see to the tree outside your window. Biologists spend a lot of time in the lab, so you need three skills: how to **work safely in the lab**, how to record what you see with drawings and tables, and how to test one idea fairly. In five lab models you'll pick out equipment, send waste to the right place, practise the steps after an accident, draw a cell and cut a body along its planes.

Biology is far too big for one scientist. One person studies plants, another studies germs, and another studies how the heart beats. Each of these narrower studies is called a **field of biology**, and each field leads to careers you'll find in Malaysia:

| Field | What it studies | A career in Malaysia |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Botany | Plants | Crop researcher at MARDI |
| Zoology | Animals | Wildlife ranger with PERHILITAN |
| Microbiology | Microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi | Medical laboratory technologist in a hospital |
| Physiology | How organs and the body work | Physiotherapist |
| Genetics | Genes and how traits are inherited | Forensic DNA chemist at the Department of Chemistry Malaysia |
| Ecology | How organisms interact with their surroundings | Forest researcher at FRIM |

Every career in that table shares one workplace: the lab. A biology lab holds two kinds of hazard at once. There are chemicals, and there are biological materials such as bacterial cultures, blood and tissue. Lab rules protect you from both, and they also protect the student who uses the same bench after you.

## Kit That Protects Your Body

Think about your body first, before you touch anything. Your skin can catch a spill, your eyes can catch a splash, and your nose can breathe in vapour or tiny droplets. Anything you wear to stop those hazards from reaching your body is called **personal protective equipment**: a lab coat, safety goggles, gloves and a face mask.

That equipment is only one of three groups of things in a lab. **Apparatus** is what you run experiments with, such as a beaker or a microscope. **Safety equipment** hangs on the wall for emergencies, such as a fire blanket and an eyewash station. Pick a group, then an item, and read what it's for and how to handle it.

Lab rules are easiest to remember by time. Before a practical, tie long hair back, wear shoes that cover your feet and button your lab coat, because an open coat doesn't protect your clothes. Food, drinks and bags stay outside the lab. Germs on the bench can get onto food, and from there into your mouth.

During a practical, **put nothing in your mouth**, not even a pencil. Never pipette by mouth: use a pipette filler. Gloved hands stay away from your face, and a Bunsen flame is always watched and kept away from anything flammable. After the practical, wipe the bench with disinfectant, take off your gloves, then wash your hands with soap and water before you leave.

## Sorting Lab Waste Safely

After a practical, your bench is full of things to throw away. One bin for everything isn't safe. A sharp blade can cut the cleaner, live germs can spread, and chemicals can pollute rivers. So **every kind of waste has its own place**.

The sink only takes solutions that are dilute, dissolve in water and aren't toxic, such as a dilute salt solution, with plenty of running water. Organic solvents and heavy metal solutions go into a labelled chemical waste bottle. Scalpel blades and needles go into a puncture-resistant sharps container. A broken test tube or beaker that's clean goes into the broken glass box.

Waste that carries live germs, such as a Petri dish with a culture, goes into a **biohazard bag**. That bag doesn't go straight into the bin. It goes into an **autoclave** first, a steel chamber that works like a giant pressure cooker. The steam inside is trapped, so the pressure rises and the temperature can climb above the boiling point of water.

Boiling water at <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> kills most germs, but **bacterial spores** survive that temperature. An autoclave heats the waste to <InlineMath math="121^\circ\text{C}" /> and holds it there for at least <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes once the waste has reached that temperature. A large, tightly packed bag needs longer, because the steam takes time to reach its centre.

A chemical in the eye is the most urgent accident. Go straight to the eyewash station, hold your eyelids open with your fingers and rinse with running water for **at least <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes**. Fifteen minutes feels long, but the water needs that long to dilute the chemical and carry it off every part of the eye.

Some moves feel right but add danger. Compare the tempting move with the right one:

| Accident | Tempting move | Why it's wrong | Right move |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Chemical in the eye | Rubbing the eye | Rubbing can add to the damage | Rinse with running water, eyelids held open |
| Mercury spill | Sweeping it with a brush | A brush breaks mercury into smaller droplets and spreads them | Gather the beads with a card, then sprinkle sulfur powder |
| Mercury spill | Vacuuming it up | A vacuum cleaner blows mercury into the air as vapour | Let the teacher clean it up with a card and a dropper |
| Burning spirit | Pouring water on it | Water spreads the burning liquid | Cover it with the fire blanket |

Look at the brush in that table. A brush and dustpan are the right tools for broken glass, yet the same tools are wrong for mercury. So **the right move depends on what spilled**, and that's why you tell your teacher before you touch anything.

## Testing One Idea Fairly

Say you want to know whether warm soil makes corn seeds come up faster. You plant one seed in warm soil and one in cold soil, but you plant the second one deeper. If the second seed comes up late, was it the temperature or the depth? You can't tell. A fair test **changes only one thing** and keeps everything else the same.

A scientific investigation starts with a **problem**, a question you can test: does soil temperature affect how long corn seedlings take to come up? Then you write a **hypothesis**, a possible answer that links two things: the higher the soil temperature, the fewer days corn seedlings take to come up. A good hypothesis is one that data could prove wrong.

Anything that can change in an experiment is called a **variable**, and each one has its own job:

| Variable | What it means | In the corn test |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Manipulated | What you change on purpose | Soil temperature |
| Responding | What you measure because it changes | Number of days until the seedling appears |
| Fixed | What you keep the same | Type of seed, planting depth, type and wetness of soil |

This test has really been done. J. F. Harrington of the University of California, Davis, compiled how many days seedlings took to appear at different soil temperatures, for seeds planted half an inch deep, which is <InlineMath math="1.27\text{ cm}" />. His table is in degrees Fahrenheit. Here are the corn results converted to degrees Celsius:

| Soil temperature (<InlineMath math="^\circ\text{C}" />) | Time until the seedling appears (days) |
| :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="22" /> |
| <InlineMath math="15" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="20" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> |
| <InlineMath math="25" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> |
| <InlineMath math="30" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> |

Notice how that table is laid out. The manipulated variable fills the first column, the responding variable the second, and **the units sit in the column headings**, so each cell holds only a number. If you draw a graph, soil temperature goes on the horizontal axis and the number of days on the vertical axis. Both axes are labelled with units and use an even scale.

Temperature can take any value between your readings, so you join the points on the graph with a line. If the manipulated variable were a set of separate categories, such as type of seed, you'd use a bar chart. For the corn data, the line falls steeply from <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" />, then levels off.

Now write a **conclusion that follows the data**. From <InlineMath math="10^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" />, the time falls from <InlineMath math="22" /> days to <InlineMath math="4" /> days, so the hypothesis is accepted in that range. From <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="30^\circ\text{C}" />, the time stays at <InlineMath math="4" /> days. Harrington's table also records little or no corn germination at <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />. An honest conclusion names the temperature range where the hypothesis holds.

Your turn. In an investigation, <InlineMath math="20" /> mung beans are placed in each dish of salt solution at a different concentration, and the number of beans that sprout is counted after <InlineMath math="3" /> days. Name the manipulated variable, the responding variable and two fixed variables before you read the answer.

The manipulated variable is **the concentration of the salt solution**, because that's what differs from dish to dish. The responding variable is the number of beans that sprout after <InlineMath math="3" /> days, because that's what gets counted. Fixed variables include the number of beans in each dish, the volume of solution, the temperature and the type of bean. If the volumes differed, you couldn't tell whether the salt or the amount of water caused the difference.

## Drawing from the Microscope

Cells under a microscope don't last long: the slide dries out and the specimen gets damaged. A **biological drawing** keeps what you saw so other people can read it later. The drawing has two jobs: its lines are clean and honest, and it states how many times bigger it is than the real specimen.

### Rules for Clean Lines

Every line in a biological drawing stands for a real boundary on the specimen. So extra lines, shading or colour give false information. Draw with a sharp pencil and use **single, continuous lines**. Keep each part in proportion, so a small nucleus on the specimen stays small in your drawing.

Draw label lines straight with a ruler, with no arrowheads and no crossings, and make each one end exactly on the part it labels. Line the labels up on one side of the drawing. Below the drawing, write a title and the magnification. Play the cheek cell drawing in the model, then switch to the faulty version and read why each fault is wrong.

### Finding Magnification

Your drawing is much bigger than the real cell. **Magnification** tells you how many times bigger, and you find it by dividing the drawing size by the actual size:

<BlockMath math="\text{Magnification} = \dfrac{\text{drawing size}}{\text{actual size}}" />

Both sizes must be in **the same unit** before you divide, and one millimetre is a thousand micrometres: <InlineMath math="1\text{ mm} = 1{,}000\ \mu\text{m}" />. The cheek cell in the model is about <InlineMath math="50\ \mu\text{m}" /> across, the average measured for cheek cells from healthy adults. Suppose you draw that cell <InlineMath math="40\text{ mm}" /> long:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="40\text{ mm} = 40 \times 1{,}000\ \mu\text{m} = 40{,}000\ \mu\text{m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Magnification} = \dfrac{40{,}000\ \mu\text{m}}{50\ \mu\text{m}} = 800" />
</MathContainer>

Write it as <InlineMath math="\times 800" /> below the drawing. Check it the other way round: <InlineMath math="50\ \mu\text{m} \times 800 = 40{,}000\ \mu\text{m}" />, which is <InlineMath math="40\text{ mm}" />, the length of the drawing.

If you forget to convert and just divide <InlineMath math="40" /> by <InlineMath math="50" />, you get <InlineMath math="0.8" />. A magnification below <InlineMath math="1" /> would mean the drawing is smaller than the cell, yet you can't even see that cell with the naked eye. A strange answer like that tells you the units don't match yet.

Drawing magnification is also different from microscope magnification. [A microscope's magnification is the eyepiece lens multiplied by the objective lens](/en/subjects/biology/cell-and-organisation/cell-structure-and-specialised-cells), and it doesn't depend on how big you draw.

Now try it the other way round. A drawing of a cheek cell is <InlineMath math="54\text{ mm}" /> long, and its magnification is <InlineMath math="\times 1{,}200" />. What is the actual size of the cell in micrometres?

Rearrange the formula so that **actual size is drawing size divided by magnification**, then convert millimetres to micrometres:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Actual size} = \dfrac{54\text{ mm}}{1{,}200} = 0.045\text{ mm}" />
<BlockMath math="0.045\text{ mm} = 0.045 \times 1{,}000\ \mu\text{m} = 45\ \mu\text{m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="45\ \mu\text{m} \times 1{,}200 = 54{,}000\ \mu\text{m}" />, which is <InlineMath math="54\text{ mm}" />. The value <InlineMath math="45\ \mu\text{m}" /> is also sensible, because it's close to the measured size of a cheek cell, about <InlineMath math="50\ \mu\text{m}" />.

## Cutting the Body along Planes

Picture cutting a watermelon. Cut straight down through the middle and you get a left half and a right half. Cut across and you get a top part and a bottom part. Biologists cut bodies and organs the same way, and the flat surface the knife passes through is called a **plane**.

There are three body planes. **The sagittal plane** separates the left and right parts. The frontal plane, also called the coronal plane, separates the front and back parts. The horizontal plane, also called the transverse plane, separates the upper and lower parts. All of these planes refer to the body in the **anatomical position**: standing upright, with the face and palms facing forward.

Direction terms come in pairs. Superior means closer to the head, and inferior closer to the feet. Anterior means towards the front of the body, and posterior towards the back. Medial means closer to the midline of the body, and lateral further from it. On the arms and legs, **proximal means closer to where the limb joins the body**, so the elbow is proximal to the wrist.

Small specimens such as plant stems are cut thin so the microscope's light can pass through. A **transverse section** is cut across the stem's long axis, like slicing a cucumber into round pieces. A longitudinal section is cut along the long axis, from end to end. In a dicot stem, a transverse section shows the vascular bundles arranged in a ring, while a longitudinal section shows the same bundles as long strands.
