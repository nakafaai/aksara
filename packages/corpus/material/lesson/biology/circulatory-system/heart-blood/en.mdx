## The Circulatory System Carries Substances around the Body

The **circulatory system** is made up of **the heart, blood vessels and blood**. The heart pumps blood through arteries, capillaries and veins to bring oxygen, nutrients and hormones to cells and to carry away carbon dioxide and urea. Humans have a **closed double circulatory system**: blood always stays inside vessels and passes through the heart twice in each complete circuit. In the models here, you read pressures in the cardiac cycle, calculate cardiac output, compare blood pressure along the vessels and see how a vessel's radius affects blood flow.

## Blood and Its Components

Blood is made up of **plasma** and blood cells. Plasma, about <InlineMath math="55\%" /> of blood volume, carries nutrients, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide.

| Component | Structure | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Erythrocytes | Biconcave discs with no nucleus | Carry oxygen with haemoglobin |
| Leucocytes | Have a nucleus, larger than erythrocytes | Fight pathogens by phagocytosis and antibodies |
| Platelets | Small cell fragments | Start blood clotting |
| Plasma | Pale yellow fluid, about <InlineMath math="90\%" /> water | Carries dissolved substances and heat |

### Blood Clotting

When a blood vessel is cut, platelets and damaged tissue release **thromboplastin**. With the help of calcium ions and vitamin K, thromboplastin turns prothrombin into **thrombin**. Thrombin then turns soluble fibrinogen into insoluble **fibrin**. Fibrin threads trap red blood cells and form a clot that stops the bleeding.

### Blood Groups

The ABO system depends on **antigens** on the surface of erythrocytes and **antibodies** in the plasma. Donor blood must not carry an antigen that the recipient's antibodies recognise, or the donor's erythrocytes will clump.

| Group | Antigen | Antibody | Can receive from |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A and B | None | A, B, AB, O |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B | O only |

## The Heart and the Cardiac Cycle

The human heart has **four chambers**: two atria above and two ventricles below. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps oxygenated blood around the whole body through the **aorta**. The tricuspid and bicuspid valves stop blood flowing back into the atria, while the semilunar valves stop blood flowing back into the ventricles. Each heartbeat starts at the **sinoatrial node**, the heart's natural pacemaker.

### Stages of the Cardiac Cycle

At the start of the cycle, the atria contract and fill the ventricles. The ventricles then contract, and the bicuspid valve closes, making the **first heart sound**. When ventricular pressure rises above aortic pressure, the semilunar valve opens and blood is pumped out. When the ventricle relaxes and its pressure falls below aortic pressure, the semilunar valve closes, making the second sound.

### Example Cardiac Output

**Cardiac output** is the volume of blood one ventricle pumps in one minute:

<BlockMath math="\text{Cardiac output} = \text{stroke volume} \times \text{heart rate}" />

A student's stroke volume is <InlineMath math="70\text{ cm}^3" /> and their heart rate is <InlineMath math="72" /> beats a minute.

<BlockMath math="70 \times 72 = 5040\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1} = 5.04\text{ dm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" />

Check: an adult has about <InlineMath math="5\text{ dm}^3" /> of blood, so the whole blood volume passes through the left ventricle roughly once a minute at rest. Change the stroke volume and heart rate in this model.

## Blood Vessels and Blood Pressure

| Vessel | Wall | Lumen | Valves | Pressure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Artery | Thick, muscular and elastic | Small | None | High and pulsing |
| Capillary | One cell thick | Very small | None | Low |
| Vein | Thin, less muscular | Large | Present | Lowest |

**Blood pressure** is measured with a sphygmomanometer and given as systolic pressure over diastolic pressure, for example <InlineMath math="120/80\text{ mmHg}" />. Pressure is highest in the aorta and falls steadily as blood flows through smaller and smaller vessels. This graph shows the mean pressure in each type of vessel.

### Example Reading Blood Pressure

As the cuff of a mercury sphygmomanometer is let down, the pulse sounds start at a mercury column of <InlineMath math="12.0\text{ cm}" /> and disappear at <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ cm}" />. State **the blood pressure reading** in mmHg and kPa.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="12.0\text{ cmHg} = 120\text{ mmHg}, \quad 8.0\text{ cmHg} = 80\text{ mmHg}" />
<BlockMath math="120 \times 0.133 = 16.0\text{ kPa}, \quad 80 \times 0.133 = 10.6\text{ kPa}" />
</MathContainer>

The reading is <InlineMath math="120/80\text{ mmHg}" />, which is normal. Check: the systolic value of <InlineMath math="16.0\text{ kPa}" /> matches the peak of the left ventricle curve in the cardiac cycle model.

### Vessel Radius and Blood Flow

Blood flow through a vessel is very sensitive to its radius. At the same pressure, flow is proportional to **the fourth power of the radius**. When an arteriole constricts, flow to that tissue drops sharply. That's why cholesterol plaques that narrow the coronary arteries, which is **atherosclerosis**, are so dangerous.

## The Lymphatic System

Some plasma leaks out of the capillaries into the spaces between cells as **tissue fluid**. Most of it returns to the capillaries, and the rest enters **lymph capillaries** as **lymph**. Lymph flows through lymph vessels and **lymph nodes**, where lymphocytes destroy pathogens, before it returns to the blood through the subclavian veins. The lymphatic system also carries lipids from the lacteals in the villi.

## Circulatory Systems in Animals

| Animal | Type of system | Features |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Insects | Open | Haemolymph bathes the organs directly in the haemocoel |
| Fish | Closed, single circulation | Two-chambered heart, blood passes through the heart once per circuit |
| Amphibians | Closed, incomplete double circulation | Three-chambered heart, some mixing of blood in the ventricle |
| Humans | Closed, complete double circulation | Four-chambered heart, oxygenated and deoxygenated blood don't mix |

**Double circulation** lets blood be pumped back to the body at high pressure after it passes through the lungs, so oxygen reaches the tissues faster.

## Common Mistakes about Transport

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| All arteries carry oxygenated blood. | The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs. | Arteries are defined by the direction of flow, away from the heart. |
| The right ventricle has the thickest wall. | The left ventricle is thickest because it pumps blood around the whole body. | Compare how far the blood has to travel. |
| Group AB can donate to everyone. | Group AB is the universal recipient. Group O is the universal donor. | Check the donor's antigens against the recipient's antibodies. |
| Veins have high pressure. | Veins have the lowest pressure and rely on valves and skeletal muscle. | Compare the thickness of the vessel walls. |

The first mistake is the most common one in questions on blood vessels. **Arteries go away from the heart, veins go into the heart**, whatever their oxygen content.

## Practice on Transport in Humans and Animals

These questions move from blood to the heart and blood pressure. **Show units** in every calculation.

1. An athlete's stroke volume is <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> and their heart rate is <InlineMath math="50" /> beats a minute. Calculate their cardiac output in <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" />.

2. A patient has blood group B. Name the blood groups that can safely be given to them and explain why.

3. A plaque narrows an artery's radius to <InlineMath math="0.8" /> times its original radius. Calculate the new blood flow as a percentage of the original flow.

4. Explain why veins have valves but arteries don't.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Multiply the stroke volume by the heart rate:

<BlockMath math="100 \times 50 = 5000\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1} = 5.0\text{ dm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" />

Check: this is almost the same as an ordinary student's cardiac output, because an athlete's heart pumps more blood with each beat and doesn't need to beat as often.

**Solution 2**: Groups **B and O** are safe. The patient's plasma contains anti-A antibodies, so blood that carries antigen A, groups A and AB, would clump.

**Solution 3**: Flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius:

<BlockMath math="0.8^4 = 0.41 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 41\%" />

Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="r = 3.2" />, which is <InlineMath math="0.8 \times 4" />, the percentage reads <InlineMath math="41" />. A small narrowing already cuts the flow by more than half.

**Solution 4**: Pressure in veins is **very low**, so blood can flow backwards, especially from the legs against gravity. Valves stop this backflow. In arteries, the high pressure from ventricular contraction already pushes blood forwards.
