## How Traits Pass from Parents to Offspring

**Inheritance** is the passing of traits from parents to offspring through **genes**. A gene is a stretch of DNA at a fixed place on a chromosome, and it comes in different forms called **alleles**. Each body cell carries two alleles of every gene, one from each parent. Gregor Mendel found two rules by crossing pea plants:

> A Punnett square predicts probabilities for each independent fertilisation, never an exact count guaranteed inside a small family.

- **Law of Segregation**: the two alleles of a gene separate when gametes form, so each gamete carries only one of them. Two parents that are both heterozygous give offspring in a phenotypic ratio of <InlineMath math="3:1" />.
- **Law of Independent Assortment**: the alleles of different genes go into the gametes independently of each other. Two parents heterozygous for two genes give a ratio of <InlineMath math="9:3:3:1" />.

In these notes you compare Mendel's real counts with those ratios, scale a Punnett square up to a thousand plants, split a square of probability for two genes, and follow blood groups and alleles on the X chromosome through human families.

## Genes and Alleles on a Chromosome

Genetics has its own words, and every problem uses them. The examples in the table come from plant height in peas, where the allele for tall is written <InlineMath math="T" /> and the allele for dwarf is written <InlineMath math="t" />.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gene | A unit of inheritance, a stretch of DNA that controls one characteristic | The gene for plant height |
| Locus | The fixed position of a gene on a chromosome | The height gene sits at the same locus on both homologous chromosomes |
| Allele | One of the different forms of a gene | <InlineMath math="T" /> and <InlineMath math="t" /> |
| Dominant allele | Shows its trait even when only one copy is present | <InlineMath math="T" /> |
| Recessive allele | Shows its trait only when two copies are present | <InlineMath math="t" /> |
| Genotype | The pair of alleles an organism carries | <InlineMath math="TT" />, <InlineMath math="Tt" /> or <InlineMath math="tt" /> |
| Phenotype | The trait you can observe, set by the genotype and the environment | Tall or dwarf |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles at one locus | <InlineMath math="TT" /> or <InlineMath math="tt" /> |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles at one locus | <InlineMath math="Tt" /> |

A **characteristic** is a feature such as plant height, and a **trait** is one form of it, such as tall or dwarf. The dominant allele takes a capital letter and the recessive allele the same letter in lower case. A pure-breeding line is homozygous, so it always gives offspring like itself. In a cross, the parents are the <InlineMath math="P" /> generation, their offspring the <InlineMath math="F_1" /> generation, and the offspring of <InlineMath math="F_1" /> plants the <InlineMath math="F_2" /> generation.

## Mendel Crossed Pure Breeding Peas

Mendel chose the garden pea, *Pisum sativum*, for good reasons. Its characteristics come in **pairs of contrasting traits** that are easy to tell apart, such as violet or white flowers. A pea flower normally pollinates itself, so a line stays pure-breeding, yet Mendel could also cross two plants by moving pollen by hand. Peas grow quickly and give many seeds, so each cross gave him large counts.

In a **monohybrid cross** he followed one characteristic. He crossed pure-breeding tall plants with pure-breeding dwarf plants, and every <InlineMath math="F_1" /> plant was tall. When he let the <InlineMath math="F_1" /> plants pollinate themselves, the dwarf trait came back: the <InlineMath math="F_2" /> generation had <InlineMath math="787" /> tall and <InlineMath math="277" /> dwarf plants. The dwarf trait was hidden in <InlineMath math="F_1" />, not lost, and that is what **recessive** means.

He repeated the experiment for seven characteristics. Read the bars below and compare each with the line at <InlineMath math="3" />.

### Law of Segregation in a Punnett Square

Mendel explained the <InlineMath math="3:1" /> ratio with his first law. An <InlineMath math="F_1" /> tall plant has the genotype <InlineMath math="Tt" />. When it makes gametes by meiosis, the two homologous chromosomes separate, so **each gamete carries only one allele**: half the gametes carry <InlineMath math="T" /> and half carry <InlineMath math="t" />. At fertilisation any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete.

A schematic diagram follows the alleles from parents to offspring, and a **Punnett square** crosses every gamete of one parent with every gamete of the other:

| Gametes | <InlineMath math="T" /> | <InlineMath math="t" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="T" /> | <InlineMath math="TT" />, tall | <InlineMath math="Tt" />, tall |
| <InlineMath math="t" /> | <InlineMath math="Tt" />, tall | <InlineMath math="tt" />, dwarf |

<BlockMath math="TT : Tt : tt = 1 : 2 : 1, \qquad \text{tall} : \text{dwarf} = 3 : 1" />

The square gives **probabilities, not exact counts**. Each <InlineMath math="F_2" /> offspring has a chance of <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3}{4}" /> of being tall. Move the slider to choose how many <InlineMath math="F_2" /> plants grow, and compare the expected numbers with Mendel's real flower colour count.

A tall plant can be <InlineMath math="TT" /> or <InlineMath math="Tt" />, and the two look the same. All tall offspring means <InlineMath math="TT" />, and about half dwarf offspring means <InlineMath math="Tt" />.

### Two Characteristics in a Dihybrid Cross

In a **dihybrid cross** Mendel followed two characteristics at once. He crossed pure-breeding plants with round yellow seeds, <InlineMath math="RRYY" />, with pure-breeding plants with wrinkled green seeds, <InlineMath math="rryy" />. Every <InlineMath math="F_1" /> plant had round yellow seeds and the genotype <InlineMath math="RrYy" />.

Mendel's second law, the **Law of Independent Assortment**, says the pair of alleles for seed shape separates independently of the pair for seed colour. The genes sit on different chromosomes, and each pair of homologous chromosomes lines up at metaphase I without regard to the others. So an <InlineMath math="RrYy" /> plant makes four kinds of gamete in equal numbers: <InlineMath math="RY" />, <InlineMath math="Ry" />, <InlineMath math="rY" /> and <InlineMath math="ry" />.

| <InlineMath math="RY" /> | <InlineMath math="RRYY" /> | <InlineMath math="RRYy" /> | <InlineMath math="RrYY" /> | <InlineMath math="RrYy" /> |
| <InlineMath math="Ry" /> | <InlineMath math="RRYy" /> | <InlineMath math="RRyy" /> | <InlineMath math="RrYy" /> | <InlineMath math="Rryy" /> |
| <InlineMath math="rY" /> | <InlineMath math="RrYY" /> | <InlineMath math="RrYy" /> | <InlineMath math="rrYY" /> | <InlineMath math="rrYy" /> |

## Sex and Blood Groups in Humans

A human body cell has <InlineMath math="23" /> pairs of chromosomes: <InlineMath math="22" /> pairs of **autosomes** and one pair of **sex chromosomes**. A female is <InlineMath math="XX" /> and a male is <InlineMath math="XY" />. Every egg carries an <InlineMath math="X" />, while half the sperm carry an <InlineMath math="X" /> and half a <InlineMath math="Y" />. So **the sperm decides the sex of the child**, and each child has a chance of <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2}" /> of being a boy.

Each birth is a new fertilisation, so earlier children don't change the chance for the next one. The chance that a family has only girls shrinks as the family grows. Move the slider to choose the number of children <InlineMath math="k" />.

The ABO blood group is controlled by one gene with **three alleles**, <InlineMath math="I^A" />, <InlineMath math="I^B" /> and <InlineMath math="i" />. ## Family Pedigrees and the X Chromosome

Some genes sit on the <InlineMath math="X" /> chromosome and have no matching locus on the much smaller <InlineMath math="Y" /> chromosome. Their inheritance is **sex-linked**. Red-green colour blindness and haemophilia both come from recessive alleles on the <InlineMath math="X" /> chromosome. Write <InlineMath math="X^B" /> for normal colour vision and <InlineMath math="X^b" /> for colour blindness.

A female needs two recessive alleles, <InlineMath math="X^b X^b" />, to be colour-blind. A female with <InlineMath math="X^B X^b" /> sees normally but is a **carrier**. A male has only one <InlineMath math="X" />, so a single <InlineMath math="X^b" /> makes him colour-blind. That is why **sex-linked recessive traits are more common in males**. Cross a carrier mother with a father who sees normally:

A son always gets his <InlineMath math="X" /> from his mother and his <InlineMath math="Y" /> from his father, so **a colour-blind son inherits the allele from his mother**. Haemophilia, in which blood clots too slowly, is inherited in exactly the same way.

A **pedigree** is a family tree that shows who has a trait. A square is a male, a circle is a female, a shaded shape is an affected person, a horizontal line joins two parents, and a vertical line leads down to their children. Thalassaemia, a blood disorder in which too little normal haemoglobin is made, is caused by a recessive allele on an autosome. Write <InlineMath math="A" /> for the normal allele and <InlineMath math="a" /> for the thalassaemia allele.

Analyse the pedigree. The son is <InlineMath math="aa" /> and got one <InlineMath math="a" /> from each parent. Both parents are unaffected, so each must be <InlineMath math="Aa" />. For the next child, the cross <InlineMath math="Aa \times Aa" /> gives <InlineMath math="AA : Aa : aa = 1 : 2 : 1" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(\text{next child affected}) = \tfrac{1}{4}" />
<BlockMath math="P(\text{unaffected daughter is a carrier}) = \frac{2}{1 + 2} = \frac{2}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

The daughter is unaffected, so the <InlineMath math="aa" /> box is ruled out for her, and **two of the three remaining boxes are carriers**. Check: <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 1" />, so the daughter is either <InlineMath math="AA" /> or <InlineMath math="Aa" />.
