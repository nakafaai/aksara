## Enzymes Speed Up Metabolism

**Metabolism** is every chemical reaction that happens in the cells of an organism. Almost every one of those reactions is catalysed by an **enzyme**, a protein that acts as a **biological catalyst**. An enzyme speeds up a reaction by **lowering the activation energy**, the minimum energy needed before the substrate can turn into products. The enzyme is unchanged at the end, so the same molecule can be used again and again.

## Anabolism Builds and Catabolism Breaks Down

Metabolism comes in two kinds. **Anabolism** builds complex molecules from simple ones and needs energy. **Catabolism** breaks complex molecules down into simple ones and releases energy. In a cell both run at the same time: energy from catabolism drives anabolism.

| Feature | Anabolism | Catabolism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Molecules | Simple to complex | Complex to simple |
| Energy | Needed | Released |
| Examples | Protein synthesis from amino acids, photosynthesis | Cellular respiration, starch digestion |

Try to classify this reaction. Maltase in the small intestine hydrolyses maltose:

<BlockMath math="\text{maltose} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{maltase}} \text{glucose} + \text{glucose}" />

One disaccharide molecule becomes two monosaccharide molecules, so the reaction is **catabolism**. Check the direction: when the liver joins glucose into glycogen, the molecule gets bigger and energy is needed, so that is anabolism.

## Enzyme Properties and Names

Most enzymes are named by adding the ending **-ase** to the name of their substrate. Sucrase hydrolyses sucrose, lipase hydrolyses lipids, protease hydrolyses proteins and maltase hydrolyses maltose. A few enzymes discovered early keep their old names, such as pepsin, trypsin and rennin.

Enzymes share some properties you'll need to remember:

- Enzymes are **proteins** made by ribosomes.
- Enzymes are **specific**: one enzyme acts on one kind of substrate only.
- Enzymes are needed in **small amounts** because they are unchanged and can be reused.
- Enzymes speed up a reaction but don't change the amount of product.
- Many enzyme reactions are reversible.
- Enzymes are affected by temperature, pH, inhibitors and cofactors. A **cofactor** is an inorganic ion such as a magnesium ion, while a **coenzyme** is an organic molecule, many of them made from vitamins.

**Intracellular** enzymes are made and work inside the cell, such as the respiration enzymes in mitochondria. **Extracellular** enzymes are made inside the cell but secreted to work outside it, such as salivary amylase and pepsin. ## The Lock and Key Hypothesis

Every enzyme has an **active site**, a small region whose shape is complementary to the shape of its substrate. In the **lock and key hypothesis**, the substrate is the key and the active site is the lock. Only a substrate with the matching shape fits into the active site, and that is why enzymes are specific.

Enzyme action happens in three steps. The substrate binds to the active site and forms an **enzyme-substrate complex**. Inside the complex, the substrate turns into products. The products are then released, and the enzyme returns to its original shape, ready for the next substrate.

<BlockMath math="\text{E} + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{ES} \rightarrow \text{E} + \text{P}" />

Newer research shows the active site isn't as rigid as a lock. In the **induced fit model**, the active site changes shape slightly when the substrate binds, so it holds the substrate more tightly. Both models agree on one point: if the shape of the active site is damaged, the substrate can no longer bind.

## Temperature and pH Change the Rate

As temperature rises, enzyme and substrate molecules move faster and collide more often, so the rate increases. The rate is highest at the **optimum temperature**, about <InlineMath math="37^\circ\text{C}" /> for most human enzymes. Above it, the bonds that hold the active site in shape start to break. The enzyme is **denatured**: the shape of the active site changes for good and the substrate can't bind any more.

## Substrate Concentration and Enzyme Concentration

As substrate concentration rises, more active sites are filled and the rate increases. Eventually every active site is busy all the time. At that point the rate reaches its **maximum rate**, and adding more substrate no longer raises it. Now **enzyme concentration** becomes the limiting factor: adding enzyme gives more active sites, so the maximum rate goes up.

## Enzymes in Industry and Everyday Life

Enzymes are now made on a large scale, mostly from microorganisms. Each use depends on the substrate the enzyme breaks down.

| Field | Enzyme | Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Detergents | Protease, lipase, amylase | Remove protein, fat and starch stains from fabric |
| Cheese making | Rennin (chymosin) | Clots milk protein |
| Milk processing | Lactase | Makes lactose-free milk |
| Fruit juice | Pectinase | Breaks down cell wall pectin so juice is more plentiful and clearer |
| Bread | Amylase | Breaks down flour starch and controls bread texture |
| Textiles | Amylase | Removes starch from yarn without damaging the fabric |

Biological detergents work best in warm water, because **water that's too hot denatures their enzymes**. Lactose-free milk lets people who don't make enough lactase drink milk without a stomach ache.

## Common Mistakes about Enzymes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Enzymes die at high temperature. | Enzymes aren't alive. They are denatured because the shape of the active site changes. | Use the word denatured and mention the active site. |
| Low temperature denatures enzymes. | Low temperature only makes enzymes inactive. | Warm them back to the optimum and the rate recovers. |
| Enzymes get used up in the reaction. | Enzymes are unchanged and can be reused. | The final volume of product depends on the substrate, not the enzyme. |
| Every enzyme has an optimum pH of <InlineMath math="7" />. | The optimum pH matches where the enzyme works. | Pepsin in the stomach, pH <InlineMath math="2" />. |
| Enzymes increase the amount of product. | Enzymes only speed up the reaction. | Compare where the two gas curves level off. |

The first mistake turns up most often in exam answers. Write **"the enzyme is denatured, its active site changes shape"**, not "the enzyme dies".

## Enzyme Practice

These questions start with naming and end with analysing the rate model. **Give a reason based on the active site** in every answer.

1. Name the enzymes that hydrolyse sucrose, lipids and proteins. State whether these reactions are anabolism or catabolism.

2. One tube of amylase is kept at <InlineMath math="5^\circ\text{C}" /> and another is boiled at <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" />. Both are then placed in a <InlineMath math="37^\circ\text{C}" /> water bath with starch solution. Predict what happens and explain.

3. Use the pH model in these notes. Calculate the relative activity of trypsin at pH <InlineMath math="7" />, and explain why pepsin doesn't digest protein in the small intestine.

4. Use the model <InlineMath math="r = \dfrac{20ks}{2 + s}" />. Calculate <InlineMath math="r" /> for <InlineMath math="k = 1,\ s = 2" />, for <InlineMath math="k = 2,\ s = 2" /> and for <InlineMath math="k = 1,\ s = 18" />. Explain why raising the substrate from <InlineMath math="s = 2" /> to <InlineMath math="s = 18" /> doesn't make the rate nine times bigger.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Add the ending -ase to the substrate name: **sucrase**, **lipase** and **protease**. All three break large molecules into small ones, so they are all **catabolism**.

**Solution 2**: The cold tube digests the starch, because amylase is only inactive at <InlineMath math="5^\circ\text{C}" /> and its active site is still intact. The boiled tube doesn't digest the starch, because **the amylase is denatured** and its active site has changed shape for good. The iodine test on the second tube stays blue-black.

**Solution 3**: Substitute pH <InlineMath math="7" /> into the trypsin model:

<BlockMath math="100e^{-((7 - 8)/1.2)^2} = 100e^{-0.694} \approx 50\%" />

For pepsin at pH <InlineMath math="8" />, the exponent is <InlineMath math="-((8 - 2)/1.2)^2 = -25" />, so its activity is close to zero. In the alkaline small intestine **the charges on pepsin's active site change**, so the substrate doesn't bind.

**Solution 4**: Substitute each pair of values:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="k = 1,\ s = 2: \quad r = \frac{20(1)(2)}{2 + 2} = 10" />
<BlockMath math="k = 2,\ s = 2: \quad r = \frac{20(2)(2)}{2 + 2} = 20" />
<BlockMath math="k = 1,\ s = 18: \quad r = \frac{20(1)(18)}{2 + 18} = 18" />
</MathContainer>

Doubling the enzyme doubles the rate. Raising the substrate ninefold only lifts the rate from <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="18" />, because almost **all the active sites are already saturated**. The rate can't go above <InlineMath math="20k = 20" />.
