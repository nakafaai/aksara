## Building Up and Breaking Down

Your cells never take a break. They join small molecules into big ones and break big ones apart. All the chemical reactions in a cell are called **metabolism**. Joining molecules is **anabolism**, and it needs energy. Breaking molecules apart is **catabolism**, and it releases energy.

Almost every step is sped up by an **enzyme**, a biological catalyst that living cells make. In the models here, you'll match sugars to enzymes, follow an enzyme out of a cell, heat it, fill its active sites and run milk through it.

A growing muscle cell joins amino acids into proteins: that's anabolism. The same cell breaks down glucose in cellular respiration: that's catabolism. Watch which side the energy sits on:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{anabolism: amino acids} + \text{energy} \rightarrow \text{protein}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{catabolism: glucose} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} + \text{energy}" />
</MathContainer>

**ATP carries the energy from catabolism to anabolism**.

## Lower Hills for Reactions

Why does a cell need enzymes? Picture pushing a ball over a small hill so it can roll down the other side. A substrate also needs enough energy to get over a top before it turns into products. That minimum energy is the **activation energy**. At body temperature, bumping molecules rarely get that much, so without enzymes most reactions would crawl. An enzyme offers **a path with a lower top**.

In this graph, the flat line is the energy a substrate gets from collisions. Move slider <InlineMath math="k" /> and compare the energy each path still lacks.

<MathContainer>

Most enzymes are proteins, but **not every enzyme is a protein**. Inside a ribosome, the enzyme that joins amino acids during protein synthesis is part of the ribosomal RNA.

## Shape Match at the Active Site

An enzyme does its work in one small pocket, the **active site**. Its shape matches one kind of substrate only, like one key for one padlock. That's the **lock and key hypothesis**. The substrate slips in and forms an enzyme-substrate complex, a bond breaks, the products leave, and the enzyme takes the next substrate. Lactase, for example, breaks down lactose, the sugar in milk:

<BlockMath math="\text{lactose} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{lactase}} \text{glucose} + \text{galactose}" />

Later research showed the active site isn't as stiff as a metal padlock. As the substrate enters, the active site shifts a little and grips the substrate tightly. That's the **induced fit model**.

The model shows the general properties of enzymes. An enzyme **acts on one specific substrate** and comes out unchanged, so it's used again and again and a cell needs only a little. Many enzyme reactions are reversible. Some enzymes need a helper: a cofactor is an inorganic ion such as magnesium, and a coenzyme is an organic molecule, most often made from a vitamin.

Most enzyme names come from **adding -ase to the substrate's name**: maltose gives maltase and lipid gives lipase. The IUBMB system also gives every enzyme a number and a systematic name, such as *lactose galactohydrolase* for lactase, which names the substrate and the type of reaction.

Try this before reading on. An enzyme breaks down lactose. What's its name, and can it break down sucrose too?

It's **lactase**. Lactase recognises the galactose part of lactose, but sucrose is made of glucose and fructose. So **sucrose doesn't fit lactase's active site** and no complex forms. Check it in the model with lactase and sucrose.

## Enzymes That Stay or Leave

Ribosomes build protein enzymes, but enzymes work in different places. An **intracellular enzyme** works inside the cell that made it, like the enzymes of glycolysis. Free ribosomes build them, and they stay in the cytoplasm to work.

An **extracellular enzyme** is secreted to work outside the cell, like the enzymes in pancreatic juice from the acinar cells of the pancreas. It passes through several [organelles of the cell](/en/subjects/biology/cell-and-organisation/cell-structure-and-specialised-cells). Ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum build it into the reticulum. A transport vesicle carries it to the Golgi apparatus, which modifies it and packs it into a secretory vesicle. The secretory vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases the enzyme by **exocytosis**.

## Conditions That Set the Rate

The rate of reaction is how fast the substrate gets used up or the products form. Temperature and pH change **the shape of the active site**. Substrate concentration and enzyme concentration change how many active sites are working.

### Best Temperature and pH

Warm a solution and its molecules move faster, because **their kinetic energy goes up**. More effective collisions happen, meaning substrates hit the active site the right way, so the rate rises. Most enzymes in your body work fastest near <InlineMath math="37^\circ\text{C}" />, their optimum temperature.

Go hotter, and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape break. The active site changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is **denatured**. Cooling it back down doesn't bring the active site back. Cold is different: the enzyme just slows down, and it works again once it's warmed up.

The wrong pH changes the charges on the active site, so its shape changes and the enzyme can be denatured. Each enzyme has **an optimum pH that matches where it works**. Pepsin in the acidic stomach is most active near pH <InlineMath math="2" />, while salivary amylase works in your nearly neutral mouth, as you see in [digestion of food](/en/subjects/biology/digestive-system/digestion-and-absorption).

### Every Active Site Busy

Add substrate, and more substrate bumps into active sites, so the rate goes up. Once **every active site is busy**, extra substrate has to wait its turn, so the rate stops rising at its maximum. Now enzyme concentration is the limiting factor. Add enzyme, and the rate climbs again, as long as there's enough substrate.

## Enzymes in Food and Factories

Factories make enzymes in bulk, mostly from microorganisms, and pick each enzyme by **the substrate that has to be broken down**:

| Field | Enzyme | What the enzyme does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Biological detergent | Protease, lipase, amylase | Removes protein, fat and starch stains from fabric |
| Fish processing | Protease | Removes fish skin without damaging the flesh |
| Leather making | Protease | Removes hair from animal hides |
| Medicine | Protease | Cleans dead skin from burns |
| Lactose-free milk | Lactase | Breaks lactose into glucose and galactose |
| Fruit juice | Pectinase | Breaks down pectin in cell walls so more juice comes out |

Enzymes are expensive, so factories reuse them with enzyme immobilisation. The enzyme is trapped in an insoluble solid, such as alginate beads, and is then called an **immobilised enzyme**. It stays put, separates easily from the product and is often more stable to changes in temperature and pH.
