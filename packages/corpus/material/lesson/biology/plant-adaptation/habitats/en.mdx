## Four Groups of Plants by Habitat

Plants are classified by **how much water and salt their habitat holds**. **Mesophytes** live in soil with enough water, **hydrophytes** live in or on water, **halophytes** live in salty mud such as a mangrove swamp, and **xerophytes** live in dry places such as deserts. Every group has adaptations for the same four needs: **taking in water and mineral salts, gas exchange, support and photosynthesis**.

| Group | Habitat | Main problem | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mesophyte | Moist soil, moderate water | Balancing transpiration with water uptake | Hibiscus, rambutan tree |
| Hydrophyte | Ponds, lakes, rivers | Too much water, little oxygen and light | Water lily, water hyacinth, Hydrilla |
| Halophyte | Mangrove swamps, muddy shores | Salt water, mud without oxygen, soft ground | Rhizophora, Avicennia |
| Xerophyte | Deserts, dry land | Very little water, heat | Cactus, aloe vera |

In the models here, you measure the water a leafy shoot takes up, count oxygen bubbles from Hydrilla, compare the surface area to volume ratio of a cactus stem, soak tissue strips in strong solutions, and rotate a model of mangrove roots.

## Mesophytes Control Water Loss through Stomata

Mesophytes are the reference point because they have enough water but not too much. Their leaves are broad and thin to absorb light. Their cuticle is thin to moderate. Most **stomata sit in the lower epidermis**, which is shaded from direct sunlight, so less water is lost while the stomata are open for gas exchange. Their roots branch widely to absorb water and mineral salts from the soil.

A potometer measures how fast a shoot takes up water, which is close to its rate of transpiration. Petroleum jelly seals the stomata on any surface it covers. Pick each shoot and compare how far the air bubble moves in <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes.

Use the four rates in the model, in <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^3" /> an hour. ## Hydrophytes Live with Too Much Water

A hydrophyte never runs short of water. Its problem is that **oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse very slowly through water**, and light gets dimmer the deeper the water is. Hydrophytes divide into floating plants, such as the water lily and the water hyacinth, and submerged plants, such as Hydrilla.

**Floating plants** have broad leaves that float on the water surface. **Their stomata are in the upper epidermis**, which touches the air, because the lower surface sits in the water. A thick waxy cuticle on the upper surface sheds rainwater so the stomata don't get blocked. Their leaf stalks and stems hold **aerenchyma**, a tissue with large air spaces that stores air for respiration and keeps the plant afloat. The swollen leaf stalks of the water hyacinth are aerenchyma that float the whole plant.

**Submerged plants** such as Hydrilla have no stomata and almost no cuticle. Their leaves are small and thin, so water, mineral salts and dissolved carbon dioxide diffuse straight into the cells across the whole leaf surface. Their roots are small because they mostly anchor the plant to the bottom rather than absorb water. Their xylem is poorly developed, and their stems are soft and flexible because **the water supports the plant**. Aerenchyma stores oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration so the plant can use them again.

## Xerophytes Store and Save Water

A xerophyte faces the opposite problem to a hydrophyte: there's very little water, and heat speeds up transpiration. Its adaptations cut water loss, store water and reach for water as far as possible.

- **Taking in water**: a long taproot reaches groundwater, while widespread fibrous roots near the surface soak up rainwater quickly before it evaporates.
- **Gas exchange**: a cactus's leaves have become spines, so the surface for transpiration is tiny. It has few stomata, and they're **sunken in pits**, so water vapour collects above the pore and moving air doesn't carry it away. Cactus stomata **open at night**, when it's cool, and store carbon dioxide to use in the day while the stomata stay closed.
- **Support**: the thick, fleshy stem stores water in large parenchyma cells, and the pressure of that water keeps the stem firm.
- **Photosynthesis**: the cactus stem is green and carries out photosynthesis, because the leaves have become spines. A thick waxy cuticle cuts water loss through the stem surface.

A cactus stem shaped like a fat cylinder has a small surface area compared with its volume. For a cylinder of radius <InlineMath math="r" /> and height <InlineMath math="h" />, the surface area to volume ratio is:

<BlockMath math="\frac{A}{V} = \frac{2\pi r h + 2\pi r^2}{\pi r^2 h} = \frac{2}{r} + \frac{2}{h}" />

## Mangrove Halophytes Cope with Salt and Mud

Mangrove trees live on muddy shores that seawater floods at every high tide. The trees face three problems at once: **seawater is more concentrated than the cell sap of an ordinary plant**, waterlogged mud holds almost no oxygen, and the ground is soft and pounded by waves.

Prop roots and pneumatophores carry many **lenticels**, small pores for gas exchange. At low tide, oxygen diffuses in through the lenticels and travels through aerenchyma to the roots buried in the oxygen-free mud. The widespread prop roots also **grip the soft mud** and keep the tree from toppling when waves hit it. Mangrove leaves are thick, fleshy and heavily cuticled, so they store water and cut transpiration, and photosynthesis carries on even though fresh water is hard to get.

Mangrove seeds also germinate while the fruit is still on the parent tree. This adaptation is called **vivipary**. The heavy, pointed seedling drops and spears into the mud, or floats away until it lodges in mud somewhere else.

## Compare the Adaptations for the Four Needs

The table below sorts the adaptations by the four needs of a plant. Read one row across the three habitats: **the same problem gets different solutions**, because too much water, salt water and very little water each call for different structures.

| Need | Hydrophyte | Halophyte | Xerophyte |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Water and mineral salts | Absorbed straight through the surface of submerged leaves | Root cell sap more concentrated than seawater, salt filtered or excreted | Deep taproot and wide fibrous roots, water stored in the stem |
| Gas exchange | Stomata in the upper epidermis of floating leaves, aerenchyma stores air | Pneumatophores and prop roots with lenticels | Few stomata, sunken and open at night |
| Support | Water and aerenchyma keep the plant afloat | Prop roots grip the soft mud | A thick stem kept firm by stored water |
| Photosynthesis | Broad floating leaves, chloroplasts in the epidermis of submerged leaves | Thick leaves with a thick cuticle | A green stem replaces the leaves that became spines |

## Common Mistakes about Plant Adaptations

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A floating leaf has its stomata on the lower surface. | Its lower surface sits in the water, so gas can't be exchanged with the air there. | Ask which surface touches the air. |
| A cactus can't photosynthesise because it has no leaves. | The green cactus stem contains chloroplasts and carries out photosynthesis. | Look for the green parts of the plant. |
| Pneumatophores absorb water. | Pneumatophores take in oxygen through lenticels for roots in oxygen-free mud. | Link the structure to the habitat's problem: the mud lacks oxygen. |
| Mangrove roots take in seawater because there's plenty of it. | Water only enters if the root cell sap is more concentrated than seawater. | Compare the concentration inside and outside the cell. |

One rule settles the first mistake: **stomata only work on a surface that touches the air**.

## Practice on Plant Adaptations

These questions start with classifying and end with a calculation. **Link each structure to its habitat's problem** in your answers.

1. A plant has broad floating leaves, stomata in the upper epidermis and air-filled leaf stalks. Classify the plant and explain what its leaf stalks do.

2. Explain why Hydrilla doesn't need stomata or well-developed xylem.

3. In the light model, murky water has <InlineMath math="k = 0.8" />. Calculate the percentage of surface light that reaches a leaf at a depth of <InlineMath math="2" /> metres.

4. A <InlineMath math="4.00\text{ g}" /> potato strip soaks in a <InlineMath math="0.8\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution and its mass changes by <InlineMath math="-19\%" />. Calculate its final mass, then explain why mangrove roots don't suffer the same in seawater.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The plant is a **floating hydrophyte**, such as a water lily or a water hyacinth. Stomata in the upper epidermis touch the air. The air-filled leaf stalks are aerenchyma, which gives buoyancy so the leaves stay at the surface to catch light, and stores air for respiration.

**Solution 2**: Hydrilla is fully submerged, so water, mineral salts and dissolved carbon dioxide **diffuse straight through its thin leaf surface, which has no cuticle**. There's no air around the leaves, so stomata would be useless. Xylem matters less because water doesn't have to be carried far, and the water itself supports the soft stem.

**Solution 3**: Substitute <InlineMath math="k = 0.8" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 2" />:

<BlockMath math="100e^{-0.8 \times 2} = 100e^{-1.6} = 20.2\%" />

Check with the model: set <InlineMath math="k = 0.8" /> and <InlineMath math="d = 2" />, and the light reads about <InlineMath math="20\%" />. The murky water cuts the light almost in half compared with <InlineMath math="37\%" /> in clearer water.

**Solution 4**: The final mass is:

<BlockMath math="4.00 \times (1 - 0.19) = 4.00 \times 0.81 = 3.24\text{ g}" />

Check: the loss of <InlineMath math="0.76\text{ g}" /> is <InlineMath math="19\%" /> of <InlineMath math="4.00\text{ g}" />. The solution is hypertonic to the potato's cell sap, so water leaves by osmosis. Mangrove roots **keep their cell sap more concentrated than seawater**, so for mangrove roots seawater is a hypotonic solution and water still diffuses in.
