## Cells as Living Units

Picture a brick house where every tiny room runs on its own, yet all the rooms together keep the whole home working. Living things are built the same way from microscopic living units called **cells**.

Every cell is wrapped in a thin **plasma membrane**, filled with jelly-like **cytoplasm**, and guided by genetic material. In animal and plant cells, that genetic material sits safely inside a **nucleus**. Tiny structures called organelles share out the daily work inside the cytoplasm.

> Every living organism is built from cells, and every cell has a shape and set of organelles matched to its job.

Because cells are far too tiny for the naked eye, biologists measure them in micrometres, where <InlineMath math="1\ \mu\text{m} = 10^{-3}\text{ mm}" />. A human cheek cell is about <InlineMath math="60\ \mu\text{m}" /> across, so you need a microscope to see it clearly.

## Animal and Plant Organelles

Think of a cell as a small kitchen. A head chef gives orders, ovens produce power, prep tables assemble dishes, and walls keep everything upright. Inside animal and plant cells, each **organelle** plays one of those roles.

Both animal and plant cells share a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus, **mitochondria**, **ribosomes**, **endoplasmic reticulum**, a **Golgi apparatus**, and lysosomes. Notice that the plant cell still holds **mitochondria** alongside its chloroplasts. Chloroplasts capture sunlight to make food, while mitochondria break food down to release usable energy, so plants need both. Also keep the outer rigid **cell wall** distinct from the inner, partially permeable **plasma membrane** that animal cells carry too.

| Organelle | Function | Found in |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nucleus | Controls all cell activities and stores genetic material | Animal and plant |
| Mitochondrion | Site of cellular respiration that releases energy | Animal and plant |
| Ribosome | Site of protein synthesis | Animal and plant |
| Golgi apparatus | Processes, packages, and transports proteins | Animal and plant |
| Chloroplast | Site of photosynthesis | Plant only |
| Cell wall | Gives support and a fixed shape | Plant only |
| Centriole | Forms spindle fibres during cell division | Animal only |

A cell's daily workload sets which organelles appear in high numbers. Active muscle cells and sperm cells pack in **many mitochondria** for extra energy. Enzyme-secreting pancreatic cells carry dense ribosomes and a large Golgi apparatus to build and ship proteins.

## Viewing Cells by Microscope

A light microscope magnifies tiny cells by **stacking two glass lenses**. Once you know the total magnification, you can measure a drawing or photo and work back to a cell's real size, then compare animal cells against much smaller microbes.

### Lenses and Real Size

Looking through one magnifying glass makes letters look larger, and stacking a second lens multiplies that boost again. In a light microscope, **total magnification** equals the eyepiece magnification multiplied by the objective magnification:

<BlockMath math="\text{Magnification} = \text{eyepiece} \times \text{objective}" />

As you jump from the <InlineMath math="\times 4" /> lens to the <InlineMath math="\times 40" /> lens, total magnification rises from <InlineMath math="\times 40" /> to <InlineMath math="\times 400" />. You see finer detail inside one cell, yet the **field of view shrinks**, so fewer whole cells fit across the circle.

To find a cell's real length from a microscope image, always **divide the image size by the magnification** and never multiply, because the real cell must be smaller than its magnified picture. Suppose an onion epidermal cell is drawn <InlineMath math="45\text{ mm}" /> long at <InlineMath math="\times 300" /> magnification:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Real size} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}} = \frac{45}{300} = 0.15\text{ mm}" />
<BlockMath math="0.15\text{ mm} \times 1{,}000 = 150\ \mu\text{m}" />
</MathContainer>

Verify it in reverse by checking <InlineMath math="0.15 \times 300 = 45\text{ mm}" />. Try the same habit on a <InlineMath math="24\text{ mm}" /> cheek cell image viewed at <InlineMath math="\times 400" />. Dividing gives <InlineMath math="0.060\text{ mm}" />, and multiplying by <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> converts it to <InlineMath math="60\ \mu\text{m}" />.

## Life in a Single Cell

Imagine a tiny one-room cabin that works as kitchen, workshop, and nursery all at once. A **unicellular organism** lives like that. Although it consists of just one cell, that single cell moves, feeds, respires, responds to stimuli, and reproduces on its own.

*Amoeba* crawls and traps food using flowing false feet, while many simple single-celled microbes reproduce by splitting cleanly down the middle into **two identical daughter cells**. Because every new cell can split again, the population doubles each generation:

<BlockMath math="N = 2^n" />

## Specialised Cell Levels

In a **multicellular organism**, cells do not work as identical loners. Just as bricks build a wall, walls form a room, and rooms make a house, living bodies stack into clear levels: **cell, tissue, organ, system, and organism**. Keep that order tight: organs are built from tissues, never the reverse.

Every specialised cell also shapes itself to match its task. A **red blood cell** flattens into a biconcave disc with no nucleus so it packs more oxygen. A **root hair cell** stretches long and thin in dark soil, carrying no chloroplasts, so more membrane touches soil water and mineral ions.

Why does being small or thin help a cell absorb materials faster? Compare a small sugar cube with a giant block: the small cube has far more outer skin for each bit of inside volume. For a cube-shaped cell of side <InlineMath math="L" />, that **surface area to volume ratio** simplifies cleanly:

<BlockMath math="\frac{\text{SA}}{\text{V}} = \frac{6L^2}{L^3} = \frac{6}{L}" />
