## Meristems and Tissue Types

Picture a tree as a living tower of building blocks expanding on its own. At shoot and root tips sit tiny builder factories called **meristematic tissue** that divide nonstop by mitosis. Cells that finish dividing mature into **permanent tissue** specialised to protect, support, store food, or transport substances.

> Every bit of plant growth starts with meristematic cells dividing, and permanent tissues turn those fresh cells into tough stems, water pipes, and green leaves.

Meristematic cells stay small with thin walls, dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus, and no large vacuole so they split quickly. Across the outer surface, **epidermal tissue** wraps leaves and young stems under a **waxy cuticle** that slows water loss, while guard cells control stomata and root hairs absorb soil water.

### Ground Tissue Cells

Ground tissue fills most of the plant body with three cell types distinguished by **cell wall thickness**.

| Ground tissue | Cell wall | Main function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Parenchyma | Thin cellulose wall, living cell with large vacuole | Stores food, photosynthesises, supports when turgid |
| Collenchyma | Cellulose thickened unevenly at cell corners | Supports young stems and petioles while flexible |
| Sclerenchyma | Thick and lignified, dead when mature | Gives rigid strength to mature plant parts |

Sclerenchyma and xylem die at maturity because thick lignin turns them into rigid struts, whereas parenchyma, collenchyma, and phloem stay alive. When parenchyma absorbs water by osmosis, the vacuole presses outward like a water balloon inside a box, holding soft herbaceous stems **turgid** and upright.

A <InlineMath math="5.00\text{ g}" /> potato strip gaining <InlineMath math="16\%" /> in hypotonic distilled water reaches:

<BlockMath math="\text{Final mass} = 5.00 \times \left(1 + \frac{16}{100}\right) = 5.80\text{ g}" />

In hypertonic <InlineMath math="0.6\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sucrose, water exits so mass drops <InlineMath math="16\%" /> to <InlineMath math="4.20\text{ g}" /> and the strip turns limp.

### Xylem and Phloem

Vascular tissue pairs **xylem** and **phloem** from roots to leaves. Avoid a common slip: mature xylem vessels are dead lignified tubes, yet phloem sieve tubes remain **alive** alongside companion cells.

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What it carries | Water and minerals upwards | Sucrose from leaves to the whole plant |
| Main cells | Xylem vessels and tracheids | Sieve tubes and companion cells |
| Mature cells | Dead, thick lignified walls | Living, dependent on companion cells |

Blocking xylem wilts leaves even in wet soil. Stripping a ring of bark removes phloem and starves the roots because **sucrose no longer reaches the roots**.

## Primary Growth Zones

Only meristematic cells divide actively, not every plant cell. **Apical meristems** at root and shoot tips lengthen the plant through **primary growth**, whereas **lateral meristems** widen woody stems through **secondary growth**.

Behind the root cap, each fresh cell moves through three zones in order:

1. **Zone of cell division**: meristem cells divide rapidly by mitosis.
2. **Zone of cell elongation**: vacuoles absorb water and stretch cells several times longer, pushing the tip deeper.
3. **Zone of cell differentiation**: cells mature into root hairs, xylem vessels, and sieve tubes.

## Secondary Stem Thickening

In woody dicotyledons, a ring of **vascular cambium** lays down **secondary xylem inwards** and secondary phloem outwards. Far more xylem forms, so timber is secondary xylem, while **cork cambium** builds bark pierced by gas-exchanging lenticels.

Wet seasons produce wide thin-walled xylem vessels and dry seasons produce narrow thick-walled vessels, forming one **annual ring** each year. Slide trunk age below to track radius and girth.

## Sigmoid Growth Curves

**Dry mass** is the most accurate growth measure because cell water fluctuates with the weather, even though measuring it requires drying the plant.

> A dry-mass curve reveals a plant's life cycle at a glance: one S-curve for an annual, two S-curves for a biennial, and yearly steps for a perennial.

## Tissue Growth Calculations

Check your mastery across cambium growth, parenchyma osmosis, and isotonic interpolation.

1. Name the trunk-thickening meristem and the tissues formed inwards and outwards. **Answer**: **vascular cambium** forms secondary xylem inwards and secondary phloem outwards, while cork cambium forms bark.

2. A <InlineMath math="4.00\text{ g}" /> potato strip loses <InlineMath math="12\%" /> in <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sucrose. Find its final mass and cell state. **Answer**: <InlineMath math="88\%" /> remains, and parenchyma cells turn **flaccid** as water leaves by osmosis:

<BlockMath math="4.00 \times \frac{88}{100} = 3.52\text{ g}" />

3. A strip gains <InlineMath math="4\%" /> in <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sucrose and loses <InlineMath math="2\%" /> in <InlineMath math="0.3\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Estimate the isotonic concentration. **Answer**: linear interpolation places zero net osmosis at:

<BlockMath math="c = 0.2 + 0.1 \times \frac{4}{4 + 2} = 0.267\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />
