## Meristems Divide and Permanent Tissues Do the Work

A plant has two kinds of tissue. **Meristematic tissue** is made of cells that keep dividing by mitosis, so it's where growth comes from. **Permanent tissue** is made of cells that have stopped dividing and have become specialised to protect, support, store food or transport substances. Meristems at the tips of shoots and roots make the plant longer through **primary growth**, while lateral meristems make the stem thicker through **secondary growth**. The dry mass of most plants increases along an S-shaped **sigmoid curve**.

Meristematic cells are small and thin-walled, with dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus and no large vacuole. That suits a cell that divides all the time. In the models here, you open up a cell from permanent tissue, soak potato strips, look at a root tip under a microscope, and move time forward on a tree trunk and on growth curves.

## Epidermal and Ground Tissue

Permanent tissue comes in three groups: epidermal, ground and vascular tissue. **Epidermal tissue** is a single layer of cells covering the leaves, young stems and roots. On leaves and stems, the epidermal cells secrete a **waxy cuticle** that cuts down water loss. Some epidermal cells are specialised: guard cells control the size of the stomatal pore, and root hair cells absorb water from the soil. Ground tissue fills most of the plant body.

### Three Kinds of Cell in Ground Tissue

Ground tissue is made of three kinds of cell. The main difference between them is **how thick the cell wall is**, and that thickness decides each tissue's job.

| Ground tissue | Cell wall | Main function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Parenchyma | Thin and made of cellulose, living cell with a large vacuole | Stores food, carries out photosynthesis, gives support when turgid |
| Collenchyma | Cellulose thickened unevenly, mainly at the cell corners | Supports young stems and leaf stalks while staying flexible |
| Sclerenchyma | Thick and lignified, cell dead when mature | Gives strength to parts that have stopped growing longer |

A parenchyma cell is the typical plant cell you met when you studied cells: a thin cellulose wall, a large vacuole and, in a leaf, plenty of chloroplasts. Open the cell in this model and find the vacuole that fills most of the space inside.

### Turgid Parenchyma Cells Hold Up a Stem

That large vacuole is what lets parenchyma support a plant. When the cell takes in water by osmosis, the vacuole swells and presses the cytoplasm against the cell wall, so the cell becomes **turgid**. A soft herbaceous stem stands upright because its parenchyma cells are turgid, and it wilts when those cells lose water. ## Vascular Tissue Carries Water and Food

Vascular tissue is made of **xylem** and **phloem**, which run together in vascular bundles from the roots to the leaves. Xylem carries water and mineral salts upwards. Phloem carries the sucrose the leaves make to the parts that store or use it, such as roots, fruits and growing shoots.

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What it carries | Water and mineral salts, from roots to leaves | Sucrose and other organic substances, from leaves to the whole plant |
| Main cells | Xylem vessels and tracheids | Sieve tubes and companion cells |
| Mature cells | Dead, with lignified walls that also support the plant | Living, sieve tubes rely on companion cells |

Every permanent tissue matters to the plant. If the xylem gets blocked, the leaves wilt even in damp soil, because water can't reach them. If a ring of bark holding the phloem is stripped off, the roots eventually die because **sucrose no longer reaches the roots**.

## Meristems and the Zones of Growth

Meristematic tissue is found only in certain places. **Apical meristems** sit at the tips of shoots and roots, and their cell division makes the plant longer. **Lateral meristems** are the vascular cambium and the cork cambium, which form cylinders along the stem and root and make both thicker. At a root tip, each new cell passes through three zones before it becomes a cell of permanent tissue.

### Three Zones at a Root Tip

Starting from the tip, the root is protected by a **root cap** that gets pushed through the soil. Behind it lie three zones:

1. **Zone of cell division**: meristematic cells divide by mitosis and make new cells.
2. **Zone of cell elongation**: the cells take in water, vacuoles form and grow, and each cell lengthens several times over. This lengthening pushes the root tip deeper.
3. **Zone of cell differentiation**: the cells become specialised, for example into root hair cells, xylem vessels and phloem sieve tubes.

A shoot tip has the same zones, but young leaves protect its meristem instead of a root cap. Primary growth happens in every plant, because every plant has apical meristems.

## Secondary Growth Thickens a Stem

In a woody dicotyledon, the **vascular cambium** between the xylem and the phloem forms a ring. Its cells divide and make **secondary xylem towards the inside** and secondary phloem towards the outside. Far more xylem forms than phloem, so wood is secondary xylem. The widening stem stretches the epidermis, so the **cork cambium** makes cork that becomes the bark. Lenticels in the bark let gases in and out.

In a wet season, the cambium makes large, thin-walled xylem vessels. In a dry or cold season, the vessels are small and thick-walled. Each pair of layers forms one **annual ring**, so the number of rings tells you the tree's age. Move the tree's age in this model and watch the radius and girth of the trunk grow.

## Plant Growth Curves

Growth is measured as an increase in dry mass, height or volume. **Dry mass** is the most accurate measure because the water in cells changes with the weather, but measuring it means drying the plant. The shape of the curve depends on the plant's life cycle: annual, biennial or perennial.

### Annual Plants and the Sigmoid Curve

An **annual plant** such as rice or maize completes its life cycle in one season. Its curve is sigmoid: a slow phase at the start, a fast phase once many leaves are photosynthesising, then a flat phase as the plant matures. At the very start, dry mass falls a little because the seedling uses the food stored in the seed for respiration before its leaves can photosynthesise. At the end of the life cycle, dry mass falls again as leaves drop and fruits are dispersed.

### Biennial and Perennial Plants

A **biennial plant** such as the carrot completes its life cycle over two seasons. In the first season, it builds leaves and stores food in its root. In the second season, it uses that stored food to flower and make seeds, and then it dies. Its curve is made of two sigmoid curves. A **perennial plant** such as the rubber tree lives for many years, and its height increases during each year's growing season, which gives a stepped curve.

## Common Mistakes about Tissues and Growth

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Every plant cell can divide. | Only meristematic cells actively divide. Cells of permanent tissue are already specialised. | Look for the zone of division at root and shoot tips. |
| Xylem and phloem are both made of dead cells. | Xylem vessels are dead, but phloem sieve tubes are alive alongside their companion cells. | Sieve tubes still have cytoplasm and rely on living companion cells. |
| Secondary growth makes the stem longer. | Secondary growth makes the stem thicker. Apical meristems are what make it longer. | Ask whether width or length is increasing. |
| A seedling's dry mass rises from day one. | The seedling uses stored food for respiration before its leaves photosynthesise. | The annual curve dips a little at the start. |

The third mistake turns up most often in structured questions. **Apical meristems lengthen, lateral meristems thicken**, so name the meristem first before you explain the growth.

## Practice on Plant Tissues and Growth

These questions move from naming meristems to calculations and reading curves. **Show every step of a calculation** with its unit.

1. Name the meristem that thickens a tree trunk and the tissues it makes towards the inside and towards the outside.

2. A <InlineMath math="4.00\text{ g}" /> potato strip is soaked in a <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sucrose solution and loses <InlineMath math="12\%" /> of its mass. Work out its final mass and describe the state of its parenchyma cells.

3. A potato strip gains <InlineMath math="4\%" /> in a <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution and loses <InlineMath math="2\%" /> in a <InlineMath math="0.3\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution. Estimate the concentration of the solution that is isotonic with the potato cells.

4. The dry-mass curve of an annual plant falls at the start and at the end of its life cycle. Explain the reason for each fall.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The meristem is the **vascular cambium**, a lateral meristem. It makes secondary xylem towards the inside and secondary phloem towards the outside. The cork cambium, for its part, makes the cork that becomes the bark.

**Solution 2**: The mass falls by <InlineMath math="12\%" />, so **the mass left is** <InlineMath math="88\%" />:

<BlockMath math="4.00 \times \frac{88}{100} = 3.52\text{ g}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="4.00 - 3.52 = 0.48\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{0.48}{4.00} \times 100\% = 12\%" />. The solution is hypertonic, so water leaves the cells by osmosis. The parenchyma cells become **flaccid** and the strip goes limp.

**Solution 3**: The change in mass crosses zero between <InlineMath math="0.2" /> and <InlineMath math="0.3\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. Assume the change **varies linearly** across this range:

<BlockMath math="c = 0.2 + 0.1 \times \frac{4}{4 + 2} = 0.267\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />

Check: from <InlineMath math="0.2" /> to <InlineMath math="0.267" />, the change in mass drops by <InlineMath math="\frac{0.067}{0.1} \times 6 = 4\%" />, from <InlineMath math="+4\%" /> to <InlineMath math="0\%" />. In an isotonic solution, water enters and leaves the cells at the same rate.

**Solution 4**: At the start, the germinating seed **uses its stored food for respiration** while its leaves can't yet photosynthesise, so the dry mass falls. At the end of the life cycle, the leaves age and drop, and the fruits and seeds are dispersed, so the plant loses dry mass.
