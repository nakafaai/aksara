## Negative Feedback Control

Step from a cold air-conditioned room into midday tropical sun and your core temperature still hovers near <InlineMath math="37^\circ\text{C}" />. That stability is **homeostasis**, the maintenance of a nearly constant internal environment across tissue fluid despite outside swings.

Homeostasis does not lock any variable motionless. Instead, **negative feedback** lets a value drift slightly, senses the shift through receptors, routes signals through a control centre, and activates effectors that push the value back toward normal.

| Variable | Above normal response | Below normal response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Body temperature | Sweating and arteriole vasodilation | Shivering and arteriole vasoconstriction |
| Blood glucose | Insulin converts glucose to glycogen | Glucagon breaks glycogen into glucose |
| Blood osmotic pressure | High ADH raises water reabsorption | Low ADH yields dilute, plentiful urine |

> Homeostasis works like a smart thermostat that continuously nudges drifting values back toward their set point rather than freezing them rigid.

## Temperature and Glucose

Two fast physiological loops protect cells from immediate thermal or metabolic stress. The **hypothalamus** coordinates skin effectors for heat balance while **pancreatic islets** release opposing hormones directly into the bloodstream to buffer glucose.

### Skin Thermoregulation

Mammalian skin adjusts heat loss by changing both blood flow near the surface and evaporative cooling. In hot surroundings, **vasodilation** widens skin arterioles and sweat glands secrete water that absorbs latent heat as it evaporates. In cold air, **vasoconstriction** narrows those arterioles, sweat stops, and hair erector muscles contract.

### Blood Glucose Circuit

After a carbohydrate meal, blood glucose rises and beta cells in the pancreas secrete **insulin**, signalling the liver and muscles to store excess glucose as **glycogen**. Between meals, falling glucose triggers alpha cells to release **glucagon**, converting stored glycogen back into glucose.

## Nephron Filtration

Each human kidney packs roughly one million microscopic **nephrons** that filter blood plasma and shape urine across three steps:

- **Ultrafiltration**: high pressure inside the glomerulus pushes water, glucose, amino acids, salts, and urea into Bowman's capsule, while red blood cells and plasma proteins stay behind in the blood.
- **Reabsorption**: proximal convoluted tubules reclaim all filtered glucose and amino acids plus most water and essential salts back into surrounding capillaries.
- **Secretion**: distal tubules actively add excess hydrogen ions, drugs, and surplus urea into the filtrate.

At normal plasma glucose (<InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />), tubules effortlessly reclaim every milligram filtered. Pushing plasma glucose past <InlineMath math="3.0\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" /> saturates carrier proteins, causing glucose to appear in urine.

## Water Balance and ADH

Although human kidneys generate about <InlineMath math="180\text{ dm}^3" /> of filtrate daily, healthy adults excrete roughly <InlineMath math="1.5\text{ dm}^3" /> of urine because tubules recover nearly every useful molecule.

**Antidiuretic hormone** (ADH) fine-tunes final urine concentration. Heavy sweating raises blood osmotic pressure, prompting the pituitary gland to secrete more ADH so collecting ducts grow permeable to water.

> High ADH means antidiuretic action: tubules pull more water back into the blood, leaving a small volume of dark, concentrated urine.

## Homeostasis Checks

Check two quantitative nephron relationships below.

**Renal threshold check.** A patient's filtration rate drops to <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3\text{ min}^{-1}" /> while maximum tubular reabsorption stays at <InlineMath math="375\text{ mg min}^{-1}" />. Find the new glucose threshold.

<BlockMath math="\text{Threshold} = \frac{375}{100} = 3.75\text{ mg cm}^{-3}" />

A slower filtration rate delivers less glucose per minute at any plasma level, raising the concentration needed to saturate carriers.

**Urea ratio check.** Plasma urea sits at <InlineMath math="0.03\text{ g}" /> per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> while urine urea reaches <InlineMath math="1.8\text{ g}" /> per <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" />. Find the concentration factor.

<BlockMath math="\text{Factor} = \frac{1.8}{0.03} = 60" />

Urea becomes sixty times more concentrated because tubules reclaim almost all filtered water while leaving urea in the duct.
