## Common Names Can Mislead

Classification sorts living things into groups by shared features, nested like boxes inside boxes. The largest box is the **kingdom** across **six kingdoms**, while the smallest is the **species**, each carrying a two-word scientific name like *Oryza sativa* for rice.

To identify an unfamiliar organism, you follow a dichotomous key. In the sea model below, switch the language and spot the name that never changes.

Why bother with scientific names? Common names change with language and place. In Malaysia, people call a whale ikan paus (whale fish), yet a whale breathes with lungs and nurses its young, so it's a mammal. English calls a boneless, gill-less animal a jellyfish, proving **the word fish in a common name** tells you nothing.

A scientific name is the same in every language, so biologists everywhere know they mean the same animal. Knowing the right group, you can also predict features. Put the dugong in class Mammalia and you know it breathes with lungs and feeds its calf on milk, because one group means **many features in common**.

## Seven Ranks from Kingdom Down

Every organism sits on seven ranks from top to bottom: **kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species**. Any named group at any rank is a **taxon**, such as the family Felidae.

Moving down, each taxon holds **fewer kinds of organisms** sharing more features, until a species holds one kind. Comparing many features at once makes this [a natural classification rather than an artificial one](/en/subjects/biology/biodiversity/classification).

> The lower the taxonomic rank two organisms still share, the more features they have in common and the closer they are related.

A tiger and a sun bear match from kingdom Animalia down to order Carnivora, then split at family into Felidae and Ursidae. So **the lowest rank they share** is order, four of the seven ranks.

## Six Kingdoms in Four Questions

Four questions place almost any organism in its kingdom. Do its cells have a nucleus? Is it one cell or many, and do the cells form tissues? What's the cell wall made of? How does it get food? **Read all four answers together**, because one feature on its own often misleads.

| Kingdom | Nucleus | Cells | Cell wall | How it gets food | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Archaebacteria | None | One | No peptidoglycan | Autotroph or heterotroph | Sulfur bacteria, *Sulfolobus* |
| Eubacteria | None | One | Peptidoglycan | Autotroph or heterotroph | *Thermus aquaticus* |
| Protista | Yes | One, or many without tissues | In most algae, none in protozoa | Photosynthesis, eating or absorbing | Protozoa, algae, slime moulds |
| Fungi | Yes | One in yeast, many in hyphae | Chitin | Absorbs food digested outside its body | Yeast, mushrooms |
| Plantae | Yes | Many, with tissues | Cellulose | Photosynthesis | Ferns without seeds, rice with seeds |
| Animalia | Yes | Many | None | Eats food | Hydra (invertebrate), tiger (vertebrate) |

### Cells without a Nucleus

Archaebacteria and Eubacteria are both **prokaryotes**, tiny cells whose DNA has no nuclear membrane around it. Their genes differ, and so do their walls: the wall of Eubacteria contains **peptidoglycan**, and the wall of Archaebacteria doesn't.

Don't assume every microbe in a hot spring belongs to Archaebacteria. Compare two microbes that both live in hot springs:

| Feature | *Sulfolobus* | *Thermus aquaticus* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it was found | Acidic hot springs | A hot spring in Yellowstone National Park |
| Fastest growth | <InlineMath math="70" /> to <InlineMath math="75^\circ\text{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="70^\circ\text{C}" /> |
| Peptidoglycan in the wall | No | Yes |
| Kingdom | Archaebacteria | Eubacteria |

Both like water at <InlineMath math="70^\circ\text{C}" />, but only *Thermus aquaticus* has peptidoglycan in its wall. So **where an organism lives doesn't decide its kingdom**.

### Cells with a Nucleus

The other four kingdoms are **eukaryotes**, with DNA inside a nucleus. Here, outward looks can easily fool you.

A mushroom grows from soil like a plant, yet its chitin walls and food absorption place it in Fungi. A hydra clings to a pond leaf like a tiny plant, yet its wall-less cells and prey-catching tentacles place it in **Animalia**.

Look at the second question. The cells line up end to end, each just like its neighbour, and none of them form a root, a stem or a leaf. So **the filament has no tissues**, and *Spirogyra* is an alga in Protista. Rice has tissues such as xylem and phloem, so it stays in Plantae.

## Two Words in a Scientific Name

The **binomial nomenclature system** gives every species a two-word name in Latin form. Carolus Linnaeus used it for every plant in *Species Plantarum* in 1753. The first word is the **genus name**. The second word, the specific epithet, only means something next to its genus, so rice is *Oryza sativa*, both words together.

The rules for writing it:

- The genus starts with a capital letter, and the second word is all lowercase, even when it comes from a person, like *brookiana* from Brooke.
- In print, the name is in italics: *Ipomoea aquatica*.
- By hand, **underline each word separately**, one line per word.
- After the first full mention, shorten the genus to its first letter: *I. aquatica*.
- If the species isn't known yet, write the genus followed by sp.: *Ipomoea* sp.

Match the first word first. Water spinach (*Ipomoea aquatica*) and sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) share a genus, making them close relatives even though you eat the leaves of one and the tubers of the other.

Now match the second word. Mango, Indian fleabane and neem all end in *indica* (from India), yet sit in three separate families: Anacardiaceae, Asteraceae and Meliaceae.

> Sharing a first word means the same genus and close kinship, whereas sharing only the second word never proves two species are closely related.

## A Key to Six Small Animals

A **dichotomous key** works like a path that keeps forking. Each numbered step gives you a pair of opposite statements. You pick the one that's true for your animal and follow it to the next step or to a name.

Here's a key to six small animals found around schools:

| Step | Statement a | Statement b |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | Has legs, go to step 2 | No legs, go to step 5 |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | Three pairs of legs, go to step 3 | More than three pairs of legs, go to step 4 |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | Has wings: dragonfly | No wings: worker ant |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | Four pairs of legs: spider | One pair of legs on each body segment: centipede |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | Has a shell: giant African land snail | No shell, body in rings: earthworm |

A good step tests **one feature that every specimen shows**, with two statements that can't both be true. If step 3 read "has wings" against "three pairs of legs", a dragonfly would fit both and the key would jam. "Big" against "small" or "dark" against "light" fails too, because size and colour change with age and light.

Now add a millipede, which has **two pairs of legs on most body segments**. Which step fails, how do you fix it, and how many steps does the new key need?

Steps 1 and 2 send the millipede to step 4, where neither choice fits. Rewrite step 4 as "four pairs of legs: spider" against "many legged segments, go to step 6", and make step 6 "one pair per segment: centipede" against "two pairs on most segments: millipede".
