## Seventy Percent Water

Picture a bacterial cell weighed, dried out, then weighed again. About <InlineMath math="70\%" /> of its mass is gone, because that much of the cell is **water**. Most of the other <InlineMath math="30\%" /> belongs to four groups of compounds: **carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids**. Nucleic acids come in two kinds, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA).

The big molecules in a cell are built like a string of beads. Small units called monomers are joined one by one into a polymer.

Each time two units join, one water molecule is released in **condensation**. To break the link again, one water molecule goes back in during **hydrolysis**. So a cell builds and breaks its big molecules by trading water.

| Group | Elements | Small unit | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Carbohydrates | Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with twice as much hydrogen as oxygen | Monosaccharides, such as glucose | Starch, glycogen, cellulose |
| Proteins | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, sometimes sulfur and phosphorus | Amino acids | Haemoglobin, insulin, enzymes |
| Lipids | Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with very little oxygen | Glycerol and fatty acids | Fats, oils, phospholipids |
| Nucleic acids | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus | Nucleotides | DNA and RNA |

In five models you'll watch each group at work. Water molecules cling to each other, sugar units join and split, a protein chain folds and then falls apart when it's boiled, lipids form droplets or membranes, and DNA coils up until it fits inside a nucleus.

## Sticky Water Molecules

A water molecule, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, is shaped like a tiny letter V. The oxygen atom sits at the point, with a hydrogen atom at the end of each arm and an angle of <InlineMath math="104.5^\circ" /> between the two bonds.

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons harder than hydrogen does. So the oxygen side ends up slightly negative, and each hydrogen ends up slightly positive. A molecule with charged ends like this is a **polar molecule**.

Opposite charges attract, so a hydrogen on one water molecule is drawn to the oxygen on the next one. That pull between two molecules is a [hydrogen bond between molecules](/en/subjects/chemistry/chemical-bond/types-of-chemical-bonds).

In liquid water, hydrogen bonds keep breaking and forming again as the molecules slide past each other. Water's pull on itself, its pull on walls and its slow warming all come from **hydrogen bonds between water molecules**.

Watch out for two different bonds that both involve hydrogen. The bond between oxygen and hydrogen **inside** one molecule is a covalent bond, because the two atoms share electrons. A hydrogen bond is a pull **between** two molecules, and it's only about <InlineMath math="5\%" /> to <InlineMath math="10\%" /> as strong as a covalent bond.

> Boiling water breaks only the hydrogen bonds between molecules and never breaks the covalent bonds inside <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, so steam is still made of water molecules.

Those charged ends also make water a good **solvent**. Water molecules crowd around ions such as sodium and chloride, and polar molecules such as glucose, pulling them apart so they dissolve in the cytoplasm. Oil isn't polar, so water doesn't pull on it and oil doesn't dissolve.

The same hydrogen bonds explain all three properties at once. Attraction between water molecules holds a column together, attraction to walls pulls water upward, and the web of bonds resists fast warming.

### Pulled Up a Tall Stem

Dip the corner of a tissue in water, and the water creeps up the tissue. Two pulls are at work there, and hydrogen bonds cause both of them:

| Force | Pull between | What it does in a xylem vessel |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cohesion** | One water molecule and another | Links the water into one unbroken column |
| **Adhesion** | Water and another substance, such as cellulose | Holds the water to the vessel walls |

In a narrow tube, adhesion pulls water up the walls, and cohesion drags the rest of the water along with it. That's capillary action.

In a tree, water rises through xylem vessels. Is capillary action enough to get it to the top? Pick adhesion in the model and set the tube radius to <InlineMath math="25\ \mu\text{m}" />, a xylem vessel <InlineMath math="0.05\text{ mm}" /> across.

The water climbs only about <InlineMath math="0.59\text{ m}" />. Menara, a yellow meranti tree in Danum Valley, Sabah, is <InlineMath math="100.8\text{ m}" /> tall, about <InlineMath math="170" /> times higher. It's the tallest tropical tree ever recorded.

So the water has to be **pulled from the top**. Leaves lose water by transpiration, the evaporation of water from the leaf surface. Every water molecule that leaves pulls on the one behind it, and cohesion moves the whole column up together.

At night, when transpiration stops, cohesion and adhesion together hold the water in the stem and leaves.

### Slow to Heat Up

Water has to take in a lot of heat before its temperature goes up even a little. The heat needed to raise <InlineMath math="1\text{ g}" /> of a substance by <InlineMath math="1^\circ\text{C}" /> is its **specific heat capacity**. For water it's <InlineMath math="4.184\text{ J g}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" />, the highest of any common liquid, while for ethanol it's only <InlineMath math="2.376\text{ J g}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" />.

Why is water so slow to warm up? Hydrogen bonds hold the water molecules to each other, so water has to soak up a lot of heat before its molecules move faster.

A cell is mostly water, so **the temperature of cells and of your body changes slowly**. A steady temperature protects the cell's proteins, because a big change in temperature can change a protein's shape.

Make a prediction first by picking specific heat capacity in the model. Each beaker holds <InlineMath math="50\text{ g}" /> of liquid, one water and one ethanol. If both take in <InlineMath math="2{,}092\text{ J}" /> of heat, which gets hotter and by how much? Write it down, then move the heat slider.

The heat taken in is <InlineMath math="Q = mc\Delta T" />, so the temperature rise is <InlineMath math="\Delta T = Q/(mc)" />. Put in the mass and specific heat capacity of each liquid:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Delta T_{\text{water}} = \dfrac{2{,}092}{50 \times 4.184} = 10.0^\circ\text{C}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta T_{\text{ethanol}} = \dfrac{2{,}092}{50 \times 2.376} = 17.6^\circ\text{C}" />
</MathContainer>

Ethanol gets hotter, warming <InlineMath math="1.76" /> times as much as water from the same heat. Check your answer by multiplying back:

<BlockMath math="50 \times 4.184 \times 10.0 = 2{,}092\text{ J}" />

That matches the heat you started with. If you found that the water warmed up more, you've probably swapped the two specific heat capacities.

## Building and Splitting Sugars

The smallest sugar is a single unit, like one bead. These units are **monosaccharides**: glucose, fructose and galactose. All three have the same formula, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" />, but their atoms are arranged differently. Two beads joined together make a **disaccharide**, and many glucose beads joined together make a **polysaccharide**.

Two monosaccharides join by condensation. One water molecule is released, and the bond that forms is a **glycosidic bond**. Hydrolysis breaks that bond by putting one water molecule back in. In your body, enzymes speed up hydrolysis. In the lab, boiling a sugar with dilute acid breaks glycosidic bonds too.

<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} + \text{glucose} \;\underset{\text{hydrolysis}}{\overset{\text{condensation}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; \text{maltose} + \text{water}" />

Read the arrow to the right for condensation and to the left for hydrolysis. The three disaccharides you need to know differ in their second monosaccharide:

| Disaccharide | Made from | Enzyme that hydrolyses it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Maltose | Glucose and glucose | Maltase |
| Sucrose | Glucose and fructose | Sucrase |
| Lactose | Glucose and galactose | Lactase |

So you write the word equations for sucrose and lactose the same way:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} + \text{fructose} \rightleftharpoons \text{sucrose} + \text{water}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{glucose} + \text{galactose} \rightleftharpoons \text{lactose} + \text{water}" />
</MathContainer>

Polysaccharides are built the same way, just with many more bonds. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all chains of glucose.

A bond sits between two units, so **a chain of <InlineMath math="n" /> units has <InlineMath math="n - 1" /> bonds**. A chain of six glucose units has five bonds, not six. Building it by condensation releases five water molecules, and hydrolysing it completely uses up five water molecules too.

Each polysaccharide's shape fits its job. Starch is the glucose store of plants, in roots and seeds for example, with unbranched amylose and branched amylopectin. Glycogen is the glucose store of animals in liver and muscle cells, and its molecules are highly branched.

Cellulose builds plant cell walls. Its chains are long and straight, lie side by side, and are held together by hydrogen bonds into strong, fine fibres.

Why does a cell need carbohydrates? Glucose is the main fuel for cellular respiration. Starch and glycogen **store glucose**, and when the organism needs energy, enzymes break those stores back down into glucose. Cellulose holds up plant cells.

You can also identify sugars in the lab. Glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose are **reducing sugars**. Heat one with blue Benedict's solution in a boiling water bath, and the colour changes: green or yellow means a little sugar, and a brick-red precipitate means a lot.

Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar. The glycosidic bond in sucrose locks up the parts of glucose and fructose that would normally react, so Benedict's solution stays blue.

To detect sucrose, hydrolyse it first. Boil the solution with dilute hydrochloric acid so the sucrose breaks into glucose and fructose. Then neutralise the acid with sodium hydrogen carbonate, because Benedict's solution only reacts in alkaline conditions. Now run the Benedict's test.

| Solution | Benedict's test straight away | Boiled with acid, neutralised, then Benedict's test |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Glucose | Brick-red precipitate | Brick-red precipitate |
| Sucrose | Stays blue | Brick-red precipitate |

Try this one. A drink is tested with Benedict's solution and turns green. Another sample of the same drink is boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised and tested, and this time a brick-red precipitate forms. What's in the drink?

The green colour in the first test shows **a little reducing sugar**. After hydrolysis there's far more reducing sugar, so the extra sugar must have come from something that broke down in acid.

So the drink also contains a non-reducing sugar, probably sucrose. Acid also hydrolyses starch, so an iodine test for starch can rule that out.

## Folded Chains of Amino Acids

Picture a string of coloured beads folded into one particular shape, where the shape decides what it can do. That's a protein. The beads are **amino acids**. There are <InlineMath math="20" /> common kinds of amino acid in proteins. Each one has an amino group, a carboxyl group and an R group that's different for each kind.

Two amino acids join by condensation: one carboxyl group reacts with the next amino group, releasing water and forming a **peptide bond**. That makes a dipeptide, and adding more makes a polypeptide. In digestion, enzymes like pepsin hydrolyse those peptide bonds again.

<BlockMath math="\text{amino acid} + \text{amino acid} \rightleftharpoons \text{dipeptide} + \text{water}" />

A polypeptide chain doesn't stay straight. It folds on four levels, and a different set of bonds holds each level:

| Level | What happens | Bonds that hold it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Primary | The order of amino acids in the chain | Peptide bonds |
| Secondary | The chain coils into an alpha helix or folds into a beta pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds |
| Tertiary | The whole chain folds into a three-dimensional shape | Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions between R groups |
| Quaternary | Two or more chains join into one protein | The same bonds as at the tertiary level |

Haemoglobin in your red blood cells goes all the way to the quaternary level. One haemoglobin molecule is two alpha chains and two beta chains, each about <InlineMath math="150" /> amino acids long. A protein's shape depends on weak bonds. When the temperature, the pH or the chemicals around it change, those bonds are disrupted and the protein loses its shape. The protein is **denatured**, and it can't do its job any more.

Runny egg white turns white and firm in a hot pan because the albumin in it is denatured. That change can't be undone, although some other proteins do fold back into shape once conditions return to normal.

People often mix up denaturation and hydrolysis. That's also why a boiled egg still gives you amino acids when you digest it.

> Denaturation destroys a protein's three-dimensional fold without breaking a single peptide bond, whereas hydrolysis breaks those peptide bonds down to free amino acids.

Work through one example. Insulin, the hormone that controls the glucose level in your blood, has two chains: chain A with <InlineMath math="21" /> amino acids and chain B with <InlineMath math="30" /> amino acids.

Disulfide bonds hold the two chains together. How many peptide bonds does insulin have? A chain of <InlineMath math="n" /> amino acids has <InlineMath math="n - 1" /> peptide bonds:

<BlockMath math="(21 - 1) + (30 - 1) = 20 + 29 = 49" />

The disulfide bonds don't count, because they link the R groups of the two chains, not an amino group to a carboxyl group. Check another way: <InlineMath math="51" /> amino acids in <InlineMath math="2" /> chains give <InlineMath math="51 - 2 = 49" /> peptide bonds. So hydrolysing one insulin molecule completely takes **<InlineMath math="49" /> water molecules**.

Now it's your turn. A human haemoglobin alpha chain has <InlineMath math="141" /> amino acids, and a beta chain has <InlineMath math="146" />. How many peptide bonds are there in one haemoglobin molecule? After the haemoglobin is boiled, how many peptide bonds are left?

Haemoglobin has two alpha chains and two beta chains, so count each kind twice:

<BlockMath math="2(141 - 1) + 2(146 - 1) = 280 + 290 = 570" />

One haemoglobin molecule has <InlineMath math="570" /> peptide bonds. Boiling denatures the haemoglobin, but **the peptide bonds don't break**, so all <InlineMath math="570" /> are still there. What's lost is the folding and the joining of the four chains, so the haemoglobin can't carry oxygen any more.

Check by counting the total amino acids across the four chains:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2(141) + 2(146) = 574" />
<BlockMath math="574 - 4 = 570" />
</MathContainer>

Proteins do many jobs in your body. Enzymes speed up reactions, insulin controls blood glucose, haemoglobin carries oxygen, and antibodies fight pathogens. Keratin and collagen build structures, while actin and myosin contract muscles. Every job depends on **the protein having the right shape**.

## Fat Droplets and Membranes

Pour a little cooking oil into water, and the oil floats as droplets. Lipids are mostly nonpolar chains of carbon and hydrogen, so water molecules don't pull on them.

Lipids contain oxygen too, but very little. Glucose, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" />, has <InlineMath math="6" /> oxygen atoms out of <InlineMath math="24" /> atoms, whereas a palm oil triglyceride, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{53}H_{100}O_6}" />, has just <InlineMath math="6" /> oxygen atoms out of <InlineMath math="159" />.

Lipids come in four main types. **Fats and oils** are triglycerides. **Waxes** coat the leaves of some plants and the feathers of some water birds. **Phospholipids** build cell membranes. **Steroids** have four fused rings of carbon, such as cholesterol and the hormones testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone.

A triglyceride forms when one glycerol molecule joins three fatty acids by condensation. Each fatty acid forms one **ester bond** with the glycerol, so three water molecules are released. During digestion, lipase enzymes hydrolyse those ester bonds again.

<BlockMath math="\text{glycerol} + 3\text{ fatty acids} \rightleftharpoons \text{triglyceride} + 3\text{ water}" />

Fatty acids come in two kinds. In a **saturated fatty acid**, every bond between carbon atoms is a single bond, so the chain is full of hydrogen and straight. Straight chains pack tightly, so saturated fats are usually solid at room temperature, like animal fat.

An **unsaturated fatty acid** has at least one double bond. That bond bends the chain, so the chains can't pack tightly and the fat is a liquid oil, like olive oil or corn oil.

"Saturated" means saturated with hydrogen, not full of fat. Compare stearic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{18}H_{36}O_2}" />, with oleic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{18}H_{34}O_2}" />. Both have <InlineMath math="18" /> carbons, but oleic acid has two fewer hydrogens because of one double bond.

Palm oil holds a mix: about <InlineMath math="44\%" /> saturated palmitic acid and <InlineMath math="39\%" /> unsaturated oleic acid.

A phospholipid looks almost like a triglyceride, but its glycerol carries only two fatty acids and a phosphate group in the third place. So a phospholipid has a **hydrophilic head** attracted to water and two hydrophobic tails that keep away from it.

In water, triglycerides gather into droplets, because the whole molecule keeps away from water. Phospholipids line up in two layers instead, with the heads facing water on both sides and the tails hiding in the middle.

This **phospholipid bilayer** is the framework of the [plasma membrane that controls what enters and leaves a cell](/en/subjects/biology/membrane-transport/diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport), and it's only about <InlineMath math="4\text{ nm}" /> thick.

Try this calculation. In palm oil, a typical triglyceride carries palmitic acid at both ends of the glycerol and oleic acid in the middle.

Palmitic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{16}H_{32}O_2}" />, has <InlineMath math="M_r = 256" />. Oleic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{18}H_{34}O_2}" />, has <InlineMath math="M_r = 282" />, and glycerol, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_3H_8O_3}" />, has <InlineMath math="M_r = 92" />. What is <InlineMath math="M_r" /> of this triglyceride, and are all its chains saturated?

Three fatty acids form three ester bonds, so **three water molecules** are released, each with <InlineMath math="M_r = 18" />:

<BlockMath math="M_r = 92 + 2(256) + 282 - 3(18) = 832" />

Check with its formula, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_{53}H_{100}O_6}" />:

<BlockMath math="53(12) + 100(1) + 6(16) = 832" />

The two palmitic chains are saturated, but the oleic chain in the middle has one double bond, so not all its chains are saturated. This one molecule already carries both kinds of fatty acid, just as palm oil holds a mix of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids.

Lipids do many jobs in cells and multicellular organisms. Fat is a long-term energy store, giving more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. Fat under the skin also insulates the body against heat loss.

On leaves and feathers, wax stops water from sticking. Phospholipids build every cell membrane, and steroids such as testosterone and oestrogen act as hormones.

## Nucleotides in DNA and RNA

DNA stores the instructions for building and running a cell, and it writes them with just four kinds of "letter". Each letter is part of a unit called a **nucleotide**.

A nucleotide has three parts: a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar with five carbon atoms, and a phosphate group. The sugar of one nucleotide links to the phosphate of the next, forming a long chain called a polynucleotide.

DNA is two polynucleotide chains twisted into a **double helix**, like a twisted ladder. The sugars and phosphates form the backbone on the outside, while the bases pair up on the inside like rungs.

Adenine always pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine always pairs with cytosine through three. The two chains run in opposite directions.

The helix is <InlineMath math="2\text{ nm}" /> wide. Each base pair sits <InlineMath math="0.34\text{ nm}" /> from the next, and one turn of the helix holds <InlineMath math="10" /> base pairs:

<BlockMath math="10 \times 0.34\text{ nm} = 3.4\text{ nm}" />

If one DNA chain reads ATGC, its partner chain reads TACG. Every A is matched with T, every T with A, every G with C and every C with G. This rule of **complementary base pairing** is what lets one chain act as a template for the other.

RNA isn't just half a DNA molecule. RNA is usually a single chain, its sugar is ribose, and it has uracil instead of thymine:

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | Adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine | Adenine, uracil, guanine, cytosine |
| Number of chains | Two, twisted into a double helix | Usually one |
| Place in a eukaryotic cell | Stays in the nucleus | mRNA is made in the nucleus, then works in the cytoplasm |

The order of bases in DNA is **hereditary information**. Each time a cell divides, that order is copied, so the daughter cells inherit it.

The same order also directs **the making of proteins**. In the nucleus, part of the DNA is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), which attaches to a [ribosome, the organelle where proteins are built](/en/subjects/biology/cell-and-organisation/cell-structure-and-specialised-cells) in the cytoplasm. Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids there, and every three bases on the mRNA set one amino acid.

## Coiling DNA into Chromosomes

If you had a very long thread, you'd wind it onto a spool so it wouldn't tangle. A cell does the same with its DNA.

If the DNA from all <InlineMath math="46" /> chromosomes in one human cell nucleus were laid end to end, it would be about <InlineMath math="2\text{ m}" /> long. Yet that nucleus is only about <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="10\ \mu\text{m}" /> across.

At the first level, DNA wraps around proteins called **histones**. Eight histone molecules, two of each of four kinds, form one core, and DNA wrapped around one core is a nucleosome.

Nucleosomes are joined by linker DNA and look like beads on a string. That string of beads coils again into a chromatin fibre <InlineMath math="30\text{ nm}" /> thick.

Before a cell divides, its DNA is copied, and the chromatin fibres coil even more tightly with the help of scaffolding proteins. Once they're this tightly coiled, the chromosomes show up under a light microscope, reaching about <InlineMath math="700\text{ nm}" /> wide at metaphase.

The X shape you see is two identical **chromatids** held together at the centromere. So a chromosome is always DNA plus protein, and it isn't X-shaped all the time.
