## Muscles Pull on a Firm Frame

Your body can stand up and move because two parts work together. The **skeleton** is a firm frame, and **muscles** pull on that frame. A muscle can only pull. When it contracts, it gets shorter and pulls on a bone, but it can't push the bone back. So muscles work in pairs across a joint: one muscle bends the joint, and its partner straightens it again. A pair like this is called **antagonistic muscles**.

In the models here, you turn vertebrae around, move joints, bend an arm and take a step, watch four animals move, and compare healthy bones with bones that have a problem.

Try it yourself. Hold your upper arm with your other hand, then bend your elbow. The muscle at the front of your upper arm goes hard and bulges, because it's contracting. Now straighten your arm and press your palm down on a table. This time the muscle at the back of your upper arm goes hard. Each muscle pulls its own way, so your elbow needs both.

A muscle needs something firm to pull on, and that something is the skeleton. Skeletons come in different forms. A **hydrostatic skeleton** is fluid trapped in a body cavity, as in an earthworm. An **exoskeleton** is a hard case on the outside of the body, as on a grasshopper. An **endoskeleton** is bone and cartilage inside the body, as in fish, birds and you.

Besides giving muscles a place to attach, a skeleton does several other jobs for the body:

| Job of the skeleton | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Supporting the body and giving it shape | Your leg bones carry your weight, and an earthworm's fluid keeps its round shape |
| Protecting soft organs | The skull wraps around the brain, and the rib cage surrounds the heart and lungs |
| Allowing movement | Muscles pull on bones at joints |
| Making blood cells and storing minerals | Bone marrow makes blood cells, and bone stores calcium and phosphate |
| Cutting down water loss | The waxy layer on an insect's exoskeleton stops water evaporating |

An exoskeleton has one problem. The hard case doesn't grow along with the body. So a young grasshopper has to **moult**, which is called ecdysis: the old case splits, the animal climbs out in a new case that's still soft, and its body swells before the new case hardens. An endoskeleton doesn't have this problem, because your bones grow as you grow.

## Bones from Skull to Tailbone

An adult skeleton has <InlineMath math="206" /> bones. Picture a pole with arms and legs hanging off it. The pole in the middle is the **axial skeleton**: the skull, the vertebral column, the ribs and the sternum, plus the hyoid bone in your neck and the tiny ossicles in your ears. Together they make <InlineMath math="80" /> bones. The bones of the arms and legs, with the pectoral and pelvic girdles that join them to the pole, form the **appendicular skeleton**, with <InlineMath math="126" /> bones.

<BlockMath math="80 + 126 = 206" />

Don't mix up two names that sound almost the same. The **axis** is just one bone, the second vertebra in your neck. The axial skeleton is the whole central pole of the body with its <InlineMath math="80" /> bones, and the axis is only one of them.

The vertebral column, or backbone, is built from small bones called **vertebrae**. Their number depends on the region: <InlineMath math="7" /> cervical vertebrae in the neck, <InlineMath math="12" /> thoracic vertebrae in the chest, <InlineMath math="5" /> lumbar vertebrae in the lower back, <InlineMath math="5" /> sacral vertebrae and <InlineMath math="4" /> caudal vertebrae at the bottom. The sacral vertebrae fuse into one bone, the sacrum, and the caudal vertebrae fuse into the tailbone, the coccyx. So an adult backbone has fewer bones than vertebrae:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Vertebrae} = 7 + 12 + 5 + 5 + 4 = 33" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Adult bones} = 7 + 12 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 26" />
</MathContainer>

Most vertebrae follow the same plan. At the front is a thick, round block that carries weight, called the **body** of the vertebra. Behind it is a large hole, the neural canal, where the spinal cord runs and stays protected. From the ring of bone around that hole, a spinous process sticks out backwards and two transverse processes stick out sideways. Muscles and ligaments attach to these points. Between two vertebrae sits an **intervertebral disc**, a pad of cartilage that resists squashing.

What changes from region to region is the size and some extra features, and each difference matches what that vertebra does:

| Region | How many | What stands out | Job |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cervical | <InlineMath math="7" /> | Small body, a hole in each transverse process, spinous process often forked | Supports the neck, and an artery to the brain runs through the holes |
| Atlas, first cervical | <InlineMath math="1" /> | Ring-shaped, with no body and no spinous process | Holds up the skull so your head can nod |
| Axis, second cervical | <InlineMath math="1" /> | A peg called the dens points up into the ring of the atlas | Acts as a pivot so your head can turn |
| Thoracic | <InlineMath math="12" /> | Facets for the ribs, long spinous process pointing down | Gives the ribs a joint to move at |
| Lumbar | <InlineMath math="5" /> | Biggest, thickest body, short blunt spinous process | Carries the most body weight |
| Sacral | <InlineMath math="5" />, fused | One triangular bone | Joins the hip bones |
| Caudal | <InlineMath math="4" />, fused | The small coccyx | Takes a little weight when you sit |

Look down the column at the size of the bodies. Each vertebra carries the weight of everything above it, so **the bodies get bigger and thicker further down**. A cervical vertebra only holds up the head, so its body is small. A lumbar vertebra holds up the head, the arms and the whole trunk, so its body is the biggest.

Your own head shows what the atlas and axis do. When you nod yes, your skull moves on the atlas. When you shake your head no, the atlas and skull turn together around the dens of the axis. In the model, pick a region, then turn the vertebra that lifts out and find the features in the table.

## Joints from Locked to Free

A **joint** is where two bones meet. Not every joint moves the same amount. Some are locked tight, some move a little, and some move freely:

| Kind of joint | Example | Movement |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Immovable joint | Sutures between the skull bones | None, the bones are joined by fibrous tissue |
| Slightly movable joint | Between two vertebrae | A little, because the cartilage disc between them can bend |
| Hinge joint, freely movable | Elbow, knee | Bends and straightens in one plane, like a door hinge |
| Ball-and-socket joint, freely movable | Shoulder, hip | In every direction, and it can rotate |

The locked sutures turn your skull into a hard box around the brain. In a freely movable joint, the shape of the bone ends sets the directions. In a hinge joint, the rounded end of one bone fits into a hollow in the other, so the bone can only swing forwards and backwards. In a ball-and-socket joint, a ball-shaped end fits into a cup-shaped socket, so the bone can swing in every direction and rotate.

Your elbow is a hinge joint between the humerus in your upper arm and the radius and ulna in your forearm. The ends of all three bones are capped with **cartilage**, which is smooth and absorbs shocks. A capsule wraps the whole joint. Its inner lining, the **synovial membrane**, makes **synovial fluid** and releases it into the joint cavity. This fluid oils the joint so the cartilage surfaces don't grind, and it carries nutrients to the cartilage, which has no blood vessels of its own.

Bone is tied to bone by **ligaments**, strong bands of tissue that limit how far the joint can move. Skeletal muscle is joined to bone by **tendons**. When your biceps contracts, its tendon pulls the radius and your elbow bends.

Lots of students mix up tendons and ligaments. To check, look at what's at each end. If one end is a muscle, it's a tendon. If both ends are bones, it's a ligament. In the model, pick a kind of joint and try to move its bone. A suture won't budge, a hinge only swings in one plane, and a ball-and-socket joint can swing round in a circle.

## Paired Muscles in Arm and Leg

A skeletal muscle contracts when a nerve impulse reaches it, a signal carried by neurones in the [nervous system that coordinates your body](/en/subjects/biology/coordination/nerves-and-hormones). The contracting muscle gets shorter and pulls on a bone through its tendon. To pull that bone back, another muscle on the opposite side of the joint has to contract. Two muscles that work in opposite directions across one joint are **antagonistic muscles**. The muscle that bends a joint is called a flexor, and the muscle that straightens it is called an extensor.

### Bending Your Elbow

Your biceps sits at the front of your upper arm, and its lower tendon attaches to the radius. Your triceps sits at the back of your upper arm, and its tendon attaches to the end of the ulna, the hard point at the back of your elbow. These two muscles are an **antagonistic pair**:

| Elbow movement | Muscle that contracts | Muscle that relaxes | Bone pulled |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bending | Biceps, the flexor | Triceps | Radius |
| Straightening | Triceps, the extensor | Biceps | Ulna |

A relaxing muscle doesn't push the bone. It just stops contracting and lets its partner stretch it. That's why one muscle isn't enough for a joint. Your biceps can bend your elbow, but it can't straighten it again. In the model, change the elbow angle slowly, and watch which muscle gets shorter and fatter and which one gets longer.

### Taking One Step

Walking uses the same idea, but **each joint in your leg has its own pair of muscles**. One muscle of each pair sits at the front of your leg, and the other sits at the back:

| Joint | Muscle at the front | Muscle at the back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Knee | Quadriceps, straightens the knee | Hamstrings, bend the knee |
| Ankle | Tibialis anterior, lifts the front of the foot | Gastrocnemius, lifts the heel |

The gastrocnemius is your calf muscle. It lifts your heel by pulling on the heel bone through the Achilles tendon.

In one step, these movements happen in order. First your heel lifts and your toes push the ground backwards, so your body moves forwards. As your foot swings forwards, your knee bends and the front of your foot lifts so your toes don't catch on the floor. Before your foot lands, your knee straightens again and your heel touches the floor first. Meanwhile, your other leg carries your weight.

Before you switch the model to Leg, make a prediction. As your heel lifts off the floor, **which muscle in the ankle pair contracts**, and which one relaxes? Then pick Lift the heel in the model and check.

The gastrocnemius contracts. It pulls on the Achilles tendon, which is attached to your heel bone, so your heel lifts. The tibialis anterior at the front of your shin relaxes and gets stretched. You can check this on your own body: stand on your tiptoes, and your calf goes hard.

## Crawling to Flying

Animals move the same way you do. Muscles pull on a skeleton, and the body pushes on the ground, the water or the air. That push sends the animal the opposite way. What changes is **the kind of skeleton the muscles pull on**.

An earthworm has no bones, but it does have a skeleton. Its body is divided into many segments, and each segment is full of fluid. Fluid can hardly be squashed, so **each segment keeps the same volume**. Think of a water balloon: squeeze its middle and it gets longer. That's a hydrostatic skeleton.

The earthworm's body wall has two layers of muscle that form an antagonistic pair. When the **circular muscles** contract, a segment gets thin and long, so the front of the body reaches forwards. When the **longitudinal muscles** contract, a segment gets short and fat. On the fat segments, tiny stiff bristles called chaetae dig into the soil and grip it, so the body doesn't slide backwards. Waves of lengthening and shortening run along the body, and the back end gets pulled forwards.

Have a think before you read the answer. An earthworm gets pierced, and fluid leaks out of a few of its segments. **Can those leaking segments still help the worm move**? Explain by saying what the muscles press against.

No, they can't. The circular and longitudinal muscles can only pull on the body wall. In a healthy segment, that pull squeezes the fluid. The fluid can't be squashed, so it pushes the wall out another way, and the segment gets longer or fatter. Without the fluid, a contracting muscle just wrinkles a floppy wall. The segment doesn't get longer or fatter, so its chaetae can't grip the soil. That's why the fluid counts as a skeleton: it does the same job as the bones in your body.

Now work out how much a segment bulges. Treat one segment as a cylinder with a radius of <InlineMath math="3\text{ mm}" /> and a length of <InlineMath math="3\text{ mm}" />, whose volume doesn't change. The longitudinal muscles shorten the segment to <InlineMath math="2\text{ mm}" />. **What's the new radius of the segment**?

The volume of a cylinder is <InlineMath math="V = \pi r^2 L" />. No fluid gets added or lost, so set the volume before and after the segment shortens equal, then solve for the new radius <InlineMath math="r" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\pi \times 3^2 \times 3 = \pi \times r^2 \times 2" />
<BlockMath math="r^2 = \frac{27}{2} = 13.5" />
<BlockMath math="r = \sqrt{13.5} \approx 3.67\text{ mm}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the volume: <InlineMath math="\pi \times 13.5 \times 2 = 27\pi" />, the same as <InlineMath math="\pi \times 3^2 \times 3 = 27\pi" />, which is about <InlineMath math="84.8\text{ mm}^3" />. The segment is now <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{3}" /> of its old length, and its radius is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.22" /> times as big, about <InlineMath math="22\%" /> more. Fat segments like this one press the chaetae into the walls of the burrow.

A fish has an endoskeleton with a bendy backbone. Along each side of its body are blocks of muscle called **myotomes**. The myotomes on the left and right contract in turn, and each contraction travels from head to tail. The fish's body bends into an S shape, and its body and tail fin push the water backwards. The dorsal and anal fins keep the body steady like the keel of a boat, while the pectoral and pelvic fins help the fish turn.

A grasshopper has an exoskeleton, so its muscles sit inside that hard tube and attach to its inner surface. The femur of its hind leg holds a big extensor muscle, which straightens the tibia, and a smaller flexor muscle, which folds the tibia.

Before a jump, the grasshopper folds its tibia tight against the femur. The flexor holds the tibia folded while the extensor contracts, so **energy gets stored in stiff parts of the knee**, like a bow being drawn. When the flexor relaxes, the tibia kicks back in <InlineMath math="25" /> to <InlineMath math="30" /> milliseconds and pushes on the ground, and the grasshopper shoots into the air.

A bird has a light endoskeleton with many hollow bones, and its sternum has a big keel. Two flight muscles attach to that keel. The pectoralis major pulls the wing down, and this downstroke pushes on the air. The muscle that lifts the wing is the supracoracoideus. Many school books call it the pectoralis minor.

Lots of students think the muscle that lifts the wing must sit above the wing, on the bird's back. In fact, **both flight muscles sit on the chest**, below the wing. The supracoracoideus tendon runs over a bone at the shoulder and attaches to the top of the humerus, like a rope running over a pulley. That's how a muscle on the chest can pull the wing up.

| Animal | Skeleton | Muscle pair | Pushes against |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Earthworm | Hydrostatic skeleton | Circular and longitudinal muscles | Soil |
| Fish | Endoskeleton | Left and right myotomes | Water |
| Grasshopper | Exoskeleton | Extensor and flexor of the hind leg | Ground |
| Bird | Endoskeleton | Pectoralis major and supracoracoideus | Air |

## Weak Bones and Sore Joints

Bone is living tissue that's always being broken down and built up again. Until about age <InlineMath math="30" />, building wins, and **bone density reaches its peak**. After that, bone slowly gets less. A skeleton can also go wrong in other ways: bones can get thin, soft or bent, and joints can wear out or get inflamed.

### Thin Soft and Bent Bones

In **osteoporosis**, bone is broken down faster than it's built, so bone mass falls. Bones get porous and thin and break easily, especially at the top of the femur, in the vertebrae and at the wrist. Women are at most risk after the menopause, at about age <InlineMath math="50" />, because the ovaries stop releasing oestrogen, a hormone that helps build bone.

**Osteomalacia** happens in adults. The soft base material of bone still gets made, but not enough calcium and phosphate get laid down in it, so the bones turn soft. The cause is usually too little vitamin D, because without vitamin D your small intestine can't take in calcium during [digestion and absorption of food](/en/subjects/biology/digestive-system/digestion-and-absorption). **Rickets** is the same problem in children. A child's bones are still growing, so soft bones can change shape under the body's weight.

**Scoliosis** changes the shape of the backbone instead. Seen from behind, a healthy backbone looks straight. In scoliosis, the backbone curves sideways and twists. The most common kind shows up in children aged <InlineMath math="11" /> and over, and the curve tends to get worse while teenagers grow fast. Its cause is usually unknown.

Osteoporosis and osteomalacia both make bones weak, so they're easy to mix up. The difference is what's missing. Picture a brick wall. In osteoporosis, there are too few bricks, but each brick is still hard. In osteomalacia, there are enough bricks, but **the bricks never fully harden**.

| Condition | What changes | Most at risk | Main cause |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Osteoporosis | Bone mass falls, bones get porous and brittle | Women after the menopause | Bone broken down faster than it's built |
| Osteomalacia | Bones lack mineral and turn soft | Adults | Too little vitamin D |
| Rickets | Growing bones turn soft and change shape | Children | Too little vitamin D |
| Scoliosis | Backbone curves sideways and twists | Children and teenagers, more often girls | Usually unknown |

### Worn Joints and Habits

**Arthritis** is inflammation of a joint that makes it painful and stiff. The most common kind, osteoarthritis, happens when the joint cartilage wears away until bone rubs on bone. The risk goes up with age, joint injuries and carrying too much body weight. In rheumatoid arthritis, the immune system attacks the joints and the synovial membrane gets inflamed. Gout happens when crystals of uric acid build up inside a joint. Pick Arthritis in the model and compare healthy joint cartilage with worn cartilage.

Most of these problems can be prevented or slowed down with daily habits. Each habit looks after one part of your muscles and skeleton:

| Habit | Example | What it does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Exercise | Walking, running, jumping and weight training | Bones that carry a load get denser |
| Balanced diet | Milk, anchovies, green vegetables, and morning sun for vitamin D | Calcium and vitamin D build bone mineral |
| Correct posture | Sitting up straight, lifting with bent knees, holding your phone at eye level | Less strain on the backbone and discs |
| Suitable clothes | Sports shoes that fit when you exercise, clothes that don't restrict movement | Feet are supported and joints move freely |

Your phone is a clear example. An adult's head weighs about <InlineMath math="5\text{ kg}" /> when they look straight ahead. A computer model of the neck worked out that when the head tips <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> down towards a phone, **the neck carries a load as heavy as about <InlineMath math="27\text{ kg}" />**. So lift your phone higher, and straighten up after looking down for a long time.

One last question for you to think through. A teenager plays football <InlineMath math="3" /> times a week and drinks milk every day. **Justify why these habits lower their risk of osteoporosis** at age <InlineMath math="70" />, even though osteoporosis is rare in teenagers.

Bone density peaks at about age <InlineMath math="30" />, and after that everyone slowly loses bone. Running and jumping in football are weight-bearing exercise, so the bones in the legs and hips get denser. Milk supplies calcium, the mineral that hardens bone. Both habits raise **peak bone mass**. Starting from a higher peak, the same loss of bone after age <InlineMath math="30" /> takes longer to make the bones brittle.
