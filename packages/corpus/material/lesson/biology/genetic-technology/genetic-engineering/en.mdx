## Genetic Engineering Changes the DNA of an Organism

**Genetic engineering** is changing an organism's DNA on purpose, usually by moving a gene from one organism into another. The result is a **genetically modified organism (GMO)**: an organism whose DNA was altered this way. DNA that joins genes from two different sources is called **recombinant DNA**. **Biotechnology** is the wider field: using living things, or parts of them, to make products or solve problems. Next you look inside a bacterium, grow a colony that makes insulin, copy DNA with PCR, and run a gel to match a DNA profile.

Moving a gene takes three tools. A **restriction enzyme** cuts DNA at one exact base sequence. **DNA ligase** joins two cut pieces back into one strand. A **vector** carries the new gene into the host cell, and the vector used most often is a **plasmid**: a small ring of DNA that sits in a bacterium apart from its main chromosome.

[Bacteria](/en/subjects/biology/biodiversity/bacteria) make good hosts because they copy their plasmids every time they divide, and they read a human gene with the same genetic code that human cells use. Open the bacterium below and find the small ring beside the tangled nucleoid.

## Bacteria Can Make Human Insulin

Some people with diabetes cannot make enough **insulin**, the hormone that lowers blood glucose. Insulin used to come from the pancreas of pigs and cattle. Since 1982, **recombinant human insulin** made by bacteria has been sold, and it has exactly the same amino acid sequence as the insulin a human pancreas makes.

The steps follow the three tools in order:

1. Cut the **human insulin gene** out of human DNA with a restriction enzyme.
2. Cut a plasmid open with **the same restriction enzyme**, so both cuts leave matching single-stranded ends, called sticky ends.
3. Mix the gene and the plasmid. Their sticky ends pair up, and **DNA ligase** seals them into a recombinant plasmid.
4. Put the recombinant plasmids into bacteria. Only some cells take one up, so those cells are selected and kept.
5. Grow the selected bacteria in a large fermenter. Each cell reads the insulin gene and makes insulin, which is then extracted and purified.

## Genetically Modified Organisms and Food

A GMO gets a gene that gives it a trait it did not have. Food made from a GMO, or containing parts of one, is called **genetically modified food (GMF)**. Read each row as "which gene went in, and what did it change?"

| GMO | Gene added | New trait |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bt maize and Bt cotton | A toxin gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis | Kills the caterpillars that eat the plant, so farmers spray less insecticide |
| Golden rice | Genes that make beta-carotene in the rice grain | Grains supply vitamin A, which plain rice lacks |
| Herbicide-tolerant soybean | A gene that lets the plant survive one herbicide | Farmers can kill weeds without killing the crop |
| Fast-growing salmon | A growth hormone gene from another salmon species | Reaches market size sooner |
| Bacteria that make insulin | The human insulin gene | Make human insulin in a fermenter |

The benefits are real: **higher yields, fewer pesticides, better nutrition and cheaper medicine**. The risks are real too, and they need checking case by case. A gene can spread through pollen to wild relatives. Insects that keep eating Bt crops can evolve resistance to the toxin. A new protein in food has to be tested so it does not trigger allergies. Malaysia controls the release and import of GMOs under the **Biosafety Act 2007**, so a GMO is assessed before it reaches farms or food shops.

## Biotechnology Puts Living Things to Work

Biotechnology is older than genetic engineering. Making tempeh with a mould and yogurt with bacteria are biotechnology, because living things do the work. Genetic engineering is the modern part of it that changes the organism's DNA first. Biotechnology is grouped by **the field it serves**:

| Field | Example | What the living thing does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Medicine | Insulin, the hepatitis B vaccine | Bacteria or yeast make a human protein or a virus protein |
| Agriculture | GM crops, tissue culture of oil palm | Plants carry a new gene, or one plant is cloned into thousands |
| Industry | Enzymes in washing powder | Microbes make enzymes that break down protein and fat stains |
| Environment | Cleaning oil spills | Bacteria break down oil into harmless substances |
| Forensics | DNA profiling | DNA identifies a person from a drop of blood or a hair |

**Gene therapy** is a medical use that treats a genetic disease by putting a working copy of a gene into the patient's own cells. A harmless virus often serves as the vector. It aims at the cause, the faulty gene, instead of treating the symptoms for life.

## PCR Makes Millions of Copies of a DNA Sample

A hair or a drop of blood holds too little DNA to test. The **polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** copies one stretch of DNA again and again. Each cycle has three steps. Heating to about <InlineMath math="95^\circ\text{C}" /> separates the two strands. Cooling lets short primers attach at the start of the stretch. At about <InlineMath math="72^\circ\text{C}" /> a heat-stable DNA polymerase builds a new strand on each old one. ## DNA Profiling Compares Band Patterns

**DNA profiling**, also called DNA fingerprinting, identifies a person from their DNA. About <InlineMath math="99.9\%" /> of the DNA of any two people is the same, so a profile looks only at regions where people differ. The most useful regions hold a short sequence repeated many times, and the **number of repeats differs from person to person**. Only identical twins share the same profile. ## Benefits and Limits of Genetic Technology

Genetic technology **solves problems that older methods could not**. Insulin no longer depends on animal organs. A crop can carry a trait that years of cross-breeding could never give it, such as a bacterial toxin gene. A tiny trace of DNA can identify a suspect, free a wrongly accused person, or name a victim after a disaster.

The same power is why it needs rules. A gene released in a crop cannot be recalled. A DNA profile is private information about a person and their relatives. Changing human genes raises the question of which changes are treatments and which are not. So **each use is judged by its evidence**: what the change does, what it might harm, and who decides.

## Common Mistakes in Genetic Technology

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The gene and the plasmid can be cut with different restriction enzymes. | Only the same enzyme leaves sticky ends that match and pair. | Name one enzyme for both cuts. |
| Ligase cuts DNA. | Restriction enzymes cut, and ligase joins the cut ends. | Ligase links, restriction enzymes cut. |
| Every GMO is a crop. | Bacteria, animals and plants can all be GMOs. | Ask whether the organism's DNA was changed. |
| PCR adds a fixed number of copies each cycle. | Each cycle doubles the copies, so the growth is <InlineMath math="2^n" />. | Ten cycles must give <InlineMath math="1{,}024" /> copies from one. |
| Larger DNA fragments travel farther in a gel. | Larger fragments move more slowly through the gel. | The smallest fragment sits farthest from the well. |

The fourth mistake leads to answers that are far too small. If you add <InlineMath math="2" /> copies per cycle, ten cycles give <InlineMath math="21" /> copies instead of <InlineMath math="1{,}024" />. Remember: **each cycle doubles what is already there**.

## Practice with Genetic Technology

The problems go from the tools to the numbers behind insulin, PCR and profiling. **Write the formula first** in each calculation, then substitute.

1. A student cuts the insulin gene with enzyme X and the plasmid with enzyme Y. Explain why the gene will not join the plasmid, and name the enzyme that joins DNA.

2. A fermenter starts with <InlineMath math="100" /> recombinant bacteria that double every <InlineMath math="30" /> minutes. How many bacteria are there after <InlineMath math="3" /> hours?

3. How many PCR cycles turn one copy of a DNA stretch into more than one million copies?

4. In the gel model <InlineMath math="d = h(7 - 2\log L)" />, how far apart are a <InlineMath math="250" /> base pair band and a <InlineMath math="500" /> base pair band after <InlineMath math="1" /> hour?

### Answers

**Answer 1**: Enzymes X and Y cut at different sequences, so the ends they leave **do not pair**. The single-stranded ends of the gene cannot match the ends of the plasmid, and the gene cannot slot in. Cutting both with the same enzyme fixes it, and **DNA ligase** then seals the joins. Check: in the insulin steps, step 2 uses the same enzyme for exactly this reason.

**Answer 2**: Three hours is <InlineMath math="180" /> minutes, so the bacteria double <InlineMath math="6" /> times:

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{180}{30} = 6, \qquad N = 100 \times 2^6 = 100 \times 64 = 6{,}400" />

Check by doubling step by step: <InlineMath math="100, 200, 400, 800, 1{,}600, 3{,}200, 6{,}400" />. That is six doublings.

**Answer 3**: You need the smallest <InlineMath math="n" /> with <InlineMath math="2^n > 1{,}000{,}000" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2^{19} = 524{,}288 < 1{,}000{,}000" />
<BlockMath math="2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576 > 1{,}000{,}000" />
</MathContainer>

So **<InlineMath math="20" /> cycles** are needed. Check: <InlineMath math="2^{10} = 1{,}024 \approx 10^3" />, and <InlineMath math="(10^3)^2 = 10^6" />, so about twenty cycles is right.

**Answer 4**: Find each distance at <InlineMath math="h = 1" />, then subtract:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="d_{250} = 7 - 2\log 250 = 7 - 4.796 = 2.204\text{ cm}" />
<BlockMath math="d_{500} = 7 - 2\log 500 = 7 - 5.398 = 1.602\text{ cm}" />
<BlockMath math="d_{250} - d_{500} = 0.602\text{ cm}" />
</MathContainer>

The bands are about <InlineMath math="0.60\text{ cm}" /> apart, and **the shorter fragment is lower**, farther from the well. Check: <InlineMath math="500" /> is twice <InlineMath math="250" />, so the gap is <InlineMath math="2\log 2 = 0.602" />, the same answer.
