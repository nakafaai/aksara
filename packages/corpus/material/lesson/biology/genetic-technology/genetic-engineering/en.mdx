## Tools of Genetic Engineering

Imagine snipping one recipe out of a giant cookbook and taping it into a tiny pocket notebook that a fast chef copies all day. **Genetic engineering** works in the same spirit by moving a useful gene from one organism into another.

An organism whose DNA is changed this way is a **genetically modified organism (GMO)**, and DNA combining genes from two sources is **recombinant DNA**. **Biotechnology** is the broader field of using living things, or parts of them, to make helpful products or solve practical problems.

Transferring a gene takes three exact tools. A **restriction enzyme** acts as molecular scissors, cutting DNA at one specific base sequence. **DNA ligase** acts as molecular glue, sealing two cut pieces into one strand. Remember never to mix those two roles up: restriction enzymes cut, while ligase joins.

> Cutting both the target gene and the plasmid with the same restriction enzyme creates matching sticky ends that pair up before DNA ligase seals them.

The third tool is a **vector** that carries the gene into a host cell, usually a small bacterial DNA ring called a **plasmid**. [Bacteria](/en/subjects/biology/biodiversity/bacteria) copy plasmids at every division and read human genes with the same genetic code. Because the plasmid floats apart from the main chromosome, scientists can splice a new gene into that ring without breaking the bacterium's survival genes. Once inside, the cell's own ribosomes read the inserted recipe and build the new protein.

## Making Insulin with Bacteria

People with certain forms of diabetes cannot make enough **insulin**, the hormone that lowers blood glucose. Insulin once had to be extracted from pig and cattle pancreases. Today, **recombinant human insulin** made by bacteria matches the human amino acid sequence exactly.

Building an insulin factory follows the three tools in order:

1. Cut the **human insulin gene** out of human DNA using a restriction enzyme.
2. Open a bacterial plasmid with **the same restriction enzyme** so both pieces expose matching single-stranded sticky ends.
3. Mix the gene and open plasmid so their sticky ends pair up, then seal the joins with **DNA ligase**.
4. Insert the recombinant plasmids into bacteria and select the cells that actually took one up.
5. Grow the selected bacteria in a large fermenter, then extract and purify the human insulin they produce.

If a student accidentally cuts the gene with one enzyme and the plasmid with a different enzyme, their sticky ends will not match and the gene cannot slot into the ring. Trace how the single-enzyme pipeline flows below.

## Modified Organisms and Biotech

Genetic engineering powers far more than insulin vials. It reaches farm crops, food nutrition, industrial enzymes, environmental cleanups, and medical therapies, which is why **biosafety rules weigh every benefit against its risks**.

### Modified Crops and Foods

When an organism receives a gene for a new trait, it becomes a GMO, and food made from it is **genetically modified food (GMF)**. GMOs are not only crops: bacteria, animals, and plants can all be genetically modified.

| GMO | Gene added | New trait |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bt maize and Bt cotton | A toxin gene from Bacillus thuringiensis | Kills caterpillars that eat the crop, reducing insecticide sprays |
| Golden rice | Genes that make beta-carotene in the grain | Supplies vitamin A, which plain rice lacks |
| Herbicide-tolerant soybean | A gene that lets the plant survive one herbicide | Lets farmers clear weeds without harming the crop |
| Fast-growing salmon | A growth hormone gene from another salmon species | Reaches market size sooner |
| Insulin bacteria | The human insulin gene | Produces human insulin inside a fermenter |

The benefits include **higher yields, fewer chemical sprays, richer nutrition, and affordable medicine**. Yet every case requires careful checks. Pollen can carry an inserted gene to wild relatives, pests can evolve resistance to Bt toxin, and new food proteins need allergy testing before release under laws like Malaysia's **Biosafety Act 2007**.

### Fields and Gene Therapy

Biotechnology is much older than genetic engineering. Fermenting tempeh with mould or yogurt with bacteria already puts living microbes to work, while genetic engineering is the modern branch that edits DNA directly.

| Field | Example | What the living thing does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Medicine | Insulin and hepatitis B vaccine | Bacteria or yeast build human or viral proteins |
| Agriculture | GM crops and oil palm tissue culture | Plants carry a new trait, or one plant clones into thousands |
| Industry | Enzymes in washing powder | Microbes make enzymes that break down protein and fat stains |
| Environment | Cleaning oil spills | Bacteria break oil into harmless compounds |
| Forensics | DNA profiling | DNA identifies a person from a tiny blood or hair trace |

In medicine, **gene therapy** treats a genetic disease at its root by placing a working gene copy into a patient's cells with a harmless viral vector. Because released genes cannot be recalled and DNA profiles carry private family data, every use needs clear evidence and oversight.

## Copying DNA with PCR

A single hair or dried drop of blood holds far too little DNA to analyse directly. Think of the **polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** as a molecular photocopier that turns one faint page into millions of clear copies inside a few hours.

Every PCR cycle repeats three temperature steps. Heating to about <InlineMath math="95^\circ\text{C}" /> unzips both DNA strands, cooling lets short primers lock onto the target start, and warming to <InlineMath math="72^\circ\text{C}" /> lets heat-stable DNA polymerase build each new strand. Adding <InlineMath math="2" /> each cycle would give only <InlineMath math="21" /> copies after ten rounds instead of <InlineMath math="1{,}024" />. Suppose a swab starts with <InlineMath math="10" /> copies and runs <InlineMath math="25" /> cycles:

To turn a single starting copy into more than one million copies requires <InlineMath math="20" /> cycles, because <InlineMath math="2^{19} = 524{,}288" /> falls short while <InlineMath math="2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576" /> crosses the mark.

## Comparing DNA by Gel Bands

About <InlineMath math="99.9\%" /> of human DNA is shared by everyone, so **DNA profiling** focuses on short repeating regions whose lengths vary between individuals. Only identical twins share the exact same pattern, which labs separate by pulling fragments through a porous gel.

### Length and Gel Distance

Picture a dense jungle of tangled vines. Small runners slip through tiny gaps quickly, while tall giants keep snagging and move slowly. In gel electrophoresis, an electric field pulls negatively charged DNA toward the positive end, so **shorter fragments travel farther** than longer ones.

In our gel model, the distance <InlineMath math="d" /> in centimetres that a fragment of <InlineMath math="L" /> base pairs moves during <InlineMath math="h" /> hours follows a logarithmic law:

<BlockMath math="d = h(7 - 2\log L)" />

Slide the fragment length <InlineMath math="L" /> below to see how smoothly travel distance shrinks as DNA pieces grow longer.
