## The Body Defends Itself with Three Lines of Defence

**Immunity** is the body's ability to fight infection by pathogens. The body has **three lines of defence**. The first line stops pathogens getting in. The second line destroys pathogens that do get in, whatever kind they are. The third line is the **specific immune response** by lymphocytes, which make antibodies against a particular antigen and remember it. In the models here, you watch antibodies block virions, compare the primary and secondary responses and see how vaccination slows an outbreak.

## The First and Second Lines of Defence

The first line is made of **physical and chemical barriers**: unbroken skin, mucus and cilia in the airways, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and hydrochloric acid in the stomach.

If a pathogen gets past the first line, the second line acts:

- **phagocytosis** by neutrophils and monocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens,
- **inflammation**, where a wound turns red, hot and swollen because more blood and phagocytes are brought there,
- **fever**, where body temperature rises to slow the pathogen's reproduction.

## Antigens and Antibodies

An **antigen** is a foreign molecule, usually a protein on the surface of a pathogen, that triggers an immune response. An **antibody** is a Y-shaped protein made by B lymphocytes. Antibodies destroy pathogens in several ways:

| Action | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Neutralisation | Antibodies cover the active site of a toxin or a virus's spike proteins |
| Agglutination | Antibodies clump pathogens together so phagocytes can engulf them easily |
| Precipitation | Antibodies make soluble antigens settle out |
| Lysis | Antibodies break the pathogen's membrane |
| Opsonisation | Antibodies tag pathogens for phagocytosis |

## Primary and Secondary Responses

When an antigen enters the body for the first time, lymphocytes take several days to multiply and make antibodies. This is the **primary response**. Some lymphocytes become **memory cells**. When the same antigen enters again, memory cells act quickly and make more antibodies. This is the **secondary response**.

### Booster Doses

Vaccines make use of the secondary response. The first dose triggers a primary response and produces memory cells. A **booster dose** triggers a secondary response, so the antibody level rises higher and lasts longer.

## Types of Immunity

| Type | Natural | Artificial |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Active, the body makes its own antibodies | After recovering from an infection | After receiving a vaccine |
| Passive, antibodies come from outside | Mother's antibodies through the placenta or breast milk | Injection of antibody serum |

**Active immunity lasts a long time** because memory cells form. Passive immunity acts at once but is only temporary, because the antibodies received are eventually broken down and no memory cells form.

## Vaccination and Herd Immunity

### A Virus That Attacks the Immune System

**HIV** infects helper T cells, the lymphocytes that control the immune response. As the number of these cells falls, the body can no longer fight ordinary infections, and the patient develops **AIDS**. HIV spreads through blood, sexual contact and from mother to baby, not through everyday touch.

## Common Mistakes about Immunity

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| An antibody can fight any pathogen. | Each antibody is specific to one antigen only. | Match the shape of the binding site to the antigen. |
| Vaccines contain antibodies. | Vaccines contain weakened or killed antigens. Serum is what contains antibodies. | Ask whether the body has to make its own antibodies. |
| Passive immunity lasts a lifetime. | Passive immunity is temporary because no memory cells form. | Find where the antibodies came from. |
| Phagocytosis is a specific response. | Phagocytosis belongs to the non-specific second line. | Ask whether the cell recognises a particular antigen. |

The second mistake is the most common one in questions on types of immunity. **Vaccines carry antigens, serum carries antibodies**.

2. A baby receives antibodies through breast milk. Name this type of immunity and explain why it doesn't last.

3. A disease has <InlineMath math="R_0 = 4" />. Calculate the herd immunity threshold.

4. Explain why people who have had chickenpox rarely catch it a second time.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Unbroken **skin** is a physical barrier pathogens can't get through. **Mucus and cilia** in the airways trap pathogens and sweep them up to the throat.

**Solution 2**: This is **natural passive immunity**. The antibodies come from outside, so the baby forms no memory cells, and the antibodies are broken down within a few weeks.

**Solution 3**: Use the threshold formula:

<BlockMath math="1 - \frac{1}{4} = 0.75 = 75\%" />

Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="r = 4" />, it reads <InlineMath math="75" />.

**Solution 4**: The first infection produces **memory cells** and natural active immunity. When the same virus enters again, the secondary response makes antibodies quickly and in large amounts, so the virus is destroyed before symptoms appear.
