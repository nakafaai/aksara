## Three Lines of Body Defence

Think of your body as a guarded castle. Thick outer walls block strangers at the gate, patrolling guards inside grab any trespasser on sight, and trained specialists study one exact intruder so they can defeat it fast if it ever returns. **Immunity** works through those same **three lines of defence** against pathogens.

The first line builds **physical and chemical barriers** that keep germs out: unbroken skin, airway mucus swept upward by cilia, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and strong hydrochloric acid in the stomach.

If a pathogen slips past those barriers, the non-specific second line responds against any invader:

- **Phagocytosis**, where white blood cells such as neutrophils and monocytes engulf and digest pathogens.
- **Inflammation**, where an injured spot turns red, warm, and swollen as extra blood and phagocytes rush in.
- **Fever**, where higher body temperature slows down how fast pathogens multiply.

> The first two lines fight any germ without recognising its identity, while the third line builds specific antibodies and lasting memory against one exact antigen.

Keep phagocytosis firmly inside the non-specific second line rather than the third line: a phagocyte swallows any foreign germ it meets without matching a custom antigen shape.

Because spike proteins coat the entire outer envelope, antibodies can latch onto them before the virus touches a human cell. Depending on the pathogen, antibodies clear threats in five main ways:

| Action | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Neutralisation | Antibodies cover the active site of a toxin or a virus's spike proteins |
| Agglutination | Antibodies clump pathogens together so phagocytes engulf them easily |
| Precipitation | Antibodies make dissolved antigens settle out of solution |
| Lysis | Antibodies break open the pathogen's membrane |
| Opsonisation | Antibodies tag pathogens so phagocytes spot and swallow them faster |

## Immune Memory and Boosters

Meeting a new antigen takes days of training, yet once your lymphocytes finish that first fight they keep **long-lived memory cells**. Those saved blueprints turn future encounters and booster shots into swift, overwhelming defences.

### Primary and Secondary

On first contact with an antigen, lymphocytes need several days to multiply and produce antibodies in the **primary response**. When that same antigen returns later, memory cells wake instantly and launch a faster, much stronger **secondary response**. Because memory cells make the response **twice as fast and three times as strong**, antibody levels cross the protective line before symptoms develop. That is why people who recover from chickenpox rarely catch it a second time.

### Booster Doses and Types

Vaccines tap directly into that memory loop. A first dose introduces harmless antigens to spark a primary response and store memory cells, and a **booster dose** triggers a secondary surge that lifts protection higher and longer.

| Type | Natural | Artificial |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Active, body makes its own antibodies | Recovering from an infection | Receiving a vaccine |
| Passive, ready-made antibodies enter | Mother's antibodies via placenta or breast milk | Injection of antibody serum |

Do not confuse vaccines with serum: **vaccines carry antigens** so your own body builds memory cells, while **serum carries ready-made antibodies**. Passive immunity, such as a baby receiving antibodies through breast milk, protects at once yet fades in weeks because no memory cells are made.

## Outbreaks and Herd Immunity

Picture a spark landing in a forest where most trees are soaked with rain. Because the flame struggles to jump from one dry tree to the next, the fire fizzles out. **Herd immunity** works the same way: when enough people are immune, a pathogen runs out of hosts, shielding babies and patients who cannot be vaccinated.

## How a Virus Weakens Immunity

Now imagine an intruder that sneaks past the castle guards and knocks out the radio commander who coordinates every defence unit. **HIV** attacks the immune system in exactly that way by infecting **helper T cells**, the lymphocytes that activate and direct other immune cells.

As helper T cell counts fall over time, the body loses its ability to fight off ordinary bacteria, fungi, and viruses that a healthy immune system clears easily. That advanced stage of immune failure is called **AIDS**.

Understanding how HIV spreads prevents both infection and unfair stigma. HIV passes through infected blood, sexual contact, and from mother to baby during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding, never through shaking hands, sharing utensils, or everyday casual touch.
