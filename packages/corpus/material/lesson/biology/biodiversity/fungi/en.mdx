## The White Threads in Tempeh Are Hyphae

In tempeh, the readily visible fungal structure is a **network of white hyphae** between the soybeans. Hyphae elongate and branch into mycelium. Distinguishing this network from roots or other organisms also requires evidence about cell structure and nutrient uptake.

### A Mycelium Is a Network of Hyphae

In tempeh, a dense network of white hyphae from *Rhizopus* fungi binds the soybeans together. Those white threads are fungal hyphae. Many fungi build their bodies from hyphae that spread through food material. Unicellular fungi such as yeasts have a different body form.

**Fungi** are eukaryotic heterotrophs. **Eukaryotic** means their cells have a membrane-bound nucleus. **Heterotrophic** means they do not make their own food like chlorophyll-bearing plants. Fungi obtain nutrients by absorbing organic matter from living organisms or remains.

### Fungi Absorb Nutrients from Organic Material

Fungi are heterotrophic eukaryotes characterized by structures such as hyphae, mycelium, and spores.

Fungi do not photosynthesize. Fungal cells **absorb organic molecules** from their surroundings. In filamentous fungi, the hyphal network expands the absorbing surface.

> A fine branching network that absorbs nutrients from organic material may be fungal hyphae. Cell structure and other evidence are still needed to distinguish it from plant roots or another organism.

## Fungi Feed Differently from Animals

Fungi release enzymes onto organic material and **absorb the smaller molecules produced by digestion**. Animals usually digest food inside the body, while plants make sugars through photosynthesis.

### Digestion Happens Outside the Body

Animals usually take food into the body and digest it inside. <Highlight>Fungi release enzymes into the environment</Highlight>, break complex organic material into smaller molecules, and then absorb the products.

This process is called **extracellular digestion**. **Hyphae** are fine threads that make up much of the fungal body and provide the contact surface for digestion and absorption. A mass of hyphae is called **mycelium**. As the mycelium spreads, a larger surface can contact the food.

### Chitin Separates Fungal Walls from Plant Walls

Fungal cell walls contain **chitin**, while plant cell walls mainly contain cellulose. Chitin is also found in insect exoskeletons. Cell-wall material is one of the traits biologists use to distinguish fungi from plants.

## Fungal Dispersal through Spores

Depending on the species and life-cycle stage, fungi can form spores sexually or asexually. Spores may disperse through air, water, animals, or contact with surfaces. Under suitable conditions, **a viable spore** can germinate and produce new hyphae.

Fungi grow easily on moist, nutrient-rich food when the temperature is suitable and viable spores or hyphal fragments are present.

## A Fungal Role Depends on Its Environment

A fungus on a plant root may help the plant obtain water and minerals. A fungus on food may cause spoilage. Its effect depends on its **environment and its relationship** with nearby organisms.

### Location and Relationships Determine a Fungal Role

Fungi can be helpful, harmful, or neutral depending on context. Yeast helps fermentation. Edible mushrooms become food. Soil fungi help decompose leaves and wood. But some fungi spoil food, attack crops, or cause disease.

Fungi occur in food, soil, roots, and infected tissue. Their effects change with the **location, nutrient source, and nearby organisms**.

### Mycorrhizae between Fungi and Plant Roots

On plant roots, some fungi form [**mycorrhizae**](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/41353), relationships between fungi and roots. The fungi can support water and nutrient uptake, while the plant provides carbohydrates from photosynthesis. Fungal hyphae grow through the soil around the roots and increase the surface area available for water and mineral uptake.

## Fungi Absorb Food after External Digestion

Animals take food into the body and digest it there. Fungi spread hyphae, **break food down outside the body**, and absorb the products. The white layer in tempeh is mycelium, an absorbing network that grows through organic material.

To identify a fungal role, ask three questions: where are the hyphae growing, what material is being broken down, and what relationship is forming with nearby organisms?
