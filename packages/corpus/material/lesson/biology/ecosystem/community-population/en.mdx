## An Ecosystem Is a Community with Its Physical Surroundings

An **ecosystem** is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with the **abiotic components** around them. Organisms of one species form a **population**, all the populations in one area form a **community**, and the community together with its light, water, soil and air forms an ecosystem.

| Term | Meaning | Example in a mangrove swamp |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Species | Organisms that can breed with each other and produce fertile offspring | Mud crab |
| Population | All the organisms of one species in one area at one time | All the mud crabs in that swamp |
| Community | All the populations of different species in the same area | Mangrove trees, crabs, mudskippers, monkeys |
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives | The mud between the mangrove roots |
| Niche | An organism's role: its food, activities and interactions | Crabs eat fallen leaves and break down organic waste |
| Ecosystem | A community together with its abiotic components | The whole mangrove swamp with its salt water and mud |

The basics of energy flow and the roles of producers, consumers and decomposers are covered in [living organisms in an ecosystem](/en/subjects/biology/biodiversity/living-organisms). Here we go further: you calculate the efficiency of energy transfer, build ecological pyramids, run a predator and prey model, pile up mud in a mangrove swamp, and estimate population size with quadrats and the recapture method.

## Biotic and Abiotic Components Decide Who Lives There

Biotic components are grouped by how they get food. **Autotrophs** make their own food. Photoautotrophs such as plants use light, while chemoautotrophs such as nitrifying bacteria use energy from chemical reactions. **Heterotrophs** get food from other organisms in three ways: holozoic, which means swallowing and digesting food as animals do, saprophytic, which means digesting dead organic matter outside the body, and parasitic, which means absorbing food from a living host.

Abiotic components set the limits for every organism:

- **pH**: most plants grow well in soil that's close to neutral, and only certain plants are adapted to acidic peat soil.
- **Temperature**: temperature controls the rate of enzyme reactions, so organisms are only active within a certain range.
- **Light intensity**: light sets the rate of photosynthesis, so the plants on a rainforest floor differ from the plants in the canopy.
- **Humidity**: moist air cuts transpiration and the water that animals such as earthworms lose.
- **Topography**: altitude, gradient and the direction a slope faces change the temperature, light and water that reach an area.
- **Microclimate**: small local conditions under a rock or a canopy, such as a shady, damp spot, let small organisms live there even when the surrounding area is dry.

## Food Chains and Ecological Pyramids

Each feeding step puts an organism on a **trophic level**. Producers are on the first level, primary consumers on the second, and so on. A **food chain** follows one path, for example mangrove leaves to crabs to fish to herons. A **food web** joins many chains that cross, so one organism can sit on more than one trophic level.

Ecology uses three pyramids. A **pyramid of numbers** counts the organisms at each level, a **pyramid of biomass** weighs the dry mass of the organisms, and a **pyramid of energy** measures the energy flowing through each level over a period of time. The first two can be inverted. One large tree supports thousands of caterpillars, so its pyramid of numbers is inverted. In the English Channel, <InlineMath math="4\text{ g m}^{-2}" /> of phytoplankton supports <InlineMath math="21\text{ g m}^{-2}" /> of zooplankton, because the phytoplankton are eaten quickly but reproduce even faster. **A pyramid of energy is always upright**, because energy is lost as heat at every level.

## Six Kinds of Interaction between Organisms

Organisms in a community interact in ways that affect each side differently. The symbol <InlineMath math="+" /> means the organism benefits, <InlineMath math="-" /> means it's harmed and <InlineMath math="0" /> means it's unaffected.

| Interaction | Effect | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mutualism | <InlineMath math="+/+" /> | Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules fix nitrogen, and the plant supplies sugar |
| Commensalism | <InlineMath math="+/0" /> | A bird's nest fern grows on a tree branch to reach light without harming the tree |
| Parasitism | <InlineMath math="+/-" /> | Dodder absorbs water and food from its host plant |
| Saprophytism | Waste is broken down | Fungi and bacteria digest fallen leaves and absorb the products |
| Competition | <InlineMath math="-/-" /> | Rice seedlings compete with each other and with weeds for light and nutrients |
| Predation | <InlineMath math="+/-" /> | Barn owls hunt rats in paddy fields and oil palm plantations |

**Intraspecific competition** happens between organisms of the same species, while **interspecific competition** happens between different species. Both happen when resources such as food, light, water or space are limited.

## Nutrients Cycle through the Ecosystem

Energy flows through an ecosystem once, but elements such as carbon and nitrogen are **used again and again**. These cycles depend on decomposers and on particular bacteria.

In the **carbon cycle**, plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and build glucose. Carbon passes to animals through food. Respiration by every organism, decay by decomposers and the burning of fossil fuels return carbon dioxide to the air. Some carbon stays locked away for a long time in coal, petroleum and peat.

In the **nitrogen cycle**, air holds about <InlineMath math="78\%" /> nitrogen, but plants can't use nitrogen gas directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Rhizobium, and lightning, turn it into nitrogen compounds. Decomposers turn the protein in dead bodies into ammonia. Nitrifying bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite, then into **nitrate that roots absorb**. Denitrifying bacteria turn nitrate back into nitrogen gas in soil that lacks oxygen.

## Colonisation and Succession in a Mangrove Swamp

A mangrove swamp is an ecosystem on a muddy shore that seawater floods at high tide. Its abiotic components are salt water, soft mud that lacks oxygen, and the tides. Its biotic components include mangrove trees, crabs, prawns, mudskippers, snails and monkeys. Mangrove trees have pneumatophores, prop roots and concentrated root cell sap, as described in [plant adaptations in a mangrove swamp](/en/subjects/biology/plant-adaptations/different-habitats).

**Colonisation** is the arrival of pioneer species in a new area. **Succession** is the gradual replacement of one community by another. At the seaward edge, **the pioneer species Avicennia and Sonneratia** grow first. Their roots slow the water, so mud settles and the ground rises. Once the ground is higher and flooded less often, Rhizophora replaces the pioneers. Its prop roots trap even more mud, then Bruguiera takes over, and finally land plants grow where seawater no longer floods.

## How Populations Spread and Grow

A population changes size through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Abiotic factors such as temperature, light, pH and water decide where a population can live. Biotic factors such as food, predators, disease and competition decide how large it can grow.

When resources are plentiful, a population grows fast. As food and space run short, growth slows and the population levels off at the **carrying capacity**, the largest population the environment can support. ## Estimating Population Size

Counting every organism is rarely possible, so ecologists take samples. **The quadrat sampling technique** suits plants and animals that don't move or move slowly. Quadrats are placed at random, and each one is counted or estimated. **The capture, mark, release and recapture technique** suits animals that move freely, such as snails, fish and insects.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Frequency} = \frac{\text{quadrats with the species}}{\text{total quadrats}} \times 100\%" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Density} = \frac{\text{total individuals in all quadrats}}{\text{number of quadrats} \times \text{area of one quadrat}}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Percentage cover} = \frac{\text{total area covered}}{\text{number of quadrats} \times \text{area of one quadrat}} \times 100\%" />
</MathContainer>

### Example of Quadrat Sampling

Ten <InlineMath math="1\text{ m}^2" /> quadrats are placed at random on a field. The numbers of touch-me-not plants in them are <InlineMath math="3, 0, 5, 2, 0, 4, 6, 0, 1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />. Grass covers a total of <InlineMath math="3.2\text{ m}^2" /> across the ten quadrats. **Touch-me-not is present** in <InlineMath math="7" /> quadrats, and its total is <InlineMath math="24" /> individuals:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Frequency of touch-me-not} = \frac{7}{10} \times 100\% = 70\%" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Density of touch-me-not} = \frac{24}{10 \times 1\text{ m}^2} = 2.4\text{ individuals m}^{-2}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Grass cover} = \frac{3.2\text{ m}^2}{10 \times 1\text{ m}^2} \times 100\% = 32\%" />
</MathContainer>

Check the total: <InlineMath math="3 + 5 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 1 + 3 = 24" />, and three quadrats hold zero, so <InlineMath math="10 - 3 = 7" /> quadrats have touch-me-not.

### Example of Recapturing Snails

Researchers catch <InlineMath math="40" /> snails, mark them with paint and release them. A week later, they catch <InlineMath math="50" /> snails and <InlineMath math="8" /> of them are marked. The proportion of marked snails in the second sample is taken to equal their proportion in the whole population:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{8}{50} = \frac{40}{N}" />
<BlockMath math="N = \frac{40 \times 50}{8} = 250" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="40" /> out of <InlineMath math="250" /> is <InlineMath math="16\%" />, and <InlineMath math="8" /> out of <InlineMath math="50" /> is also <InlineMath math="16\%" />. The method assumes the marks don't come off, the marks don't harm the snails, the marked snails mix back into the population, and there are no births, deaths or migration between the two catches.

## Common Mistakes about Ecosystems

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A population and a community are the same thing. | A population is one species, a community includes every species. | Count how many species are named. |
| Every ecological pyramid must be upright. | Pyramids of numbers and biomass can be inverted, only a pyramid of energy is always upright. | Ask what the width of each layer measures. |
| Density is divided by the number of quadrats that hold the species. | Density is divided by all the quadrats, empty ones included. | Make sure the denominator is the total area of all quadrats. |
| In recapture, <InlineMath math="N = \dfrac{n_1 \times m}{n_2}" />. | The marked proportion in the second sample must equal the marked proportion in the population. | Check that <InlineMath math="N" /> is bigger than both catches. |

The third mistake shrinks the denominator, so the density comes out too large. **Empty quadrats are still part of the sample**.

## Practice on Ecosystems

These questions start with naming an interaction and end with a population estimate. **Write the formula before you substitute values**.

1. Rhizobium bacteria live in the root nodules of groundnut plants. Name this interaction and explain how each side benefits.

2. One durian tree supports <InlineMath math="2{,}000" /> caterpillars, and the caterpillars are eaten by <InlineMath math="20" /> birds. Describe the shape of the pyramid of numbers and the pyramid of energy for this food chain.

3. Eight <InlineMath math="0.5\text{ m}^2" /> quadrats hold <InlineMath math="2, 4, 0, 3, 5, 0, 1" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> goosegrass plants. Calculate the frequency and density of goosegrass.

4. Researchers mark <InlineMath math="60" /> fish and release them into a pond. Later they catch <InlineMath math="45" /> fish and <InlineMath math="9" /> of them are marked. Estimate the number of fish, then explain what happens if some of the marks fall off.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: This interaction is **mutualism**. Rhizobium fixes nitrogen from the air into nitrogen compounds that the plant uses to build protein. The plant supplies sugar and shelter to the bacteria. Both sides benefit, <InlineMath math="+/+" />.

**Solution 2**: The pyramid of numbers is **narrow at the base and wide in the middle**: one tree at the base, <InlineMath math="2{,}000" /> caterpillars in the middle and <InlineMath math="20" /> birds at the top. The pyramid of energy stays upright, because one durian tree holds far more energy than all the caterpillars, and energy is lost as heat at every level.

**Solution 3**: Goosegrass is present in <InlineMath math="6" /> of the <InlineMath math="8" /> quadrats, and its total is <InlineMath math="2 + 4 + 3 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 16" /> individuals:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Frequency} = \frac{6}{8} \times 100\% = 75\%" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Density} = \frac{16}{8 \times 0.5\text{ m}^2} = 4\text{ individuals m}^{-2}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the sampled area is <InlineMath math="4\text{ m}^2" />, and <InlineMath math="16" /> individuals in <InlineMath math="4\text{ m}^2" /> gives <InlineMath math="4" /> individuals per square metre.

**Solution 4**: Use the proportion of marked fish:

<BlockMath math="N = \frac{60 \times 45}{9} = 300" />

Check: <InlineMath math="9/45 = 20\%" /> and <InlineMath math="60/300 = 20\%" />. If some marks fall off, fewer marked fish are recaptured than there should be. The value of <InlineMath math="m" /> shrinks, so **the population estimate comes out too large**.
