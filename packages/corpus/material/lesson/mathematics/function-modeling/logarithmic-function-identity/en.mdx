## Understanding Logarithmic Identities

Logarithmic identities turn multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, and powers into coefficients. Those conversions make many logarithmic expressions and equations easier to work with.

Recall that logarithms undo exponentiation. If <InlineMath math="b^x = a" />, then <InlineMath math="\log_b a = x" />. Throughout this lesson, every logarithm has a positive argument, and its base satisfies <InlineMath math="b>0" /> and <InlineMath math="b\neq1" />.

## Basic Logarithmic Identities

### Product Identity

<BlockMath math="\log_b(MN) = {\log_b M} + {\log_b N}" />

The logarithm of a product equals the sum of the logarithms of each number.

**Example:**

<BlockMath math="\log_2(8 \times 4) = {\log_2 8} + {\log_2 4} = 3 + 2 = 5" />

### Quotient Identity

<BlockMath math="\log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = {\log_b M} - {\log_b N}" />

The logarithm of a quotient equals the difference between the logarithm of the numerator and the logarithm of the denominator.

**Example:**

<BlockMath math="\log_3\left(\frac{81}{9}\right) = {\log_3 81} - {\log_3 9} = 4 - 2 = 2" />

### Power Identity

<BlockMath math="\log_b(M^p) = p \cdot {\log_b M}" />

The logarithm of a number raised to a power equals the power multiplied by the logarithm of that number.

**Example:**

<BlockMath math="\log_2(4^3) = 3 \cdot {\log_2 4} = 3 \times 2 = 6" />

## Special Logarithmic Identities

### Change of Base

<BlockMath math="\log_b M = \frac{\log_a M}{\log_a b}" />

This identity allows us to change the logarithm base as needed.

**Example:**

Because <InlineMath math="9=3^2" />, the numerator is exactly twice the denominator:

<BlockMath math="\log_3 9 = \frac{\log_{10} 9}{\log_{10} 3} = \frac{2\log_{10}3}{\log_{10}3} = 2" />

### Equality Identity

If <InlineMath math="\log_b M = {\log_b N}" />, then <InlineMath math="M = N" />

Two numbers that have the same logarithmic value (with the same base) must be the same number.

### Inequality Identity

- If <InlineMath math="b > 1" /> and <InlineMath math="\log_b M < {\log_b N}" />, then <InlineMath math="M < N" />
- If <InlineMath math="0 < b < 1" /> and <InlineMath math="\log_b M < {\log_b N}" />, then <InlineMath math="M > N" />

## Applications of Logarithmic Identities

### Simplifying Expressions

Simplify <InlineMath math="\log_2 32 + {\log_2 8} - {\log_2 4}" />

**Solution:**

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_2 32 + {\log_2 8} - {\log_2 4}" />
  <BlockMath math="= {\log_2(32 \times 8)} - {\log_2 4}" />
  <BlockMath math="= {\log_2 256} - {\log_2 4}" />
  <BlockMath math="= {\log_2\left(\frac{256}{4}\right)}" />
  <BlockMath math="= {\log_2 64} = 6" />
</MathContainer>

### Solving Equations

Find the value of <InlineMath math="x" /> if <InlineMath math="\log_3 x + {\log_3 9} = {\log_3 81}" />

**Solution:**

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_3 x + {\log_3 9} = {\log_3 81}" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_3(x \times 9) = {\log_3 81}" />
  <BlockMath math="x \times 9 = 81" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{81}{9} = 9" />
</MathContainer>

## Applications in Models

### A Logarithmic Magnitude Scale

A simplified magnitude scale can compare a measured seismic-wave amplitude <InlineMath math="A" /> with a reference amplitude <InlineMath math="A_0" />:

<BlockMath math="M = \log\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right)" />

Where:

- <InlineMath math="M" /> is the magnitude in this simplified model
- <InlineMath math="A" /> is the measured amplitude
- <InlineMath math="A_0" /> is the reference amplitude

**Example:** Suppose a measured amplitude is <InlineMath math="10^7" /> times the reference amplitude. What magnitude does the simplified model give?

**Solution:**

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="M = \log\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="= \log\left(\frac{10^7 A_0}{A_0}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="= \log(10^7)" />
  <BlockMath math="= 7" />
</MathContainer>

The amplitude ratio therefore corresponds to magnitude <InlineMath math="7" /> in this simplified model. Actual earthquake-magnitude calculations also account for the instrument, wave type, and source distance.

### Battery Charging

In an idealized first-order charging model, the time needed to reach a fraction of the maximum capacity is:

<BlockMath math="t = -\frac{1}{k}\ln\left(1 - \frac{C}{C_0}\right)" />

Where:

- <InlineMath math="t" /> = charging time (in minutes)
- <InlineMath math="k" /> = charging constant
- <InlineMath math="C" /> = desired capacity
- <InlineMath math="C_0" /> = maximum capacity

**Example:** Determine the time required to charge a battery from empty to <InlineMath math="90\%" /> full. Assume <InlineMath math="k = 0.02" />.

**Solution:**

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t = -\frac{1}{k}\ln\left(1 - \frac{C}{C_0}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="= -\frac{1}{0.02}\ln\left(1 - \frac{0.9C_0}{C_0}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="= -50\ln(1 - 0.9)" />
  <BlockMath math="= -50\ln(0.1)" />
  <BlockMath math="\approx 115.13" />
</MathContainer>

Within this idealized model, reaching <InlineMath math="90\%" /> takes approximately <InlineMath math="115 \text{ minutes}" />. Real charging systems can change their rate near full capacity.

### Car Price Depreciation

If the instantaneous depreciation rate is proportional to a car's current value, an exponential model has the form:

<BlockMath math="H(t) = ce^{kt}" />

where <InlineMath math="H(t)" /> is the value at time <InlineMath math="t" />.

**Example:** A new car costs <InlineMath math="200 \text{ million rupiah}" /> and its modeled value after <InlineMath math="5 \text{ years}" /> is <InlineMath math="100 \text{ million rupiah}" />. Assuming the same exponential depreciation rate continues, determine the modeled value after <InlineMath math="10 \text{ years}" />.

**Solution:**

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="H(0) = 200 \text{ million, so} 200 = ce^0 = c" />
  <BlockMath math="H(5) = 100 \text{ million, so} 100 = 200e^{5k}" />
  <BlockMath math="e^{5k} = \frac{1}{2} \text{, therefore } 5k = \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="k = \frac{1}{5}\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \approx -0.1386" />
</MathContainer>

From these results, the car's price at any time <InlineMath math="t" /> is:

<BlockMath math="H(t) = 200e^{\frac{\ln(1/2)}{5}t} \approx 200e^{-0.1386t}" />

Therefore, the car's price after <InlineMath math="10 \text{ years}" /> of use is:

<BlockMath math="H(10) = 200e^{2\ln(1/2)} = 200\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 50 \text{ million rupiah}" />

## Exercises

**Problem** <InlineMath math="1" />

Simplify: <InlineMath math="\log_5 125 + {\log_5 25} - {\log_5 5}" />

**Problem** <InlineMath math="2" />

If <InlineMath math="\log_2 x = 3" /> and <InlineMath math="\log_2 y = 5" />, find the value of <InlineMath math="\log_2(xy)" />

**Problem** <InlineMath math="3" />

Find the value of <InlineMath math="x" /> if <InlineMath math="\log_4(x-1) = {\log_4 16} - {\log_4 2}" />

### Worked Solutions

**Answer** <InlineMath math="1" />

Each number is a power of <InlineMath math="5" />. Evaluate the three logarithms before combining them:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="125=5^3,\quad25=5^2,\quad5=5^1" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_5 125 + {\log_5 25} - {\log_5 5}=3+2-1=4" />
</MathContainer>

The simplified value is <InlineMath math="4" />.

**Answer** <InlineMath math="2" />

Use the product identity because the target contains <InlineMath math="xy" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_2(xy) = {\log_2 x} + {\log_2 y}" />
  <BlockMath math="= 3 + 5 = 8" />
</MathContainer>

Thus, <InlineMath math="\log_2(xy)=8" />.

**Answer** <InlineMath math="3" />

Use the quotient identity on the right-hand side, then use equality of logarithms with the same base:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_4(x-1) = {\log_4 16} - {\log_4 2}" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_4(x-1) = {\log_4\left(\frac{16}{2}\right)}" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_4(x-1) = {\log_4 8}" />
  <BlockMath math="x - 1 = 8" />
  <BlockMath math="x = 9" />
</MathContainer>

The logarithm requires <InlineMath math="x-1>0" />. Since <InlineMath math="9-1=8>0" />, the solution is valid.
