## Repeating Motion and Angle Measurement

Motion that **repeats regularly** can be modeled with sine or cosine. Such a model describes an ideal wave, rather than every change in ocean water.

Before studying trigonometric function graphs, we need to understand angle measurement in radians. In daily life, we are accustomed to using degrees. However, in advanced mathematics, radians are more frequently used.

## Converting Degrees and Radians

**One complete rotation** of a circle is <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> or <InlineMath math="2\pi" /> radians. This relationship gives us conversion formulas:

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians}" />

<BlockMath math="1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180} \text{ radians}" />

<BlockMath math="1 \text{ radian} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

### Conversion Examples

Converting **degrees to radians**:

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="90^\circ = 90 \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ radians}" />

<BlockMath math="60^\circ = 60 \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{3} \text{ radians}" />

<BlockMath math="45^\circ = 45 \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{4} \text{ radians}" />
</MathContainer>

Converting radians to degrees:

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="\frac{\pi}{6} \text{ radians} = \frac{\pi}{6} \times \frac{180}{\pi} = 30^\circ" />

<BlockMath math="\frac{3\pi}{4} \text{ radians} = \frac{3\pi}{4} \times \frac{180}{\pi} = 135^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

## Amplitude and Period of a Sine Graph

Two quantities describe the basic shape of a trigonometric graph: **amplitude** and **period**. The amplitude tells you how far the curve rises above and falls below its middle line, and the period tells you how much horizontal distance one full repetition needs.

### Amplitude

Amplitude is <Highlight>the maximum vertical distance from a sinusoid's midline</Highlight> to a peak or trough. In the unshifted functions <InlineMath math="y = A \sin x" /> and <InlineMath math="y = A \cos x" />, that midline is the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and the amplitude is <InlineMath math="|A|" />.

### Amplitude and Period from the Coefficients

For the formulas below, take <InlineMath math="A\neq0" /> and <InlineMath math="B\neq0" />. Amplitude uses the **absolute value**, while the fundamental period is the smallest positive repeat distance. If either coefficient is zero in sine or cosine, the function is constant and has no smallest positive period.

| Function | Amplitude | Period |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="A\sin(Bx)" /> | <InlineMath math="\lvert A\rvert" /> | <InlineMath math="2\pi/\lvert B\rvert" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A\cos(Bx)" /> | <InlineMath math="\lvert A\rvert" /> | <InlineMath math="2\pi/\lvert B\rvert" /> |
| <InlineMath math="A\tan(Bx)" /> | Undefined | <InlineMath math="\pi/\lvert B\rvert" /> |

### Angle Conversion

Convert units using the appropriate **conversion factor**.

- <InlineMath math="120^\circ\cdot\frac{\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}{180^\circ}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\,\mathrm{rad}" />
- <InlineMath math="\frac{5\pi}{6}\,\mathrm{rad}\cdot\frac{180^\circ}{\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}=150^\circ" />

One full turn is three hundred sixty degrees or two pi radians. Their ratio equals one, so multiplying by the conversion factor changes the unit while preserving the angle.

### Amplitude and Period

Use the **absolute value of the coefficient** for amplitude. The negative sign reflects the cosine curve. It does not make its amplitude negative.

- <InlineMath math="y = 3 \sin(2x)" />: amplitude <InlineMath math="3" />, period <InlineMath math="\frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi" />
- <InlineMath math="y = -2 \cos(\frac{x}{3})" />: amplitude <InlineMath math="2" />, period <InlineMath math="\frac{2\pi}{1/3} = 6\pi" />

For the second curve, the input coefficient is one third. The cosine argument needs an input increase of six pi to advance by two pi, so its period is three times the basic cosine period.

<BlockMath math="\frac{T}{3}=2\pi \quad\Rightarrow\quad T=6\pi" />

### A Tidal Model

The water level oscillates about a **midline** of five meters, with a maximum displacement of two meters.

- Maximum height: <InlineMath math="5 + 2 = 7 \text{ meters}" />
- Minimum height: <InlineMath math="5 - 2 = 3 \text{ meters}" />
- Period: <InlineMath math="\frac{2\pi}{\pi/6} = 12 \text{ hours}" />

Sine lies between negative one and one, so this model always gives heights between three and seven meters. A period is the time required for the sine argument to increase by two pi.

<BlockMath math="\frac{\pi}{6}T=2\pi \quad\Rightarrow\quad T=12" />

### Building the Equation

Find the frequency coefficient from the **given period**, then place the horizontal shift inside the parentheses.

<BlockMath math="2\pi/B=\pi\;\Rightarrow\;B=2" />

<BlockMath math="y = 3 \sin(2(x - \frac{\pi}{4})) + 2" />

Expanding the argument gives the equivalent form:

<BlockMath math="y = 3 \sin(2x - \frac{\pi}{2}) + 2" />
