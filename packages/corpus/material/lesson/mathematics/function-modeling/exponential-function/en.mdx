## A Variable in the Exponent

An exponential function has <Highlight>its independent variable in the exponent</Highlight>. The growth and decay models below use the general form <InlineMath math="f(x) = a \cdot b^x" /> with <InlineMath math="a > 0" />, <InlineMath math="b > 0" />, and <InlineMath math="b \neq 1" />.

**Components of exponential functions**:

In the function <InlineMath math="f(x) = a \cdot b^x" />:

- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the multiplier constant that determines the initial
  value of the function
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the exponential base that determines the rate of growth
  or decay
- <InlineMath math="x" /> is the independent variable (exponent)

## Characteristics of Exponential Functions

With a positive initial value, the **base determines the direction of change**. At input zero, the function equals its initial multiplier.

Increasing the input by one always multiplies the output by the same base. This distinguishes exponential change from adding a fixed amount in a linear function.

<BlockMath math="\frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=\frac{ab^{x+1}}{ab^x}=b" />

### How the Graph Behaves

The exponent rules give the following identities. Multiplying two function values includes the **initial multiplier** twice. We must therefore divide that product by the initial multiplier to obtain the value at the sum of the inputs.

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="f(0) = a \cdot b^0 = a \cdot 1 = a" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(x_1 + x_2) &amp;= a \cdot b^{x_1 + x_2} \\ &amp;= a \cdot b^{x_1} \cdot b^{x_2} \end{aligned}" />

<BlockMath math="f(x_1 - x_2) = a \cdot b^{x_1 - x_2} = \frac{a \cdot b^{x_1}}{b^{x_2}}" />

<BlockMath math="f(x_1+x_2)=\frac{f(x_1)f(x_2)}{a},\quad f(x_1-x_2)=\frac{a f(x_1)}{f(x_2)}" />
</MathContainer>

### Types of Exponential Functions

| Type | Base | Change | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Growth** | <InlineMath math="b>1" /> | Increasing | <InlineMath math="2^x" /> |
| **Decay** | <InlineMath math="0<b<1" /> | Decreasing | <InlineMath math="(1/2)^x" /> |

## Transformations of Exponential Functions

A **vertical shift** moves the asymptote, while a horizontal shift moves a reference point on the graph. Each constant added to the function shifts the graph in one fixed direction, and the transformed equation shows which direction that is.

### Vertical Translation

The function <InlineMath math="f(x) = a \cdot b^x + c" /> shifts the graph **upward** (if <InlineMath math="c > 0" />) or downward (if <InlineMath math="c < 0" />).

### Horizontal Translation

The function <InlineMath math="f(x) = a \cdot b^{x-h}" /> shifts the graph to the right (if <InlineMath math="h > 0" />) or to the left (if <InlineMath math="h < 0" />). The shift moves every point by the same amount, so the asymptote stays where it is and the shape of the curve does not change.

For a **shift two units right**, add two to each original input while preserving its output.

<BlockMath math="g(x)=2^{x-2},\qquad g(2)=2^0=1" />

| Original point | New point |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="(0,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,1)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="(1,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,2)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="(2,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,4)" /> |

## Applications of Exponential Functions

The quantity in each example below changes by a **constant factor** over equal intervals. That factor represents population growth, decay, or an increase in an account balance due to interest.

The same percentage is applied to the latest amount, so the absolute increase is not constant. Ten percent growth changes one hundred to one hundred ten, then to one hundred twenty-one.

<BlockMath math="100\cdot1.1=110,\qquad110\cdot1.1=121" />

### Population Growth

When a population has a constant proportional growth rate and its conditions do not change, an exponential model can describe its growth. If the initial population is <InlineMath math="P_0" /> and the growth rate is <InlineMath math="r" /> per time period, then:

<BlockMath math="P(t) = P_0 \cdot (1 + r)^t" />

**Example**: Bacterial population that reproduces every hour at a rate of <InlineMath math="20\%" />:

- Initial population: <InlineMath math="1000 \text{ bacteria}" />
- Growth rate: <InlineMath math="r = 0.2" />
- Function: <InlineMath math="P(t) = 1000 \cdot (1.2)^t" />

| Time (hours) | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| ------------ | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| Population   | <InlineMath math="1000" /> | <InlineMath math="1200" /> | <InlineMath math="1440" /> | <InlineMath math="1728" /> | <InlineMath math="2074" /> | <InlineMath math="2488" /> |

The model gives <InlineMath math="2073.6" /> and <InlineMath math="2488.32" /> at hours <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="5" />. The table rounds those populations to the nearest whole bacterium.

### Radioactive Decay

Radioactive substances decay following exponential functions. If the initial mass is <InlineMath math="M_0" /> and the half-life is <InlineMath math="t_{1/2}" />, then:

<BlockMath math="M(t) = M_0 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}" />

For example, an initial mass of eighty grams with a **two-year half-life** halves every two years.

<BlockMath math="M(4)=80\left(\frac12\right)^{4/2}=80\cdot\frac14=20" />

| Time (years) | Mass (grams) |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="80" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="40" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="20" /> |

### Compound Interest

Investments with compound interest grow exponentially. If the initial capital is <InlineMath math="P" />, interest rate is <InlineMath math="r" /> per year, and time is <InlineMath math="t \text{ years}" />:

<BlockMath math="A = P \cdot \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}" />

In this formula, <InlineMath math="n" /> is the number of times interest is compounded per year.

Suppose an initial one million rupiah earns a nominal annual rate of ten percent, **compounded twice a year**. This model has no deposits, withdrawals, fees, or taxes.

<BlockMath math="A=1{,}000{,}000\left(1+\frac{0.10}{2}\right)^{2\cdot1}=1{,}000{,}000(1.05)^2=1{,}102{,}500" />

Every half-year, the balance is multiplied by the same factor. The model gives an end-of-year balance of Rp1,102,500.

## Exponential Equations

An exponential equation is an equation that contains a variable in the exponent. General form:

<BlockMath math="b^x = c" />

**Method 1: Equalizing Bases**

If <InlineMath math="b^{f(x)} = b^{g(x)}" />, then <InlineMath math="f(x) = g(x)" />

**Example**: Solve <InlineMath math="2^{x+1} = 8" />

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="2^{x+1} = 8" />

<BlockMath math="2^{x+1} = 2^3" />

<BlockMath math="x + 1 = 3" />

<BlockMath math="x = 2" />
</MathContainer>

**Method 2: Using Logarithms**

For <InlineMath math="b>0" />, <InlineMath math="b\neq1" />, and <InlineMath math="c>0" />, solve <InlineMath math="b^x = c" /> with logarithms:

<BlockMath math="x = \log_b c = \frac{\log c}{\log b}" />

## Exercises

Each problem gives information about an exponential function and asks for the function itself or for one of its values, so write the general form first and substitute the given point.

1. Determine the value of <InlineMath math="f(3)" /> if <InlineMath math="f(x) = 5 \cdot 2^x" />

2. Solve the equation <InlineMath math="3^{2x-1} = 27" />

3. A city's population is <InlineMath math="50{,}000 \text{ people}" /> and grows <InlineMath math="3\%" /> per year. What will the population be after <InlineMath math="10 \text{ years}" />?

4. A radioactive substance has a **half-life** of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ years}" />. If the initial mass is <InlineMath math="100 \text{ grams}" />, how much mass remains after <InlineMath math="15 \text{ years}" />?

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. Substitute <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> into <InlineMath math="f(x) = 5 \cdot 2^x" />. Evaluate the **exponent before multiplying** by the initial factor.

<BlockMath math="f(3) = 5 \cdot 2^3 = 5 \cdot 8 = 40" />

<InlineMath math="f(3) = 40" />.

**Problem 2**. Rewrite <InlineMath math="27" /> as <InlineMath math="3^3" />. Because base three is positive and not one, equal powers have **equal exponents**.

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="3^{2x-1} = 3^3" />

<BlockMath math="2x - 1 = 3" />

<BlockMath math="x = 2" />
</MathContainer>

Checking gives <InlineMath math="3^{2(2)-1} = 3^3 = 27" />, so the solution is correct.

**Problem 3**. The initial population is <InlineMath math="P_0 = 50{,}000" />, the annual **growth rate** is <InlineMath math="r = 0.03" />, and the time is <InlineMath math="t = 10" /> years. Use the annual growth factor <InlineMath math="1 + r = 1.03" />.

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="P(10) = 50{,}000 \cdot (1.03)^{10}" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} P(10) &amp;\approx 50{,}000 \cdot 1.343916 \\ &amp;\approx 67{,}195.82 \end{aligned}" />

<BlockMath math="P(10) \approx 67{,}196 \text{ people}" />
</MathContainer>

The population is rounded to the nearest whole person.

**Problem 4**. Fifteen years contain <InlineMath math="15/5 = 3" /> **half-life** periods. The mass is therefore multiplied by <InlineMath math="1/2" /> three times.

<MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="M(15) = 100 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3" />

<BlockMath math="M(15) = 100 \cdot \frac{1}{8} = 12.5 \text{ grams}" />
</MathContainer>

After <InlineMath math="15 \text{ years}" />, <InlineMath math="12.5 \text{ grams}" /> remains.
