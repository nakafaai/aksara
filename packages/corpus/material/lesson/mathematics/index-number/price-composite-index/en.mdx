## An Index Compares Two Times

An **index number** measures the change in a quantity, such as a price, against its value in the **base year**:

<BlockMath math="I = \frac{Q_1}{Q_0} \times 100" />

Here <InlineMath math="Q_0" /> is the value in the base year and <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> is the value in the year being studied. An index of <InlineMath math="100" /> means no change, an index of <InlineMath math="125" /> means a rise of <InlineMath math="25\%" />, and an index of <InlineMath math="90" /> means a fall of <InlineMath math="10\%" />. For several items, a **composite index** combines the index of each item by its weight.

In these notes you change the current price and watch the index move, compare the indices of several items, adjust the weights in a composite index, and join two indices to change the base year. Every price in these notes is a sample price for practice.

## The Price Index of One Item

A <InlineMath math="10\text{ kg}" /> bag of rice costs **RM26.00** in <InlineMath math="2020" /> and **RM32.50** in <InlineMath math="2024" />. With <InlineMath math="2020" /> as the base year:

<BlockMath math="I_{2024/2020} = \frac{32.50}{26.00} \times 100 = 125" />

The price of rice rose by <InlineMath math="25\%" /> over that time. Change the current price below. The index changes linearly, because the base-year price is fixed.

## The Weighted Composite Index

The **composite index** <InlineMath math="\bar{I}" /> is the weighted mean of the indices of all the items:

<BlockMath math="\bar{I} = \frac{\sum I_i w_i}{\sum w_i}" />

For the four items above:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sum I_i w_i = 125(4) + 140(3) + 110(2) + 120(1) = 1260" />
<BlockMath math="\bar{I} = \frac{1260}{4 + 3 + 2 + 1} = 126.0" />
</MathContainer>

The total cost of this basket rose by <InlineMath math="26\%" />. If a family spent <InlineMath math="\text{RM}800" /> on these items in <InlineMath math="2020" />, the same shopping in <InlineMath math="2024" /> costs <InlineMath math="800 \times \dfrac{126}{100} = \text{RM}1008" />.

The weights change the composite index. Adjust the weight of mackerel, the item that rose the most, and watch the composite index move towards <InlineMath math="140" />.

## Chained Indices and a New Base Year

If you know the index from year <InlineMath math="0" /> to year <InlineMath math="1" />, and from year <InlineMath math="1" /> to year <InlineMath math="2" />, join them by **multiplying**:

<BlockMath math="I_{2/0} = \frac{I_{2/1} \times I_{1/0}}{100}" />

For example, the price index of a bus fare is <InlineMath math="115" /> for <InlineMath math="2022" /> based on <InlineMath math="2020" />, and <InlineMath math="110" /> for <InlineMath math="2024" /> based on <InlineMath math="2022" />. Then <InlineMath math="I_{2024/2020} = \dfrac{110 \times 115}{100} = 126.5" />, not <InlineMath math="115 + 10 = 125" />. Change the two indices below.

## Common Mistakes with Index Numbers

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Working out <InlineMath math="\dfrac{Q_0}{Q_1} \times 100" />. | The base-year value must be in the denominator. | A price that rose must give an index above <InlineMath math="100" />. |
| Averaging the indices without weights. | A more important item must have a larger effect. | Multiply each index by its weight first. |
| Adding chained indices, such as <InlineMath math="115 + 110" />. | Chained indices are multiplied, then divided by <InlineMath math="100" />. | The overall index must be near <InlineMath math="126.5" />, not <InlineMath math="225" />. |
| Reading an index of <InlineMath math="126" /> as a price of RM126. | An index is a ratio without a unit. | An index only means something next to <InlineMath math="100" />. |
| Forgetting to multiply by <InlineMath math="100" />. | Indices are written on a base of <InlineMath math="100" />, not <InlineMath math="1" />. | The base year must give an index of <InlineMath math="100" />. |

The third mistake is the most common. **Chained indices are always multiplied**, because each rise is worked out on a value that has already changed.

## Index Numbers Practice

Write the base year of each index before you calculate so that **the numerator and denominator do not swap**.

1. A book costs RM40.00 in <InlineMath math="2021" /> and RM46.00 in <InlineMath math="2024" />. Work out the price index of the book in <InlineMath math="2024" /> based on <InlineMath math="2021" />.

2. The price index of an item in <InlineMath math="2024" /> based on <InlineMath math="2020" /> is <InlineMath math="130" />. Its price in <InlineMath math="2024" /> is RM19.50. Find its price in <InlineMath math="2020" />.

3. Three items have indices <InlineMath math="120" />, <InlineMath math="105" /> and <InlineMath math="140" />, with weights <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />. Work out the composite index.

4. Given <InlineMath math="I_{2021/2019} = 125" /> and <InlineMath math="I_{2023/2021} = 108" />, work out <InlineMath math="I_{2023/2019}" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The base year is <InlineMath math="2021" />, so the price of RM40.00 goes in the denominator:

<BlockMath math="I = \frac{46.00}{40.00} \times 100 = 115" />

The price of the book rose by <InlineMath math="15\%" />.

**Solution 2**: Rearrange the index formula for <InlineMath math="Q_0" />:

<BlockMath math="Q_0 = \frac{Q_1 \times 100}{I} = \frac{19.50 \times 100}{130} = 15.00" />

Its price in <InlineMath math="2020" /> was RM15.00. **Check**: <InlineMath math="\dfrac{19.50}{15.00} \times 100 = 130" />.

**Solution 3**: Use the weighted mean:

<BlockMath math="\bar{I} = \frac{120(5) + 105(3) + 140(2)}{5 + 3 + 2} = \frac{600 + 315 + 280}{10} = 119.5" />

**Solution 4**: Multiply the two indices, then divide by <InlineMath math="100" />:

<BlockMath math="I_{2023/2019} = \frac{108 \times 125}{100} = 135" />

The price rose by <InlineMath math="35\%" /> from <InlineMath math="2019" /> to <InlineMath math="2023" />.
