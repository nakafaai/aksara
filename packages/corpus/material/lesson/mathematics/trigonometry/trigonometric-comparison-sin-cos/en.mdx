## What is the Sine Ratio?

Sine of an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> in a right triangle is the ratio between the length of the opposite side and the hypotenuse.

## A Right-Triangle Model of a Pyramid

Consider a central cross-section of a regular pyramid through its apex and the midpoint of one base edge. One half of this cross-section is a right triangle. Its vertical leg is the pyramid height, its horizontal leg is half the base width, and its hypotenuse is the slant height from the base-edge midpoint to the apex.

### Using Sine to Calculate Pyramid Height

Suppose the slant height is <InlineMath math="600 \text{ m}" /> and makes an angle of <InlineMath math="41^\circ" /> with the base. The pyramid height is opposite that angle.

<Triangle
  title="Calculating Pyramid Height with Sine"
  description="Central cross-section through the apex and the midpoint of a base edge."
  angle={41}
  labels={{
    opposite: "Pyramid Height",
    adjacent: "Half of Base Width",
    hypotenuse: "Slant Height",
  }}
/>

To calculate the height of the pyramid, we use the sine ratio:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sin 41^\circ = \frac{\text{pyramid height}}{\text{slant height}}" />
  <BlockMath math="\sin 41^\circ = \frac{x}{600}" />
  <BlockMath math="\sin 41^\circ \approx 0.65606" />
  <BlockMath math="x = 600\sin 41^\circ \text{ m} \approx 393.6 \text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

The modeled pyramid height is approximately <InlineMath math="393.6 \text{ m}" />.

### Using Cosine to Calculate Half the Base Width

The horizontal leg is adjacent to the <InlineMath math="41^\circ" /> angle, so cosine gives half the base width:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\cos 41^\circ = \frac{\text{half of base width}}{\text{slant height}}" />
  <BlockMath math="\cos 41^\circ = \frac{y}{600}" />
  <BlockMath math="\cos 41^\circ \approx 0.75471" />
  <BlockMath math="y = 600\cos 41^\circ \text{ m} \approx 452.8 \text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Therefore, the full base width in this cross-section is approximately <InlineMath math="2y = 905.7 \text{ m}" />.

## Differences and Similarities Between Sin, Cos, and Tan

### Differences

1. **Sine** <InlineMath math="\sin \theta" /> compares the opposite side with the hypotenuse.
2. **Cosine** <InlineMath math="\cos \theta" /> compares the adjacent side with the hypotenuse.
3. **Tangent** <InlineMath math="\tan \theta" /> compares the opposite side with the adjacent side.

### Similarities

1. All three are trigonometric ratios in right triangles.
2. All three change their values according to the angle.
3. These three ratios have a mathematical relationship:

   <BlockMath math="\tan \theta = \frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta},\qquad \cos \theta \ne 0" />

## Practice Problem

A child is flying a kite at a height of <InlineMath math="3.5 \text{ m}" /> above level ground and holds the end of the taut, straight string <InlineMath math="60 \text{ cm}" /> above the ground. If the string forms an angle of <InlineMath math="25^\circ" /> with the horizontal, what is its length?

<Triangle
  title="Kite Problem"
  description="Visualization of the kite string length problem."
  angle={25}
  labels={{
    opposite: "Kite Height",
    adjacent: "Horizontal Distance",
    hypotenuse: "String Length",
  }}
/>

To solve this problem, which trigonometric ratio should we use?

**Correct Solution:**

1. We need to calculate the string length (hypotenuse)
2. We know the effective height of the kite, which is <InlineMath math="3.5 \text{ m} - 0.6 \text{ m} = 2.9 \text{ m}" />.
3. We know the elevation angle <InlineMath math="25^\circ" />.
4. Since we're looking for the hypotenuse and we know the opposite side (effective height), we use the sine ratio:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="\sin 25^\circ = \frac{\text{effective height}}{\text{string length}}" />
     <BlockMath math="\sin 25^\circ = \frac{2.9}{x}" />
     <BlockMath math="\sin 25^\circ \approx 0.42262" />
     <BlockMath math="x = \frac{2.9}{\sin 25^\circ}\text{ m} \approx 6.86 \text{ m}" />
   </MathContainer>

Rounded to the nearest tenth of a metre, the string is approximately <InlineMath math="6.9 \text{ m}" /> long.
