## What is Trigonometry?

The word trigonometry is built from Greek words for triangle and measure. Right triangles provide a natural starting point because their side ratios are determined by their angles. Trigonometry later extends these ideas to the unit circle, periodic functions, and many settings beyond triangles.

### Recalling the Pythagorean Theorem

For a right triangle with legs <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> and hypotenuse <InlineMath math="c" />, the Pythagorean theorem states:

<BlockMath math="a^2 + b^2 = c^2" />

The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle. This theorem connects all three side lengths and supports the special-triangle values used later.

### Understanding Ratios and Proportions

Trigonometric functions begin as ratios of side lengths. A ratio compares two quantities, while a proportion states that two ratios are equal.

Suppose a vertical ruler and a vertical tree stand on the same level ground and their shadows are measured at the same time. Parallel sunlight makes the two height-shadow triangles similar. If the ruler is <InlineMath math="6 \text{ cm}" /> tall, its shadow is <InlineMath math="3 \text{ cm}" />, and the tree's shadow is <InlineMath math="150 \text{ cm}" />, let the tree height be <InlineMath math="h" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{\text{ruler height}}{\text{ruler shadow}} = \frac{\text{tree height}}{\text{tree shadow}}" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{6}{3}=\frac{h}{150}" />
  <BlockMath math="h=\frac{6}{3}\cdot150\text{ cm}=300\text{ cm}=3\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

The proportion works because the two triangles share the same two acute angles, not merely because both objects cast shadows.

## Similar Triangles

Similar triangles have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides. Any one of these standard tests is sufficient to prove similarity:

| Test | Sufficient information |
| --- | --- |
| AA | Two pairs of corresponding angles are equal. The third pair is then equal automatically. |
| SSS | All three pairs of corresponding sides are proportional. |
| SAS | Two pairs of corresponding sides are proportional and their included angles are equal. |

Similar triangles have the same shape even when their sizes differ. Every corresponding length is multiplied by the same scale factor.

### Applications of Similar Triangles

The concept of similar triangles is widely used in everyday life:

1. **Height measurement**: Shadows or sight lines can form similar triangles for objects that are difficult to measure directly.

2. **Scale drawings**: A plan or model preserves proportions while every length is enlarged or reduced by one scale factor.

3. **Geometry and construction**: Indirect measurements can be checked through corresponding angles and proportional sides.

## Trigonometric Ratios

Trigonometric ratios compare the sides of a right triangle relative to a chosen acute angle.

Imagine a right triangle with an angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> (theta). We name its sides as:

- **Hypotenuse**: the side opposite to the right angle
- **Opposite side**: the side opposite to angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />.
- **Adjacent side**: the side adjacent to angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> other than the hypotenuse.

From these three sides, we obtain six basic trigonometric ratios. For an acute angle in a nondegenerate right triangle, all three side lengths are positive, so every ratio below is defined:

1. **Sine** (<InlineMath math="\sin" />):

   <BlockMath math="\sin \theta = \frac{\text{opposite side}}{\text{hypotenuse}}" />

2. **Cosine** (<InlineMath math="\cos" />):

   <BlockMath math="\cos \theta = \frac{\text{adjacent side}}{\text{hypotenuse}}" />

3. **Tangent** (<InlineMath math="\tan" />):

   <BlockMath math="\tan \theta = \frac{\text{opposite side}}{\text{adjacent side}}" />

4. **Cosecant** (<InlineMath math="\csc" />):

   <BlockMath math="\csc \theta = \frac{1}{\sin \theta} = \frac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{opposite side}}" />

5. **Secant** (<InlineMath math="\sec" />):

   <BlockMath math="\sec \theta = \frac{1}{\cos \theta} = \frac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{adjacent side}}" />

6. **Cotangent** (<InlineMath math="\cot" />):

   <BlockMath math="\cot \theta = \frac{1}{\tan \theta} = \frac{\text{adjacent side}}{\text{opposite side}}" />

### Why Does Each Angle Have Fixed Ratios?

Two right triangles with the same acute angle, for example <InlineMath math="\theta=30^\circ" />, also share the right angle. The AA test therefore proves that they are similar. Scaling every side by the same factor leaves each quotient of corresponding side lengths unchanged.

Simple example:

- Triangle <InlineMath math="A" /> has an opposite side of <InlineMath math="3 \text{ cm}" /> and a hypotenuse of <InlineMath math="6 \text{ cm}" />.
- Triangle <InlineMath math="B" /> has an opposite side of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ cm}" /> and a hypotenuse of <InlineMath math="10 \text{ cm}" />.

If we calculate <InlineMath math="\sin \theta" /> for both triangles:

- <InlineMath math="\sin \theta" /> for triangle <InlineMath math="A" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{6} = 0.5" />.
- <InlineMath math="\sin \theta" /> for triangle <InlineMath math="B" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{5}{10} = 0.5" />.

Although the triangles have different sizes, <InlineMath math="\sin \theta" /> has the same value. The same reasoning applies to the other side ratios.

On the unit circle, the point corresponding to <InlineMath math="\theta" /> has coordinates <InlineMath math="(\cos \theta,\sin \theta)" />. This definition extends sine and cosine beyond the acute angles of a right triangle.

The right-triangle ratios and the unit-circle coordinates agree for acute angles, so the same values can be used in problems with triangles of different sizes.

## Applications of Trigonometry in Real Life

Trigonometry connects angles with lengths and periodic change, so it appears in measurement, navigation, construction, and wave models.

### Measuring Height Using Shadows

For nearby vertical objects on level ground, height divided by shadow length is the same when the shadows are measured at the same time. This follows from parallel sunlight and similar triangles.

More than <InlineMath math="2200 \text{ years}" /> ago, Eratosthenes estimated Earth's circumference by comparing the Sun's angle at two locations and combining the angular difference with the distance between those locations. The distance was essential; the shadows alone were not enough.

### Navigation

Ancient sailors used trigonometry for navigation by measuring the angle between the horizon and stars.

### Architecture and Construction

Architects use trigonometry to calculate roof slopes, tower heights, and many structural aspects of buildings.

### Electronics and Waves

Sound waves, radio waves, and alternating electric currents can be modeled using sine and cosine functions.

## Simple Experiment

Choose nearby vertical objects on level ground and measure their shadows at nearly the same time. This keeps the solar angle effectively the same.

1. Measure a reference height <InlineMath math="h_r" /> and its horizontal shadow length <InlineMath math="s_r" />.

2. Calculate the common ratio <InlineMath math="k=h_r/s_r" />.

3. Measure another object's shadow length <InlineMath math="s" /> and estimate its height with <InlineMath math="h=ks" />.

4. If desired, calculate the Sun's elevation angle with <InlineMath math="\theta=\arctan k" />.

For example, if a <InlineMath math="1.5 \text{ m}" /> reference object casts a <InlineMath math="2.0 \text{ m}" /> shadow, then <InlineMath math="k=0.75" />. An object with an <InlineMath math="8.0 \text{ m}" /> shadow has the estimated height

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="h=0.75\times8.0\text{ m}=6.0\text{ m}" />
  <BlockMath math="\theta=\arctan(0.75)\approx36.87^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

If the ground slopes, an object is not vertical, or the measurement times differ substantially, this simple model no longer applies without adjustment.
