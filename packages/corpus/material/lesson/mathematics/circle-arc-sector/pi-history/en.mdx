## Understanding and Meaning of Pi

For every circle, the ratio of its circumference to its diameter is the same. We call that constant <InlineMath math="\pi" /> (pi), and its value begins <InlineMath math="3.14159" />.

Mathematically, <InlineMath math="\pi" /> can be defined as:

<BlockMath math="\pi = \frac{\text{circle circumference}}{\text{circle diameter}}" />

The ratio does not depend on the circle's size. If every length doubles, the circumference and diameter double together, so their ratio stays unchanged.

## Early Approximations

Surviving ancient sources record several approximations rather than one universal value. A Mesopotamian approximation was <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{25}{8}=3.125" />.

The Egyptian Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, dated to about <InlineMath math="1650 \text{ BC}" />, gives a rule equivalent to <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{256}{81}" />. This is about <InlineMath math="3.16049" />. Neither fraction equals <InlineMath math="\pi" /> exactly, and the Mesopotamian value <InlineMath math="3.125" /> is actually the closer of these two approximations.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{256}{81} = 3.16049\ldots" />
</MathContainer>

## Archimedes' Method and Polygons

Archimedes of Syracuse, a Greek mathematician who lived around <InlineMath math="287\text{-}212 \text{ BC}" />, developed a systematic method for bounding <InlineMath math="\pi" /> more precisely. He compared regular polygons inscribed in and circumscribed around a circle.

The perimeter of an inscribed regular polygon is less than the circle's circumference, while the perimeter of a circumscribed regular polygon is greater. Increasing the number of sides brings those lower and upper bounds closer together.

## Contributions of Chinese Mathematicians

In the <InlineMath math="5" />th century AD, the Chinese mathematician and astronomer Zu Chongzhi refined the polygon method and established the remarkably tight bound <InlineMath math="3.1415926 < \pi < 3.1415927" />.

Zu Chongzhi also gave <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{355}{113}" />, which equals <InlineMath math="3.1415929" />. The fraction agrees with <InlineMath math="\pi" /> through the first six digits after the decimal point. His seven-decimal bound was not surpassed until al-Kashi's work in the <InlineMath math="15" />th century, almost a millennium later.

<BlockMath math="\frac{355}{113} = 3.1415929\ldots" />
</MathContainer>

## Modern Era and Pi Symbol

The Welsh mathematician William Jones used <InlineMath math="\pi" /> with its present meaning in his <InlineMath math="1706" /> work "Synopsis Palmariorum Matheseos". The notation was not immediately universal.

Leonhard Euler later adopted the symbol in his influential work, helping <InlineMath math="\pi" /> become the standard notation still used today.

## Special Properties of Pi

The number <InlineMath math="\pi" /> is irrational, so it cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. Johann Heinrich Lambert supplied the first rigorous proof of its irrationality in the <InlineMath math="18" />th century. Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in <InlineMath math="1882" /> that <InlineMath math="\pi" /> is also transcendental, meaning it is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients.

For many classroom calculations, <InlineMath math="\pi \approx 3.14" /> or <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{22}{7}" /> is sufficient. Scientific and engineering work uses as many digits as the required precision demands.

The fundamental relationship of <InlineMath math="\pi" /> with circle geometry can be expressed as:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="C = \pi \cdot d" />
<BlockMath math="A = \pi \cdot r^2" />
</MathContainer>

where <InlineMath math="C" /> is circumference, <InlineMath math="d" /> is diameter, <InlineMath math="A" /> is area, and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the radius of the circle.
