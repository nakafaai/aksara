## Definition of Pi

For every circle, <Highlight>the ratio of its circumference to its diameter</Highlight> is the same. We call that constant <InlineMath math="\pi" /> (pi), and its value begins <InlineMath math="3.14159" />.

Mathematically, <InlineMath math="\pi" /> can be defined as:

<BlockMath math="\pi = \frac{\text{circle circumference}}{\text{circle diameter}}" />

The ratio does not depend on the circle's size. If every length doubles, the circumference and diameter double together, so their ratio stays unchanged.

Roll a circle of diameter 1 along a line for one full turn. The point that started at the bottom touches the line again after a distance equal to the circumference, <InlineMath math="\pi \approx 3.14159" />.

## Early Approximations

Surviving ancient sources record several **approximations**. A Mesopotamian approximation was <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{25}{8}=3.125" />.

The Egyptian Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, dated to about <InlineMath math="1650 \text{ BC}" />, gives a rule equivalent to <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{256}{81}" />. This is about <InlineMath math="3.16049" />. Neither fraction equals <InlineMath math="\pi" /> exactly, and the Mesopotamian value <InlineMath math="3.125" /> is actually the closer of these two approximations.

In the Egyptian area rule, a circle of diameter <InlineMath math="9" /> receives the approximate area <InlineMath math="8^2=64" />. Comparing this with <InlineMath math="\pi r^2" /> shows where the fraction comes from:

<BlockMath math="\pi\approx\frac{64}{(9/2)^2}=\frac{64}{81/4}=\frac{256}{81}" />


## Method of Archimedes and Polygons

Archimedes of Syracuse, a Greek mathematician who lived around <InlineMath math="287\text{-}212 \text{ BC}" />, developed a systematic method for bounding <InlineMath math="\pi" /> more precisely. He compared regular polygons inscribed in and circumscribed around a circle.

The perimeter of an inscribed regular polygon is less than the circle's circumference, while the perimeter of a circumscribed regular polygon is greater. Increasing the number of sides brings those lower and upper bounds closer together.

For the hexagons shown, the radius is <InlineMath math="3" /> and the diameter is <InlineMath math="6" />. Each inner-hexagon side equals the radius. The outer-hexagon side follows from a right triangle with angle <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="P_{\mathrm{in}}=6(3)=18,\qquad P_{\mathrm{out}}=6\left(2(3)\tan30^\circ\right)=12\sqrt3" />

Dividing both polygon perimeters by the same diameter gives **lower and upper bounds**:

<BlockMath math="\frac{18}{6}<\pi<\frac{12\sqrt3}{6}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad3<\pi<2\sqrt3" />


Using <InlineMath math="96" />-sided polygons, Archimedes proved that <InlineMath math="\pi" /> lies between <InlineMath math="\frac{223}{71}" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{22}{7}" />. In particular, <InlineMath math="\frac{22}{7}" /> is the upper end of this bound. Its value is slightly larger than <InlineMath math="\pi" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="3.1408 < \pi < 3.1429" />
</MathContainer>

## Contributions of Chinese Mathematicians

In the <InlineMath math="5" />th century AD, the Chinese mathematician and astronomer Zu Chongzhi established the recorded bound <InlineMath math="3.1415926 < \pi < 3.1415927" />, an interval only <InlineMath math="10^{-7}" /> wide.

Zu Chongzhi also gave the **fractional approximation** <InlineMath math="\pi \approx \frac{355}{113}" />, whose decimal expansion begins <InlineMath math="3.1415929" />. The fraction agrees with <InlineMath math="\pi" /> through the first six digits after the decimal point. His seven-decimal bound was not surpassed until al-Kashi's work in the <InlineMath math="15" />th century, almost a millennium later.

## Modern Era and Pi Symbol

The Welsh mathematician William Jones used <InlineMath math="\pi" /> with its **present meaning** in his <InlineMath math="1706" /> work "Synopsis Palmariorum Matheseos". The notation was not immediately universal.

Leonhard Euler later adopted the symbol in his influential work, helping <InlineMath math="\pi" /> become the standard notation still used today.

## Special Properties of Pi

The number <InlineMath math="\pi" /> is **irrational**, so it cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. Johann Heinrich Lambert supplied the first rigorous proof of its irrationality in the <InlineMath math="18" />th century. Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in <InlineMath math="1882" /> that <InlineMath math="\pi" /> is also **transcendental**, meaning it is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients.

For a circle of diameter <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />, using <InlineMath math="3.14" /> gives a circumference of <InlineMath math="31.4\text{ cm}" />. A more precise pi value gives about <InlineMath math="31.41593\text{ cm}" />. The difference is about <InlineMath math="0.01593\text{ cm}" />, so the approximation should match the required precision.

The circumference and area formulas use the same constant:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="C = \pi \cdot d" />
<BlockMath math="A = \pi \cdot r^2" />
</MathContainer>

In these equations, <InlineMath math="C" /> is circumference, <InlineMath math="d" /> is diameter, <InlineMath math="A" /> is area, and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the radius of the circle.
