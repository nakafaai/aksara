## Arc Length and Sector Area Proportion

Arc length and sector area take the same fraction of their full-circle values, so <Highlight>both change by the same fraction</Highlight> as the central angle changes:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\frac{\text{Arc Length}}{\text{Circle Circumference}} \\ &amp;=\frac{\text{Sector Area}}{\text{Circle Area}} \\ &amp;=\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

All three fractions describe the same share of a complete circle.

## Formulas for Arc Length and Sector Area

Multiply the **angle fraction** by the circumference to obtain arc length, or by the circle area to obtain sector area. Both formulas use the radius and angle of the same sector:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Arc Length} = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Sector Area} = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \times \pi r^2" />
</MathContainer>

Let arc length be <InlineMath math="s" /> and sector area be <InlineMath math="A" />. For a nonzero angle, divide the formulas. The common angle factor cancels:

<BlockMath math="\frac{A}{s}=\frac{\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}\pi r^2}{\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}2\pi r}=\frac{r}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=\frac{sr}{2}" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Sector Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Arc Length} \times r \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

## Application in Earth Measurement

In a simplified model of Eratosthenes' measurement, Earth is spherical, both cities lie on the **same meridian**, and sunlight arrives in parallel rays. At noon on the summer solstice, the Sun is taken to be overhead at Syene. In Alexandria, sunlight makes an angle of about <InlineMath math="7.2^\circ" /> with the vertical. Parallel rays make this equal to the angle between the cities at Earth's center.

Taking the surface distance between the cities as <InlineMath math="5{,}000 \text{ stadia}" /> makes it one fiftieth of the circumference. This model explains the geometric method, rather than giving exact modern measurements for the cities.

Using the proportional relationship:

Let <InlineMath math="D" /> be the distance from Alexandria to Syene and <InlineMath math="C" /> the circumference of the Earth:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac DC=\frac{7.2^\circ}{360^\circ}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \frac{5{,}000 \text{ stadia}}{\text{Earth Circumference}} &amp;= \frac{7.2^\circ}{360^\circ} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{50} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Earth Circumference} \\ &amp;= 5{,}000 \times 50 \\ &amp;= 250{,}000 \text{ stadia} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The ancient stadion has an **uncertain length**. Assuming 160 meters per stadion converts 250,000 stadia to 40,000 km. A different assumed stadion length produces a different conversion.

## Relationship Between Arc and Sector

For a circle with radius <InlineMath math="r" /> and a **nonzero central angle**, dividing the sector area by its arc length gives <InlineMath math="r/2" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{\text{Sector Area}}{\text{Arc Length}} = \frac{r}{2}" />
</MathContainer>

Changing the central angle changes both quantities by the same factor, so their ratio stays <InlineMath math="r/2" />.

## Exercises

The first two problems use the **area and arc-length relationship** to find an area or radius. The third uses the arc-to-circumference ratio for a spherical Earth model.

1. A circle has a radius of <InlineMath math="14 \text{ cm}" />. If the arc length of a sector is <InlineMath math="22 \text{ cm}" />, determine the area of that sector.

2. Given a sector area of <InlineMath math="154 \text{ cm}^2" /> and an arc length of <InlineMath math="22 \text{ cm}" />. Determine the radius of the circle.

3. Two cities lie on the same great circle and are <InlineMath math="1{,}000 \text{ km}" /> apart along that circle. If the corresponding angle at Earth's center is <InlineMath math="9^\circ" />, determine the estimated circumference of Earth.

### Worked Solutions

Identify the **unknown quantity**. Multiply arc length by half the radius to find area. Rearrange that equation to find radius. Use the fraction of a full turn to find Earth's circumference.

**Solution 1**

Given: <InlineMath math="r = 14 \text{ cm}" />, arc length = <InlineMath math="22 \text{ cm}" />

Using the relationship: <InlineMath math="\text{Sector Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Arc Length} \times r" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Sector Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 22 \times 14" />
<BlockMath math="= \frac{22 \times 14}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="= \frac{308}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="= 154 \text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution 2**

Given: sector area = <InlineMath math="154 \text{ cm}^2" />, arc length = <InlineMath math="22 \text{ cm}" />

Using the relationship: <InlineMath math="\frac{\text{Sector Area}}{\text{Arc Length}} = \frac{r}{2}" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{154}{22} = \frac{r}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="7 = \frac{r}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="r = 14 \text{ cm}" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution 3**

Given: distance = <InlineMath math="1{,}000 \text{ km}" />, angle = <InlineMath math="9^\circ" />

Using the proportion: <InlineMath math="\frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Earth Circumference}} = \frac{9^\circ}{360^\circ}" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \frac{1{,}000}{\text{Earth Circumference}} &amp;= \frac{9^\circ}{360^\circ} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{40} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Earth Circumference} = 1{,}000 \times 40" />
<BlockMath math="= 40{,}000 \text{ km}" />
</MathContainer>
