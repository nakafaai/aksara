## An Arc and Its Sector Take the Same Share

An arc and the sector cut off by the same central angle take **the same share of the circle**. The arc's share of the circumference equals the sector's share of the circle's area, and both equal the central angle divided by <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />. That equality gives a short formula linking the sector area <InlineMath math="A" />, the arc length <InlineMath math="s" />, and the radius <InlineMath math="r" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{s}{2\pi r} = \frac{A}{\pi r^2} = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} s r" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> sector diagram uses radius <InlineMath math="4" />. Its arc is one eighth of the circumference, and its sector is one eighth of the circle's area.

## Sector Area from Arc Length

The formula <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} s r" /> comes from **dividing the sector area formula by the arc formula**. The shared angle factor cancels, so the central angle is no longer needed:

<BlockMath math="\frac{A}{s} = \frac{\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}\pi r^2}{\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}2\pi r} = \frac{r}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \frac{1}{2} s r" />

This looks like the triangle area formula, half the base times the height. A very thin sector is almost a triangle with base equal to the arc <InlineMath math="s" /> and height <InlineMath math="r" />, and the whole sector is the sum of many thin triangles like that. Use the formula when a problem gives the arc length and radius but not the angle.

## Measuring the Earth with an Arc

At noon on the summer solstice, the Sun is taken to be straight above Syene. In Alexandria, the direction of the Sun makes an angle of about <InlineMath math="7.2^\circ" /> with the vertical. Because the rays are parallel, that angle equals the angle between the two cities at the Earth's center.

The surface distance between the cities is taken as <InlineMath math="5{,}000" /> stadia. An arc of <InlineMath math="7.2^\circ" /> is one fiftieth of a turn, so:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{5{,}000}{C} = \frac{7.2^\circ}{360^\circ} = \frac{1}{50}" />
  <BlockMath math="C = 5{,}000 \times 50 = 250{,}000 \text{ stadia}" />
</MathContainer>

The length of one ancient stadion is **not known exactly**. If one stadion is taken as <InlineMath math="160" /> meters, the result is <InlineMath math="40{,}000" /> km. Other choices of stadion length give different numbers, so the calculation shows the method rather than an exact modern value.

## Worked Examples on Arcs and Sectors

Every example starts by **choosing the relationship that fits** the data: the formula <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} s r" /> for an arc and a radius, or a ratio for two sectors on the same circle.

**Example 1**. A circle with radius <InlineMath math="14\text{ cm}" /> has an arc of length <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" />. Find the area of its sector.

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \times 22 \times 14 = 154\text{ cm}^2" />

Check through the angle, with <InlineMath math="\pi = \frac{22}{7}" />. The circumference is <InlineMath math="88\text{ cm}" />, so the <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" /> arc is one quarter of it, and one quarter of the circle's area <InlineMath math="616\text{ cm}^2" /> is indeed <InlineMath math="154\text{ cm}^2" />.

**Example 2**. A sector has area <InlineMath math="154\text{ cm}^2" /> and arc length <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" />. Find the radius.

<BlockMath math="\frac{A}{s} = \frac{r}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{2A}{s} = \frac{2 \times 154}{22} = 14\text{ cm}" />

**Example 3**. On one circle, the arc with central angle <InlineMath math="40^\circ" /> is <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" /> long. Find the length of the arc with central angle <InlineMath math="100^\circ" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{s_2}{10} = \frac{100^\circ}{40^\circ} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s_2 = 25\text{ cm}" />

The second sector's area is also <InlineMath math="\frac{100}{40} = 2.5" /> times the first, because the ratio is the same.

## Common Mistakes

Before you calculate, make sure the two sectors you compare lie on **the same circle**. The table pairs common mistakes with a way to check for each one.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="A = s \times r" /> | The factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> comes from <InlineMath math="\frac{\pi r^2}{2\pi r} = \frac{r}{2}" /> | Compare with the full formula for one angle |
| Comparing arcs on different circles | The ratio holds only for the same radius | Check that the radii are equal |
| Using the diameter as <InlineMath math="r" /> | The formula uses the distance from the center to the arc | A sector cannot be larger than the circle |
| Mixing units of area and length | An arc has a length unit, a sector an area unit | Check the unit of every answer |

Forgetting the factor of one half makes the sector area twice too large. A <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> sector with the same area as the whole circle is the sign of that mistake.

## Practice on Arcs and Sectors

For each problem, first decide whether you need **half the arc times the radius** or a ratio. Problem 4 needs both.

1. The arc of a sector is <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> long on a circle with radius <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />. Find the sector's area.

2. A sector with radius <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> has area <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}^2" />. Find its arc length and its approximate central angle.

3. Two cities lie on the same great circle of the Earth, <InlineMath math="1{,}000\text{ km}" /> apart along that circle. The angle between them at the Earth's center is <InlineMath math="9^\circ" />. Estimate the Earth's circumference.

4. On a circle, the arc with central angle <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> is <InlineMath math="4\pi\text{ cm}" /> long. Find the radius, the length of the arc with central angle <InlineMath math="150^\circ" />, and the area of the <InlineMath math="150^\circ" /> sector.

### Answers

**Problem 1**. The arc and radius are known, so use **half the arc times the radius**:

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 10 = 30\text{ cm}^2" />

**Problem 2**. Rearrange the formula for the arc, then divide the arc by the circumference **to get the angle**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="s = \frac{2A}{r} = \frac{2 \times 60}{12} = 10\text{ cm}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{10}{2\pi \times 12} \times 360^\circ = \frac{150^\circ}{\pi} \approx 47.7^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 12 = 60" />.

**Problem 3**. An arc of <InlineMath math="9^\circ" /> is **one fortieth of a turn**, because <InlineMath math="\frac{9}{360} = \frac{1}{40}" />:

<BlockMath math="C = 1{,}000 \times 40 = 40{,}000\text{ km}" />

**Problem 4**. A <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> arc is one sixth of the circumference, so the circumference is <InlineMath math="6 \times 4\pi = 24\pi" /> and <InlineMath math="r = 12\text{ cm}" />. The <InlineMath math="150^\circ" /> arc comes from **the ratio of the angles**, and its sector area from half the arc times the radius:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="s = \frac{150^\circ}{60^\circ} \times 4\pi = 10\pi\text{ cm}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \times 10\pi \times 12 = 60\pi\text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the full formula: <InlineMath math="\frac{150}{360} \times \pi \times 12^2 = 60\pi" />.
