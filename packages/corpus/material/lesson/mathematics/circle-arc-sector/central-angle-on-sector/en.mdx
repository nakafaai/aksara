## The Central Angle Sets the Share of a Sector

The central angle of a sector is the angle at the center of the circle formed by **the two radii that bound the sector**. It decides what share of the circle the sector takes: a sector with central angle <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> takes <InlineMath math="\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ}" /> of the circle's area and of its circumference. If you know the sector area or the arc length, you can work back to the central angle:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{A}{\pi r^2} \times 360^\circ" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{s}{2\pi r} \times 360^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

In both formulas, <InlineMath math="A" /> is the sector area, <InlineMath math="s" /> the arc length, and <InlineMath math="r" /> the radius. An angle of <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> is one third of a full turn, so the sector takes one third of the circle's area. Its area is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3} \times \pi \times 4^2 = \frac{16\pi}{3}" />, and its arc is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3} \times 8\pi = \frac{8\pi}{3}" /> long.

Suppose the sector area is known to be <InlineMath math="\frac{16\pi}{5}" /> square units. Its share of the circle is the sector area divided by the circle's area:

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{16\pi/5}{\pi \times 4^2} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{5} \times 360^\circ = 72^\circ" />

The arc of the same sector is <InlineMath math="\frac{8\pi}{5}" /> units long. The arc formula gives the same angle, so the two routes check each other:

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{8\pi/5}{2\pi \times 4} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{5} \times 360^\circ = 72^\circ" />

## The Central Angle in Radians

A central angle can also be given in radians. A full turn is <InlineMath math="2\pi" /> radians, so **the share of the circle** is <InlineMath math="\frac{\theta}{2\pi}" /> for an angle of <InlineMath math="\theta" /> radians. The arc and area formulas get shorter:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="s = r\theta" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2}\theta r^2" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="72^\circ" /> sector in the parameter diagram is <InlineMath math="\theta = \frac{72}{180}\pi = \frac{2\pi}{5}" /> radians. The radian formulas give the same results as the degree formulas:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="s = 4 \times \frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{8\pi}{5}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2\pi}{5} \times 4^2 = \frac{16\pi}{5}" />
</MathContainer>

Use the radian formulas only when the angle really is in radians. Putting <InlineMath math="72" /> straight into <InlineMath math="s = r\theta" /> gives an arc of length <InlineMath math="288" />, far longer than the whole circumference.

## Worked Examples on the Central Angle of a Sector

Every example starts by **writing the share of the circle** as a fraction. Once you have that fraction, multiply it by <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />.

**Example 1**. A sector of a circle with radius <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> has area <InlineMath math="6\pi\text{ cm}^2" />. Find its central angle.

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{6\pi}{\pi \times 6^2} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{6} \times 360^\circ = 60^\circ" />

Check with the area formula: <InlineMath math="\frac{60}{360} \times 36\pi = 6\pi" />.

**Example 2**. The arc of a sector is <InlineMath math="5\pi\text{ cm}" /> long on a circle of radius <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />. Find its central angle.

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{5\pi}{2\pi \times 10} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{4} \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ" />

Check: a <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> arc is one quarter of the circumference <InlineMath math="20\pi" />, which is <InlineMath math="5\pi" />.

**Example 3**. In a survey of <InlineMath math="120" /> students, <InlineMath math="54" /> chose football. The results are drawn as a pie chart. Find the central angle of the football sector.

A pie chart works the same way: the sector's share is the number of students who chose it divided by the total.

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{54}{120} \times 360^\circ = 0.45 \times 360^\circ = 162^\circ" />

Check with the rest of the chart. The other <InlineMath math="66" /> students take <InlineMath math="\frac{66}{120} \times 360^\circ = 198^\circ" />, and <InlineMath math="162^\circ + 198^\circ = 360^\circ" />.

## Common Mistakes

Before you calculate, make sure **the angle unit and the radius** are right. The table pairs common mistakes with a way to check for each one.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the diameter as the radius | The circle's area uses <InlineMath math="r^2" />, so this makes the area four times too large | Halve the diameter first |
| Putting degrees into a radian formula | <InlineMath math="s = r\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2}\theta r^2" /> work only in radians | An arc cannot be longer than the circumference |
| Dividing the sector area by the circumference | Compare area with area and arc with circumference | The share of the circle must lie between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Forgetting to multiply the fraction by <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> | The fraction is only the share, not the angle yet | A sector's central angle lies between <InlineMath math="0^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> |

The diameter mistake shows when your angle comes out four times too small. Getting <InlineMath math="15^\circ" /> for a sector that looks like one sixth of the circle means the diameter was used as the radius.

## Practice on the Central Angle of a Sector

For each problem, first write **the share of the circle** as a fraction. Problem 4 uses radians.

1. A circle is split into <InlineMath math="9" /> equal sectors. Find the central angle of each sector.

2. A sector of a circle with radius <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> has area <InlineMath math="36\pi\text{ cm}^2" />. Find its central angle.

3. A circle with diameter <InlineMath math="28\text{ cm}" /> has an arc of length <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" />. Using <InlineMath math="\pi = \frac{22}{7}" />, find the central angle of the arc.

4. A sector with radius <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" /> has an arc of length <InlineMath math="4\text{ cm}" />. Find its central angle in radians and its area.

### Answers

**Problem 1**. A full turn is shared equally among <InlineMath math="9" /> sectors, so **each central angle** is <InlineMath math="\frac{360^\circ}{9} = 40^\circ" />. Check: <InlineMath math="9 \times 40^\circ = 360^\circ" />.

**Problem 2**. The share of the circle is the sector area divided by **the circle's area** <InlineMath math="\pi \times 12^2 = 144\pi" />:

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{36\pi}{144\pi} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{4} \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{90}{360} \times 144\pi = 36\pi" />.

**Problem 3**. The radius is **half the diameter**, <InlineMath math="14\text{ cm}" />, so the circumference is <InlineMath math="2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 88\text{ cm}" />:

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{22}{88} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{4} \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ" />

Check: one quarter of <InlineMath math="88\text{ cm}" /> is <InlineMath math="22\text{ cm}" />.

**Problem 4**. In radians, <InlineMath math="s = r\theta" />, so **the angle is the arc divided by the radius**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\theta = \frac{s}{r} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8\text{ radians}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.8 \times 5^2 = 10\text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the relationship <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} s r = \frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 5 = 10" />. In degrees, the angle is about <InlineMath math="0.8 \times \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 45.8^\circ" />.
