## Concept of Central Angle on Sector

A circle sector is bounded by two radii and the arc between them. The two radii form a central angle whose vertex lies at the center of the circle. An open hand fan or a slice of cake has the same basic shape.

The central angle determines what fraction of the circle the sector occupies. For a fixed radius, a larger angle produces a larger sector area, while a smaller angle produces a smaller one.

## Properties of Central Angle in Sectors

Central angles on sectors have characteristics that distinguish them from other angles:

- **Both angle sides**: Always consist of radii of equal length
- **Angle vertex**: Always located exactly at the center of the circle
- **Proportion to circle**: The size of the central angle determines the sector's proportion to the entire circle

Within the same circle, equal central angles produce equal sector areas. Dividing the full angle into equal angles therefore divides the circle into equal areas.

## Relationship Between Central Angle and Sector Area

The relationship between central angle and sector area can be expressed as a mathematical ratio. There are two main formulas used:

**For angles in degrees:**

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times \pi r^2" />

**For angles in radians:**

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \theta r^2" />

The variables in both formulas are:
- <InlineMath math="A" /> = sector area
- <InlineMath math="\theta" /> = central angle, measured in degrees in the first formula and radians in the second
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = radius

This ratio shows that sector area is proportional to the central angle size. When the central angle is doubled, the sector area also becomes twice as large. Conversely, if the central angle is halved, the sector area becomes half.

## Measurement and Identification of Central Angles

To identify a central angle, check that its vertex lies at the center of the circle and that both sides are radii. By contrast, an inscribed angle has its vertex on the circumference.

**Central angle measurement methods:**

- **Using a protractor**: Place the protractor's center point exactly at the circle's center, ensure the base line is parallel to one radius, then read the scale at the position of the second radius
- **Mathematical calculation**: Using relationships among central angle, radius, arc length, and sector area
- **Circle division**: dividing <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> by the number of equal parts

Once the central angle is known, its value can be used to calculate the corresponding sector area or arc length.

## Central Angle and Arc Theorem

For a central angle and its intercepted arc, the following relationship holds:

**Main theorem:**
- The central angle measure equals the arc measure it subtends
- If a central angle measures <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> degrees, then the arc it forms also measures <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> degrees

**Properties:**

- In the same circle, congruent central angles produce congruent arcs and sectors
- If two sectors of the same circle have the same area, then their central angles have the same measure
- When adjacent central angles are added, the measures of their corresponding arcs are added as well

These properties connect central angles, arc measures, and sector areas within one circle. Always distinguish an arc's degree measure from its physical length.

## Calculating Central Angle from Sector Information

Determining the central angle measure can be done through various known information about the sector:

1. **From sector area and radius:**

   <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ \times A}{\pi r^2}" />

2. **From arc length and radius:**

   <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{180^\circ \times s}{\pi r}" />

3. **From equal circle division:**

   <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ}{n}" />

where:
- <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> = central angle (in degrees)
- <InlineMath math="A" /> = sector area
- <InlineMath math="s" /> = arc length
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = radius
- <InlineMath math="n" /> = number of divisions

Choose the formula that matches the given information: sector area, arc length, or the number of equal parts.
