## How a Central Angle Sets the Size of a Sector

A circle sector is bounded by <Highlight>two radii and the arc</Highlight> between them. The two radii form a central angle whose vertex lies at the center of the circle. An open hand fan or a slice of cake has the same basic shape.

The central angle determines what fraction of the circle the sector occupies. For a fixed radius, a larger angle produces a larger sector area, while a smaller angle produces a smaller one.

## Properties of Central Angle in Sectors

Within the **same circle**, equal central angles produce equal sector areas. The two radii form the angle sides, and the center is the vertex. Dividing a full turn into eight equal angles gives each sector one eighth of the circle area.

<BlockMath math="\theta=\frac{360^\circ}{8}=45^\circ,\qquad A=\frac{1}{8}\pi r^2" />

## Relationship Between Central Angle and Sector Area

A full turn corresponds to the whole circle area. For a sector, the area comes from the central angle, and the angle unit decides which formula applies:

**For angles in degrees:**

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times \pi r^2" />

**For angles in radians:**

<BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} \theta r^2" />

The variables in both formulas are:
- <InlineMath math="A" /> = sector area
- <InlineMath math="\theta" /> = central angle, measured in degrees in the first formula and radians in the second
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = radius

Both formulas show that <Highlight>sector area is proportional to the central angle</Highlight> size. For a fixed radius, when the central angle is doubled, the sector area also becomes twice as large. Conversely, if the central angle is halved, the sector area becomes half.

## Measurement and Identification of Central Angles

To identify a central angle, check that its vertex lies **at the center of the circle** and that both sides are radii. By contrast, an inscribed angle has its vertex on the circumference.

**Central angle measurement methods:**

- **Using a protractor**: Place the protractor's center point exactly at the circle's center, ensure the base line is parallel to one radius, then read the scale at the position of the second radius
- **Mathematical calculation**: Using relationships among central angle, radius, arc length, and sector area
- **Circle division**: dividing <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> by the number of equal parts

Once the central angle is known, its value can be used to calculate the corresponding sector area or arc length.

## Central Angle and Arc Theorem

An arc's **degree measure** equals the central angle that subtends it. This differs from arc length: at the same angle, a larger radius produces a longer arc. In the same circle, equal angles produce congruent arcs and sectors. Equal sector areas also imply equal angles.

For adjacent sectors that do not overlap, add their angles, arc lengths, and areas. For example, the 45-degree and 90-degree sectors in the diagram combine into a 135-degree sector:

<BlockMath math="\frac{45^\circ}{360^\circ}\pi r^2+\frac{90^\circ}{360^\circ}\pi r^2=\frac{135^\circ}{360^\circ}\pi r^2" />

## Calculating Central Angle from Sector Information

Determining the central angle measure can be done through various known information about the sector:

**From sector area and radius:**

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ \times A}{\pi r^2}" />

**From arc length and radius:**

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{180^\circ \times s}{\pi r}" />

**From equal circle division:**

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ}{n}" />

The variables mean:
- <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> = central angle (in degrees)
- <InlineMath math="A" /> = sector area
- <InlineMath math="s" /> = arc length
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = radius
- <InlineMath math="n" /> = number of divisions

Choose the formula that matches the **given information**: sector area, arc length, or the number of equal parts.

In the diagram, take the radius as 4 units. If the sector area is <InlineMath math="16\pi/5" /> square units, rearrange the area formula to find the angle:

<BlockMath math="\alpha=\frac{360^\circ\times A}{\pi r^2}=\frac{360^\circ\times(16\pi/5)}{\pi\times4^2}=\frac{360^\circ}{5}=72^\circ" />

The same sector has arc length <InlineMath math="8\pi/5" /> units. The arc-length formula gives the same angle:

<BlockMath math="\alpha=\frac{180^\circ\times s}{\pi r}=\frac{180^\circ\times(8\pi/5)}{4\pi}=72^\circ" />

**Formula application examples:**

- Round pizza cut radially into <InlineMath math="8" /> equal sectors: <InlineMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ}{8} = 45^\circ" />
- Adjacent hour marks on a clock face with <InlineMath math="12" /> equal divisions: <InlineMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ}{12} = 30^\circ" />
- Round cake cut radially into <InlineMath math="6 \text{ equal sectors}" />: <InlineMath math="\alpha = \frac{360^\circ}{6} = 60^\circ" />

These examples demonstrate the direct application of circle division formulas in daily life, where each part has an equal central angle.
