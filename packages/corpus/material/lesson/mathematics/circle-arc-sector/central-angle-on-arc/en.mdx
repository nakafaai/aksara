## Definition of Central Angle

A central angle is formed by two radii and has its vertex <Highlight>at the center of the circle</Highlight>. A slice of pizza gives the same shape: its pointed tip lies at the center, and its two straight edges follow radii.

In a circle with center <InlineMath math="O" />, let <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> lie on the circumference. Then <InlineMath math="\angle AOB" /> is a central angle, and it subtends the arc <InlineMath math="AB" /> between those two points.

## Relationship Between Central Angle and Arc

<Highlight>A central angle and its intercepted arc</Highlight> have the same degree measure. A <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> central angle therefore intercepts a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> arc.

If <InlineMath math="\angle AOB = 60^\circ" />, then arc <InlineMath math="AB" /> also measures <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Both numbers are degree measures. The arc length uses a length unit such as centimeters.

## Types of Arcs Based on Central Angle

The central angle selects a **fraction of the circumference**. Compare it with half a turn to distinguish the three arc types. Two arcs with the same endpoints together complete a full turn.

### Minor Arc

A minor arc is subtended by a central angle less than <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. It is **shorter than half the circumference**. At <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> it covers a quarter of the circumference, because <InlineMath math="90/360" /> is exactly one quarter.

### Semicircle Arc

A semicircular arc is subtended by a central angle of exactly <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. Its endpoints lie on **a diameter**, so its length is half the circle's circumference.

### Major Arc

A major arc is subtended by a **reflex central angle** greater than <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. It is longer than half the circumference. Its degree measure is the remainder of the full turn after subtracting the corresponding minor arc measure:

<BlockMath math="\theta_{\mathrm{major}}=360^\circ-60^\circ=300^\circ" />

## Arc Length Calculation

Arc length can be calculated using the ratio between the central angle and the full angle of the circle. The basic formula for calculating arc length is:

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />

The variables in this formula are:
- <InlineMath math="s" /> = arc length
- <InlineMath math="\theta" /> = central angle measure in degrees
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = radius of the circle

An angle in radians is **arc length divided by radius**, so an angle in radians gives the formula directly:

<BlockMath math="s = \theta \times r" />

Here <InlineMath math="\theta" /> must be measured in radians.

## Calculating Arc Length from a Central Angle

Consider a circle with radius <InlineMath math="r = 6 \text{ cm}" /> and central angle <InlineMath math="\theta = 120^\circ" />.

This angle covers **one third of a turn**, so take one third of the circumference:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="s = \frac{120^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi \times 6" />
<BlockMath math="s = \frac{1}{3} \times 12\pi" />
<BlockMath math="s = 4\pi \text{ cm}" />
</MathContainer>

The arc corresponding to the central angle <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> is therefore <InlineMath math="4\pi \text{ cm}" /> long, or approximately <InlineMath math="12.57 \text{ cm}" />.

## Examples of Central Angles

A clock's **minute hand** completes one turn each hour. In five minutes, it sweeps <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />. If the hand is <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" /> long, its tip travels the arc:

<BlockMath math="s=\frac{30^\circ}{360^\circ}\times2\pi(10)=\frac{5\pi}{3}\text{ cm}" />

## Exercises

Each problem relates a central angle to the arc it cuts, so choose between **degrees and radians** and keep one unit throughout the calculation.

1. A circle has a radius of <InlineMath math="8 \text{ cm}" />. If the central angle facing an arc is <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, calculate the length of that arc.

2. Given that the arc length of a circle is <InlineMath math="10\pi \text{ cm}" /> and its radius is <InlineMath math="15 \text{ cm}" />. Determine the measure of the central angle facing that arc.

3. In a circle with center <InlineMath math="O" />, there is a central angle <InlineMath math="\angle AOB = 72^\circ" />. If the circle's radius is <InlineMath math="r = 5 \text{ cm}" />, determine the length of arc <InlineMath math="AB" /> and express the result in terms of <InlineMath math="\pi" />.

4. A bicycle wheel with radius <InlineMath math="30 \text{ cm}" /> rotates about a fixed axle through <InlineMath math="150^\circ" />. How far does a point on its rim move along its circular path?

### Worked Solutions

Track the **fraction of a full turn** in problems one, three, and four. In problem two, rearrange the equation because the angle is the unknown.

**Solution 1**

Given: <InlineMath math="r = 8 \text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="\theta = 45^\circ" />

Find: Arc length <InlineMath math="s" />

**Step 1**: Use the arc length formula

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />

**Step 2**: Substitute the known values

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{45^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi \times 8" />

**Step 3**: Simplify the fraction

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{1}{8} \times 16\pi" />

**Step 4**: Calculate the final result

<BlockMath math="s = 2\pi \text{ cm}" />

Therefore, the arc length is <InlineMath math="2\pi \text{ cm}" /> or approximately <InlineMath math="6.28 \text{ cm}" />.

**Solution 2**

Given: <InlineMath math="s = 10\pi \text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="r = 15 \text{ cm}" />

Find: Central angle measure <InlineMath math="\theta" />

**Step 1**: Use the arc length formula

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />

**Step 2**: Substitute the known values

<BlockMath math="10\pi = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi \times 15" />

**Step 3**: Simplify the equation

<BlockMath math="10\pi = \frac{\theta \times 30\pi}{360^\circ}" />

**Step 4**: Isolate <InlineMath math="\theta" />

<BlockMath math="\theta = \frac{10\pi \times 360^\circ}{30\pi}" />

**Step 5**: Calculate the final result

<BlockMath math="\theta = \frac{3600^\circ}{30} = 120^\circ" />

Therefore, the central angle measure facing that arc is <InlineMath math="120^\circ" />.

**Solution 3**

Given: <InlineMath math="\angle AOB = 72^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="r = 5 \text{ cm}" />

Find: Length of arc <InlineMath math="AB" />

**Step 1**: Use the arc length formula

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />

**Step 2**: Substitute the known values

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{72^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi \times 5" />

**Step 3**: Simplify the fraction

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{72^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 10\pi = \frac{1}{5} \times 10\pi" />

**Step 4**: Calculate the final result

<BlockMath math="s = 2\pi \text{ cm}" />

The length of arc <InlineMath math="AB" /> is <InlineMath math="2\pi \text{ cm}" />.

**Solution 4**

Given: <InlineMath math="r = 30 \text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="\theta = 150^\circ" />

Find: Distance traveled (arc length)

**Step 1**: Use the arc length formula

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />

**Step 2**: Substitute the known values

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{150^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi \times 30" />

**Step 3**: Simplify the fraction

<BlockMath math="s = \frac{150^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 60\pi = \frac{5}{12} \times 60\pi" />

**Step 4**: Calculate the final result

<BlockMath math="s = 25\pi \text{ cm}" />

The point moves <InlineMath math="25\pi \text{ cm}" /> along its circular path, or approximately <InlineMath math="78.54 \text{ cm}" />.
