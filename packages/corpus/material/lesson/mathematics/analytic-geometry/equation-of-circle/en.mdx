## Locating a Circle from Its Center and Radius

A circle equation describes every point on a circle in the coordinate plane. Imagine drawing with a compass on graph paper: the needle fixes the center, the opening fixes the radius, and the pencil traces exactly the points that satisfy the equation.

The circle equation contains the coordinates of the **center** and the **radius**, so both can be determined without drawing the circle first.

## Circle Centered at Origin

Start with the simplest case, a circle centered at the origin <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" />.

If we have a circle with center at <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" /> and radius <InlineMath math="r>0" />, then every point <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> on that circle has the same distance from the center, which is <InlineMath math="r" />.

Using the distance formula, we get:

<BlockMath math="\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r" />

If we **square both sides**, we get the circle equation with center at origin:

<BlockMath math="x^2 + y^2 = r^2" />

The graph shows that every point has the same distance from the center.

## Circle with Arbitrary Center

For a circle centered at <InlineMath math="P(a,b)" />, let the radius be <InlineMath math="r" />.

Every point <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> on this circle must have the same distance <InlineMath math="r" /> from center <InlineMath math="P(a,b)" />. Using the distance formula:

<BlockMath math="\sqrt{(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2} = r" />

After squaring, we get the <Highlight>standard equation for a circle</Highlight> with an arbitrary center:

<BlockMath math="(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2" />

## General Form of Circle Equation

Sometimes we find circle equations that have already been expanded into general form. For example, from the equation <InlineMath math="(x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 9" />, if we expand it:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 9" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 2x + 1 + y^2 - 4y + 4 = 9" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 4y + 5 = 9" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 4y - 4 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

This last form is called the **general form of circle equation**:

<BlockMath math="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" />

An equation in general form can be converted back to standard form by **completing the square**.

> Not all equations of the form <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" /> are circle equations. The condition is <InlineMath math="D^2 + E^2 - 4F > 0" />. If this value is zero, then it's just a single point, and if negative, then there's no curve at all.

Completing the square shows where the center and radius formulas come from:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + Dx = \left(x + \frac{D}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{D^2}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="y^2 + Ey = \left(y + \frac{E}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{E^2}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\left(x + \frac{D}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y + \frac{E}{2}\right)^2 \\ &amp;= \frac{D^2 + E^2 - 4F}{4} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

## Determining Center and Radius

From the **standard form** <InlineMath math="(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2" />, we can directly know:

The center is at <InlineMath math="(a,b)" /> and the radius is <InlineMath math="r" /> (obtained from <InlineMath math="r = \sqrt{r^2}" />).

While from the general form <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" />, we can determine:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{Center: } \left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Radius: } r = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{D^2 + E^2 - 4F}" />
</MathContainer>

## Practice Problems

Choose the equation form from the **given information**. Substitute a known center and radius directly into standard form. If the diameter endpoints are given, first find their midpoint and half the diameter length.

1. Determine the equation of a circle centered at <InlineMath math="(3,-2)" /> with radius <InlineMath math="5" />.

2. Given a circle with equation <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0" />. Determine the center and radius of the circle.

3. A circle passes through point <InlineMath math="(6,-8)" /> and is centered at <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" />. Determine the equation of the circle.

4. Determine the equation of a circle that has a diameter with endpoints at <InlineMath math="A(1,3)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(7,-1)" />.

### Worked Solutions

**Solution 1**:

Given center <InlineMath math="(a,b) = (3,-2)" /> and radius <InlineMath math="r = 5" />.

Using the **circle equation formula**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2" />
  <BlockMath math="(x-3)^2 + (y-(-2))^2 = 5^2" />
  <BlockMath math="(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25" />
</MathContainer>

So the circle equation is <InlineMath math="(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25" />.

**Solution 2**:

From the equation <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0" />, we **identify the coefficients**:

<InlineMath math="D = -6" />, <InlineMath math="E = 4" />, <InlineMath math="F = -12" />

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Center: } \left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right) \\ &amp;= \left(-\frac{(-6)}{2}, -\frac{4}{2}\right) \\ &amp;= (3, -2) \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} r &amp;= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{D^2 + E^2 - 4F} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(-6)^2 + 4^2 - 4(-12)} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} r &amp;= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{36 + 16 + 48} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{100} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \\ &amp;= 5 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

So the circle center is at <InlineMath math="(3,-2)" /> with radius <InlineMath math="5" />.

**Solution 3**:

Since the circle is centered at <InlineMath math="O(0,0)" /> and passes through point <InlineMath math="(6,-8)" />, the radius is the **distance from the center** to that point.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} r &amp;= \sqrt{(6-0)^2 + (-8-0)^2} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{6^2 + (-8)^2} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{36 + 64} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{100} \\ &amp;= 10 \end{aligned}" />

The circle equation: <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = r^2 = 10^2 = 100" />

**Solution 4**:

The circle center is the **midpoint of diameter** <InlineMath math="AB" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Center} \\ &amp;= \left(\frac{x_A + x_B}{2}, \frac{y_A + y_B}{2}\right) \\ &amp;= \left(\frac{1+7}{2}, \frac{3+(-1)}{2}\right) \\ &amp;= \left(\frac{8}{2}, \frac{2}{2}\right) \\ &amp;= (4, 1) \end{aligned}" />

The radius is half the diameter length:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}AB&amp;=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2}\\&amp;=\sqrt{(7-1)^2+(-1-3)^2}\\&amp;=\sqrt{6^2+(-4)^2}\\&amp;=\sqrt{36+16}\\&amp;=\sqrt{52}\\&amp;=2\sqrt{13}\end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="r = \frac{\text{diameter}}{2} = \frac{2\sqrt{13}}{2} = \sqrt{13}" />
</MathContainer>

The circle equation: <InlineMath math="(x-4)^2 + (y-1)^2 = (\sqrt{13})^2 = 13" />

In Solution 4 the diameter <InlineMath math="AB" /> fixes everything. Its midpoint <InlineMath math="(4, 1)" /> is the center, and half its length, <InlineMath math="\sqrt{13}" />, is the radius, so both <InlineMath math="A(1, 3)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(7, -1)" /> lie on <InlineMath math="(x - 4)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 13" />.
