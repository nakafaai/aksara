## Three Ways a Line Can Meet a Circle

A line and a circle in the same plane have only three possibilities. The line **cuts the circle at two points**, **touches the circle at exactly one point**, or **does not meet the circle at all**. A line that cuts the circle twice is called a secant, and a line that touches it once is called a tangent.

You can decide which case you have in two ways, and both always give the same answer:

| Position | Discriminant after substitution | Distance from center to line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cuts at two points | <InlineMath math="\Delta > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d < r" /> |
| Touches at one point | <InlineMath math="\Delta = 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d = r" /> |
| Does not meet | <InlineMath math="\Delta < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d > r" /> |

## Why the Discriminant Counts Intersection Points

An intersection point of the line and the circle must satisfy **both equations at once**. So substitute the line <InlineMath math="y = mx + c" /> into the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" />. The result is a quadratic equation in <InlineMath math="x" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + (mx + c)^2 + Dx \\ &amp;\quad{}+ E(mx + c) + F = 0 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + m^2x^2 + 2mcx + c^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ Dx + Emx + Ec + F = 0 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;(1 + m^2)x^2 + (2mc + D + Em)x \\ &amp;\quad{}+ (c^2 + Ec + F) = 0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The coefficients use capital letters so they are not confused with the line's constant <InlineMath math="c" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="A = 1 + m^2" />
  <BlockMath math="B = 2mc + D + Em" />
  <BlockMath math="C = c^2 + Ec + F" />
</MathContainer>

Each real root <InlineMath math="x" /> gives exactly one intersection point, because the line's equation gives its matching <InlineMath math="y" /> at once. So the number of intersection points equals the number of distinct real roots, and the **discriminant** tells you that number:

<BlockMath math="\Delta = B^2 - 4AC" />

The coefficient <InlineMath math="A = 1 + m^2" /> is never zero, so the substitution always gives a quadratic equation. Its sign rule is the rule for the [types of roots of a quadratic equation](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-types-of-root): two different real roots, one repeated root, or no real root.

A vertical line <InlineMath math="x = k" /> cannot be written as <InlineMath math="y = mx + c" />. For such a line, substitute <InlineMath math="x = k" /> and solve the quadratic equation in <InlineMath math="y" />. For example, <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> in <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 9" /> gives <InlineMath math="9 + y^2 = 9" />, so <InlineMath math="y = 0" /> is a repeated root. The line <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> touches the circle at <InlineMath math="(3, 0)" />. Check: the distance from the center <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> to the line <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> is <InlineMath math="3 = r" />.

## Comparing the Distance from the Center with the Radius

The second way uses geometry. A tangent is always **perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact**, so the shortest distance from the center to a tangent equals the radius. A line closer to the center must pass through the inside of the circle, and a line farther away must pass outside it.

For a circle with center <InlineMath math="(a, b)" /> and the line <InlineMath math="px + qy + s = 0" />, the distance from the center to the line is

<BlockMath math="d = \frac{|pa + qb + s|}{\sqrt{p^2 + q^2}}" />

Compare <InlineMath math="d" /> with the radius <InlineMath math="r" />: <InlineMath math="d < r" /> means two intersection points, <InlineMath math="d = r" /> means one point of contact, and <InlineMath math="d > r" /> means no common point. This way needs no substitution, so it suits a question that only asks for the position. The same idea decides the [position of a point relative to a circle](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/position-of-a-point-to-a-circle), where the distance from one point to the center is compared with <InlineMath math="r" />.

## Example of a Secant and Its Intersection Points

Find the position of the line <InlineMath math="y = 2x - 1" /> relative to the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0" />, then find the intersection points.

Substitute <InlineMath math="y = 2x - 1" /> into the circle's equation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + (2x - 1)^2 - 4x \\ &amp;\quad{}+ 2(2x - 1) - 4 = 0 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + 4x^2 - 4x + 1 \\ &amp;\quad{}- 4x + 4x - 2 - 4 = 0 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="5x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

The coefficients are <InlineMath math="A = 5" />, <InlineMath math="B = -4" />, and <InlineMath math="C = -5" />, so

<BlockMath math="\Delta = (-4)^2 - 4(5)(-5) = 16 + 100 = 116" />

Since <InlineMath math="\Delta = 116 > 0" />, the line **cuts the circle at two points**. The quadratic formula gives their x-coordinates, and the line's equation gives their y-coordinates:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{116}}{10}=\frac{4\pm2\sqrt{29}}{10}=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{29}}5" />
  <BlockMath math="y=2x-1=\frac{4\pm2\sqrt{29}}5-1=\frac{-1\pm2\sqrt{29}}5" />
</MathContainer>

The upper signs go together. So the intersection points are <InlineMath math="A\left(\frac{2+\sqrt{29}}5, \frac{-1+2\sqrt{29}}5\right)" /> and <InlineMath math="B\left(\frac{2-\sqrt{29}}5, \frac{-1-2\sqrt{29}}5\right)" />. In decimals, <InlineMath math="A" /> lies near <InlineMath math="x \approx 1.48" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> near <InlineMath math="x \approx -0.68" />.

## Example of the Condition for a Tangent

The line is a tangent exactly when **its discriminant is zero**. Substitute the line into the circle:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + (x + k)^2 = 18" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + x^2 + 2kx + k^2 = 18" />
  <BlockMath math="2x^2 + 2kx + (k^2 - 18) = 0" />
</MathContainer>

The discriminant is <InlineMath math="\Delta = (2k)^2 - 4(2)(k^2 - 18) = -4k^2 + 144" />. The condition <InlineMath math="\Delta = 0" /> gives:

<MathContainer>

  <BlockMath math="k^2 = 36" />
  <BlockMath math="k = 6 \quad \text{or} \quad k = -6" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="k = 6" />, the equation becomes <InlineMath math="2x^2 + 12x + 18 = 0" />, or <InlineMath math="(x + 3)^2 = 0" />, so the point of contact is <InlineMath math="(-3, 3)" />. For <InlineMath math="k = -6" />, the point of contact is <InlineMath math="(3, -3)" />.

## A Shortcut for Circles Centered at O

For the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = r^2" />, the coefficients <InlineMath math="D" /> and <InlineMath math="E" /> are zero and <InlineMath math="F = -r^2" />. For a line that is not vertical, substituting <InlineMath math="y = mx + c" /> becomes shorter:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + (mx + c)^2 = r^2" />
  <BlockMath math="(1 + m^2)x^2 + 2mcx + (c^2 - r^2) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="\Delta = (2mc)^2 - 4(1 + m^2)(c^2 - r^2) = 4\left[r^2(1 + m^2) - c^2\right]" />
</MathContainer>

The factor <InlineMath math="4" /> is always positive, so <InlineMath math="\Delta" /> has the same sign as <InlineMath math="r^2(1 + m^2) - c^2" />. The line is a tangent exactly when <InlineMath math="c^2 = r^2(1 + m^2)" />. Divide both sides by <InlineMath math="1 + m^2" /> and take the square root: <InlineMath math="\frac{|c|}{\sqrt{1 + m^2}} = r" />. The left side is the distance from <InlineMath math="O" /> to the line <InlineMath math="mx - y + c = 0" />, so **this shortcut is the distance test in another form**.

The same condition gives the tangent with slope <InlineMath math="m" />, namely <InlineMath math="y = mx \pm r\sqrt{1 + m^2}" />, the main tool for the [equation of a tangent line to a circle](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/equation-of-a-tangent-line-to-a-circle). For the line <InlineMath math="y = x + k" /> and the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 18" />, the values <InlineMath math="m = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="r = 3\sqrt{2}" /> give <InlineMath math="c = \pm 3\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} = \pm 6" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the line's <InlineMath math="c" /> as the <InlineMath math="C" /> in <InlineMath math="\Delta" /> | The line's constant differs from the quadratic's constant term | Write out <InlineMath math="A" />, <InlineMath math="B" />, <InlineMath math="C" /> before you compute |
| Comparing <InlineMath math="d" /> with <InlineMath math="r^2" /> | <InlineMath math="d" /> is a length, <InlineMath math="r^2" /> is a squared length | For <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 9" />, compare <InlineMath math="d" /> with <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| Reading the center with flipped signs | <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0" /> has center <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" />, not <InlineMath math="(-2, 1)" /> | The center is <InlineMath math="\left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)" /> |
| Using the distance formula on <InlineMath math="y = 2x - 1" /> | The formula needs the form <InlineMath math="px + qy + s = 0" /> | Move every term to one side: <InlineMath math="2x - y - 1 = 0" /> |
| Reading the size of <InlineMath math="\Delta" /> as a distance | Multiplying the quadratic from the substitution by <InlineMath math="2" /> makes <InlineMath math="\Delta" /> four times larger but does not move the line | Only the sign of <InlineMath math="\Delta" /> matters |

The fastest check is the other method. If the discriminant and the distance test give different positions, **one calculation is wrong**, usually the center of the circle or the expansion of a square.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. Substitute <InlineMath math="y = x + 3" />:
