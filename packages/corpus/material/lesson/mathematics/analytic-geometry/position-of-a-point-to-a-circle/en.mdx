## Three Positions of a Point Relative to a Circle

A point always lies **inside the circle**, **exactly on the circle**, or **outside the circle**. To find out which, compare the point's distance to the center <InlineMath math="d" /> with the radius <InlineMath math="r" />, or compute the **power of the point** <InlineMath math="K" />. For the point <InlineMath math="A(x_0, y_0)" /> and a circle with center <InlineMath math="(a, b)" />:

<BlockMath math="K_A = (x_0 - a)^2 + (y_0 - b)^2 - r^2" />

| Power of the point | Distance to the center | Position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="K_A < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d < r" /> | Inside the circle |
| <InlineMath math="K_A = 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d = r" /> | On the circle |
| <InlineMath math="K_A > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="d > r" /> | Outside the circle |

## The Power of a Point and the Distance to the Center

The power of a point is **the squared distance to the center minus the squared radius**, <InlineMath math="K_A = d^2 - r^2" />. Factor it as <InlineMath math="K_A = (d - r)(d + r)" />. The factor <InlineMath math="d + r" /> is always positive because <InlineMath math="d \ge 0" /> and <InlineMath math="r > 0" />, so <InlineMath math="K_A" /> has the same sign as <InlineMath math="d - r" />. That is why you never need a square root: the sign of the power is enough.

For a circle in general form <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" />, the left side is the expansion of <InlineMath math="(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 - r^2" /> with <InlineMath math="a = -\frac{D}{2}" />, <InlineMath math="b = -\frac{E}{2}" />, and <InlineMath math="r^2 = a^2 + b^2 - F" />. So you get the power by **substituting the point's coordinates directly** into the left side:

<BlockMath math="K_A = x_0^2 + y_0^2 + Dx_0 + Ey_0 + F" />

This formula works only when the coefficients of <InlineMath math="x^2" /> and <InlineMath math="y^2" /> equal <InlineMath math="1" />. You read the center and radius of the general form by completing the square, as in the [equation of a circle](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/equation-of-circle).

## What the Power Means for a Point Outside

For a point outside the circle, the power is the square of a length. Draw a tangent from the point <InlineMath math="C" /> to the circle and call the point of contact <InlineMath math="T" />. The radius <InlineMath math="OT" /> is perpendicular to the tangent, so the triangle <InlineMath math="OTC" /> has a right angle at <InlineMath math="T" />, and the Pythagorean theorem gives

<BlockMath math="CT^2 = OC^2 - OT^2 = d^2 - r^2 = K_C" />

So **the power of a point equals the square of the tangent length** from that point.

## Example of a Point and a Circle in General Form

Find the position of the point <InlineMath math="A(1, -2)" /> relative to the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 8x - 2y - 8 = 0" />.

Substitute the coordinates of <InlineMath math="A" /> directly into the left side:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="K_A = 1^2 + (-2)^2 - 8(1) - 2(-2) - 8" />
  <BlockMath math="K_A = 1 + 4 - 8 + 4 - 8 = -7" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="K_A = -7 < 0" />, the point <InlineMath math="A" /> lies **inside the circle**.

## Example of a Coordinate That Puts a Point on the Circle

The point <InlineMath math="Q(k, 3)" /> and the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 20 = 0" /> are given. Find the values of <InlineMath math="k" /> for which <InlineMath math="Q" /> lies on the circle, then find when <InlineMath math="Q" /> lies inside it.

A point on the circle means **its power is zero**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="K_Q = k^2 + 9 - 4k + 6 - 20 = k^2 - 4k - 5" />
  <BlockMath math="k^2 - 4k - 5 = (k - 5)(k + 1) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="k = 5 \quad \text{or} \quad k = -1" />
</MathContainer>

The point <InlineMath math="Q" /> lies inside the circle when <InlineMath math="K_Q < 0" />, that is <InlineMath math="(k - 5)(k + 1) < 0" />. The product is negative between its two roots, so <InlineMath math="-1 < k < 5" />. Check: the circle has center <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" /> and <InlineMath math="r = 5" />, and for <InlineMath math="k = 5" /> the distance is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5" />. For <InlineMath math="k = 2" />, the power is <InlineMath math="-9" />, and <InlineMath math="2" /> does lie between <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="5" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparing <InlineMath math="d^2" /> with <InlineMath math="r" /> | A squared distance must be compared with the squared radius | For <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 16" />, compare <InlineMath math="d^2" /> with <InlineMath math="16" /> |
| Reading the center with flipped signs | <InlineMath math="(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25" /> has center <InlineMath math="(3, -2)" /> | The center is the point that makes each bracket zero |
| Substituting into <InlineMath math="2x^2 + 2y^2 - 8x = 0" /> without dividing | The result is twice the power, so the tangent length comes out wrong | Divide first until the coefficient of <InlineMath math="x^2" /> is <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Treating the power as a distance | <InlineMath math="K" /> can be negative, but a distance never is | Remember <InlineMath math="K = d^2 - r^2" /> |
| Treating a point on the circle as a point inside the circle | On the circle means exactly on the circumference, <InlineMath math="K = 0" /> | Check whether the power is exactly zero |

The fastest check is **to use both methods**: the power from direct substitution must equal <InlineMath math="d^2 - r^2" /> computed from the center and radius.

## Practice Problems

Compute the power of each point, then **check it with the point's distance to the center**.

1. Find the position of the point <InlineMath math="A(3, 4)" /> relative to the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 16" />, then find the tangent length from <InlineMath math="A" />.

2. Decide whether the point <InlineMath math="B(6, -8)" /> lies on the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 = 100" />.

3. Find the position of the point <InlineMath math="C(2, 1)" /> relative to the circle <InlineMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y - 12 = 0" />.

4. Find every value of <InlineMath math="p" /> for which the point <InlineMath math="D(p, 2)" /> lies inside the circle <InlineMath math="(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25" />.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. The power is <InlineMath math="K_A = 3^2 + 4^2 - 16 = 9" />. Since <InlineMath math="K_A > 0" />, the point <InlineMath math="A" /> lies **outside the circle**, and the tangent length is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{9} = 3" />. Check: the distance to the center <InlineMath math="d = 5" /> is greater than <InlineMath math="r = 4" />, and <InlineMath math="3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2" /> fits the right triangle <InlineMath math="OTA" />.

**Problem 2**. The power is <InlineMath math="K_B = 6^2 + (-8)^2 - 100 = 36 + 64 - 100 = 0" />. So the point <InlineMath math="B" /> lies **exactly on the circle**. Check: its distance to the center is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{100} = 10" />, equal to the radius.

**Problem 3**. Substitute directly:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="K_C = 2^2 + 1^2 - 4(2) + 6(1) - 12" />
  <BlockMath math="K_C = 4 + 1 - 8 + 6 - 12 = -9" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="K_C < 0" />, the point <InlineMath math="C" /> lies **inside the circle**. Check: the circle has center <InlineMath math="(2, -3)" /> and <InlineMath math="r^2 = 4 + 9 + 12 = 25" />, and <InlineMath math="d^2 = 0^2 + 4^2 = 16" />, so <InlineMath math="d^2 - r^2 = -9" />.

**Problem 4**. A point inside the circle has a negative power:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(p - 3)^2 + (2 + 2)^2 - 25 < 0" />
  <BlockMath math="(p - 3)^2 < 9" />
  <BlockMath math="-3 < p - 3 < 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0 < p < 6" />
</MathContainer>

Check: for <InlineMath math="p = 3" />, the power is <InlineMath math="0 + 16 - 25 = -9 < 0" />. For <InlineMath math="p = 6" />, the power is <InlineMath math="9 + 16 - 25 = 0" />, so that point lies exactly on the circle and is not included.
