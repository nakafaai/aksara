## Understanding Parabolas

If air resistance is neglected and gravity is treated as constant, the flight of a basketball follows a **parabolic** path. The arc of water from a fountain gives a similar first picture of the curve.

Its shape is also useful in technology. Satellite dishes collect signals, spotlights direct light, and parabolic arches appear in bridges and modern architecture.

The key feature is the **reflection** property. Rays arriving parallel to the axis are reflected toward the **focus**. Conversely, rays emitted from the focus leave a parabolic reflector parallel to the axis. This is why parabolic shapes can collect or direct energy effectively.

## Mathematical Definition

Mathematically, **a parabola is the locus of points that are equidistant from a fixed point and a fixed line**. The fixed point is called the **focus**, while the fixed line is called the **directrix**.

Begin with one point, the focus, and one line, the directrix. Every point whose distance to the focus equals its perpendicular distance to the directrix lies on the parabola.

This definition works for every position and orientation. The defining condition is always the same: equal distance to the focus and the directrix.

> Every point on a parabola is equally distant from the focus and the directrix.

If <InlineMath math="p > 0" />, the parabola opens right or up. If <InlineMath math="p < 0" />, it opens left or down. The **distance from the vertex to the focus** is <InlineMath math="|p|" />, and a larger <InlineMath math="|p|" /> produces a wider parabola.

For example, for the parabola <InlineMath math="y^2 = 4px" /> (opens rightward):

<MathContainer>

> The sign of <InlineMath math="p" /> determines the opening direction. In the horizontal form, positive means right and negative means left. In the vertical form, positive means up and negative means down.

## Determining Parabola Elements

When given a parabola equation, we can identify all its important elements systematically. The first step is to **recognize the parabola's orientation**:

- If variable <InlineMath math="x" /> is squared → **vertical** parabola (opens up/down)
- If variable <InlineMath math="y" /> is squared → **horizontal** parabola (opens left/right)

The following example shows the complete procedure.

**Example**: Given a parabola with equation <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x - 8y + 12 = 0" />

The first step is to convert to standard form by completing the square:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 4x - 8y + 12 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 4x = 8y - 12" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 4x + 4 = 8y - 12 + 4" />
  <BlockMath math="(x-2)^2 = 8y - 8" />
  <BlockMath math="(x-2)^2 = 8(y-1)" />
</MathContainer>

From the form <InlineMath math="(x-2)^2 = 8(y-1)" />, we identify:

- <InlineMath math="h = 2" />, <InlineMath math="k = 1" />
- <InlineMath math="4p = 8" />, so <InlineMath math="p = 2" />

Therefore, the parabola elements are:

## Example Problems

Two worked problems now connect the standard form to the vertex and focus.

**Problem** <InlineMath math="1" />: Determine the equation of a parabola that has vertex at <InlineMath math="(1, -2)" /> and focus at <InlineMath math="(4, -2)" />.

**Solution**:

Since the vertex and focus have the same <InlineMath math="y" />-coordinate, this parabola is **horizontal**.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{Vertex: } (h, k) = (1, -2)" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Focus: } (h+p, k) = (4, -2)" />
</MathContainer>

From the focus condition, we get <InlineMath math="h + p = 4" />. Substituting <InlineMath math="h = 1" />:

<BlockMath math="1 + p = 4 \Rightarrow p = 3" />

The equation for a horizontal parabola is <InlineMath math="(y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(y-(-2))^2 = 4(3)(x-1)" />
  <BlockMath math="(y+2)^2 = 12(x-1)" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem** <InlineMath math="2" />: For parabola <InlineMath math="y^2 - 6y - 4x + 13 = 0" />. Determine the coordinates of the vertex and focus.

**Solution**:

Complete the square for variable <InlineMath math="y" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="y^2 - 6y - 4x + 13 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="y^2 - 6y = 4x - 13" />
  <BlockMath math="y^2 - 6y + 9 = 4x - 13 + 9" />
  <BlockMath math="(y-3)^2 = 4x - 4" />
  <BlockMath math="(y-3)^2 = 4(x-1)" />
</MathContainer>

From the form <InlineMath math="(y-3)^2 = 4(x-1)" />, we identify:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="h = 1, \quad k = 3" />
  <BlockMath math="4p = 4 \Rightarrow p = 1" />
</MathContainer>

Therefore:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{Vertex: } (h, k) = (1, 3)" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Focus: } (h+p, k) = (1+1, 3) = (2, 3)" />
</MathContainer>
