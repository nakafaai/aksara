## Drawing an Ellipse with Two Fixed Foci

An **ellipse** is the set of points whose distances to two fixed points, the foci, always add up to <InlineMath math="2a" />, where <InlineMath math="2a" /> is larger than the distance between the foci. An ellipse centered at <InlineMath math="O" /> with a horizontal major axis has the equation <InlineMath math="\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1" /> with <InlineMath math="a \ge b > 0" />, foci at <InlineMath math="(\pm c, 0)" />, and <InlineMath math="c^2 = a^2 - b^2" />. When <InlineMath math="a = b" />, the curve becomes a [circle](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/equation-of-circle) of radius <InlineMath math="a" />.

An ellipse is the set of all points for which the <Highlight>sum of the distances to two fixed points</Highlight> is constant. Those fixed points are the **foci**. This definition can be modeled with two nails, a closed loop of string, and a pencil. Anchor the string at the nails, pull it taut with the pencil, and trace a full curve. The result is an ellipse.

The main components are:

1. **Ellipse center** is the midpoint of the ellipse, usually written with letter <InlineMath math="O" />. All measurements in the ellipse refer to this point.

2. **Foci** (<InlineMath math="F_1" /> and <InlineMath math="F_2" />) are two fixed points that define the ellipse. Their distance from each other is <InlineMath math="2c" />.

3. **Major axis** is the longest diameter, a segment through the center and both foci with endpoints on the ellipse. Its endpoints are points <InlineMath math="A_1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_2" />.

4. **Minor axis** is the shortest diameter, a segment through the center perpendicular to the major axis with endpoints on the ellipse. Its endpoints are points <InlineMath math="B_1" /> and <InlineMath math="B_2" />.

5. **Semi-major** (<InlineMath math="a" />) is half the length of the major axis, which is the distance from center to the major axis endpoint.

6. **Semi-minor** (<InlineMath math="b" />) is half the length of the minor axis, which is the distance from center to the minor axis endpoint.

> A noncircular ellipse satisfies <InlineMath math="a > b > 0" />. In the limiting case <InlineMath math="a = b" />, the two semiaxes are equal and the curve is a circle.

## Ellipse Equations

The equation form depends on the center and on whether the major axis is horizontal or vertical. Read the center from the numbers subtracted inside each square, and read the direction from the squared term with the **larger denominator**.

### From Distances to an Equation

Place the foci at <InlineMath math="(-c,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(c,0)" /> with <InlineMath math="a > c > 0" />. For a point <InlineMath math="P(x,y)" />, call the distances to the left and right foci <InlineMath math="d_1" /> and <InlineMath math="d_2" />. Write their **constant sum** as <InlineMath math="2a" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="d_1=\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2},\qquad d_2=\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}" />
  <BlockMath math="d_1+d_2=2a" />
</MathContainer>

Square <InlineMath math="d_1=2a-d_2" />. Subtracting the squared distances leaves the term <InlineMath math="4cx" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}d_1^2-d_2^2 &amp;=4a^2-4ad_2\\4cx &amp;=4a^2-4ad_2\\ad_2 &amp;=a^2-cx\end{aligned}" />

Substitute the distance formula for <InlineMath math="d_2" />, square, and collect like terms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}a^2[(x-c)^2+y^2]&amp;=(a^2-cx)^2\\a^2x^2-2a^2cx+a^2c^2+a^2y^2&amp;=a^4-2a^2cx+c^2x^2\\(a^2-c^2)x^2+a^2y^2&amp;=a^2(a^2-c^2)\end{aligned}" />

Since <InlineMath math="a^2-c^2 > 0" />, write <InlineMath math="b^2=a^2-c^2" /> and divide both sides by <InlineMath math="a^2b^2" />. This gives the **standard ellipse equation**.

<BlockMath math="b^2x^2+a^2y^2=a^2b^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1" />

## Relationships Among Ellipse Lengths

The three characteristic lengths satisfy:

<BlockMath math="c^2 = a^2 - b^2" />

In this equation, <InlineMath math="c" /> is the distance from the center to either focus.

**Eccentricity** of an ellipse is defined as:

<BlockMath math="e = \frac{c}{a}" />

For a noncircular ellipse, <InlineMath math="0 < e < 1" />. As <InlineMath math="e" /> approaches <InlineMath math="0" />, the ellipse becomes more circular. As <InlineMath math="e" /> approaches <InlineMath math="1" />, it becomes more elongated. The limiting circle has <InlineMath math="e = 0" />.

## Example of Finding the Parts of an Ellipse from Its Equation

Find the center, vertices, foci, and eccentricity of the ellipse <InlineMath math="9x^2 + 25y^2 = 225" />.

**Divide both sides by <InlineMath math="225" />** so that the right side is <InlineMath math="1" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1" />

The larger denominator sits under <InlineMath math="x^2" />, so the major axis is horizontal with <InlineMath math="a = 5" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 3" />. Then <InlineMath math="c = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4" />, the foci are <InlineMath math="(\pm 4, 0)" />, the major vertices, the ends of the major axis, are <InlineMath math="(\pm 5, 0)" />, the minor vertices are <InlineMath math="(0, \pm 3)" />, and <InlineMath math="e = \frac{4}{5}" />.

Check with the definition. The point <InlineMath math="(5, 0)" /> lies <InlineMath math="9" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> from the two foci, which add up to <InlineMath math="10 = 2a" />. The point <InlineMath math="(0, 3)" /> lies <InlineMath math="\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5" /> from each focus, which also adds up to <InlineMath math="10" />.

## Example of an Ellipse in General Form

Find the center and foci of the ellipse <InlineMath math="4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0" />.

Group the <InlineMath math="x" /> terms and the <InlineMath math="y" /> terms, then **complete the square** in each group:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 2y) = 11" />
  <BlockMath math="4(x - 2)^2 - 16 + 9(y + 1)^2 - 9 = 11" />
  <BlockMath math="4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 = 36" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 1)^2}{4} = 1" />
</MathContainer>

The center is <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" />, with <InlineMath math="a = 3" />, <InlineMath math="b = 2" />, and a horizontal major axis. Since <InlineMath math="c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt{5}" />, the foci are <InlineMath math="(2 \pm \sqrt{5}, -1)" />. Check the definition at the major vertex <InlineMath math="(5, -1)" />, an end of the major axis: its distances to the two foci are <InlineMath math="3 - \sqrt{5}" /> and <InlineMath math="3 + \sqrt{5}" />, which add up to <InlineMath math="6 = 2a" />. The point also satisfies the general form, because <InlineMath math="100 + 9 - 80 - 18 - 11 = 0" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the full axis length as <InlineMath math="a" /> | A major axis of length <InlineMath math="12" /> means <InlineMath math="a = 6" /> | <InlineMath math="a" /> is the distance from the center to a vertex |
| Writing <InlineMath math="c^2 = a^2 + b^2" /> | That formula belongs to the [hyperbola](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/hyperbola) | The definition requires <InlineMath math="2a > 2c" />, so <InlineMath math="c < a" /> and <InlineMath math="b^2 = a^2 - c^2 > 0" /> |
| Reading the axis direction from the order of the terms | The direction comes from the larger denominator | Compare the denominators once the right side is <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Reading the center of <InlineMath math="(x - 2)^2" /> as <InlineMath math="h = -2" /> | The form <InlineMath math="(x - h)^2" /> gives <InlineMath math="h = 2" /> | The center makes each square zero |
| Forgetting to divide so the right side is 1 | <InlineMath math="9x^2 + 25y^2 = 225" /> is not yet in standard form | Divide both sides by the constant on the right |

The simplest check is **the definition of an ellipse**: for every vertex, the distances to the two foci must add up to <InlineMath math="2a" />.

## Exercises

The problems provide different starting information. Distinguish **full axis lengths from semiaxes**, then use the foci or points on the curve to find the remaining parameters.

1. Find the equation of an ellipse with center at <InlineMath math="(0,0)" />, major axis length <InlineMath math="12" /> and minor axis length <InlineMath math="8" />, with horizontal major axis.

2. Given ellipse <InlineMath math="\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1" />. Find the coordinates of the foci and the eccentricity of the ellipse.

3. An ellipse has center at <InlineMath math="(3, -1)" />, foci at <InlineMath math="(3, 2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(3, -4)" />, and minor axis length <InlineMath math="6" />. Find the equation of the ellipse.

4. Find the equation of an ellipse that passes through points <InlineMath math="(4, 3)" /> and <InlineMath math="(6, 2)" /> with center at <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> and horizontal major axis.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**:

Given:
- Center at <InlineMath math="(0,0)" />
- Major axis length is <InlineMath math="12" />, so <InlineMath math="2a = 12" />, thus <InlineMath math="a = 6" />
- Minor axis length is <InlineMath math="8" />, so <InlineMath math="2b = 8" />, thus <InlineMath math="b = 4" />
- **Horizontal major axis**

Ellipse equation with **horizontal major axis**:

<BlockMath math="\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1" />

Substituting values <InlineMath math="a = 6" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 4" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{x^2}{36} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1" />

**Solution 2**:

From equation <InlineMath math="\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1" />:

An ellipse with center <InlineMath math="(0,0)" /> and **horizontal major axis** has the equation:

<BlockMath math="\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1" />

Substituting point <InlineMath math="(4,3)" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{16}{a^2} + \frac{9}{b^2} = 1 \quad \text{...(1)}" />

Substituting point <InlineMath math="(6,2)" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{36}{a^2} + \frac{4}{b^2} = 1 \quad \text{...(2)}" />

Let <InlineMath math="u = \frac{1}{a^2}" /> and <InlineMath math="v = \frac{1}{b^2}" />, then:

<MathContainer>

</MathContainer>

From equation (1): <InlineMath math="v = \frac{1-16u}{9}" />

Substituting into equation (2):

<MathContainer>

  <BlockMath math="36u + \frac{4-64u}{9} = 1" />
  <BlockMath math="324u + 4 - 64u = 9" />
  <BlockMath math="260u = 5" />
  <BlockMath math="u = \frac{1}{52}" />

Substituting back:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} v &amp;= \frac{1-16 \cdot \frac{1}{52}}{9} \\ &amp;= \frac{1-\frac{16}{52}}{9} \\ &amp;= \frac{\frac{36}{52}}{9} \\ &amp;= \frac{4}{52} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{13} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>
