## The Branches and Foci of a Hyperbola

The vertical outline of a cooling tower often resembles a hyperbola. A conical shock wave from a supersonic aircraft can also trace a hyperbolic path where it intersects the ground. A physical model may show only part of the curve. The complete mathematical hyperbola has two branches.

Geometrically, a hyperbola appears when a plane cuts both halves of a double cone. Unlike an ellipse, which is closed, a hyperbola has **two separate branches** facing away from one another.

Mathematically, a hyperbola is defined as the locus of points where the <Highlight>absolute difference of distances to two fixed points</Highlight> is always constant. These two fixed points are called the **foci** of the hyperbola. For every point <InlineMath math="P" /> on the hyperbola, the difference <InlineMath math="|PF_1 - PF_2| = 2a" /> (constant), whereas in an [ellipse](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/ellipse) the sum of distances is constant: <InlineMath math="PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a" />.

Read the graph through these five features:

1. **Center of hyperbola** is the midpoint between the two foci, usually denoted by <InlineMath math="O" />.

2. **Foci** (<InlineMath math="F_1" /> and <InlineMath math="F_2" />) are the two fixed points in the definition of a hyperbola. If <InlineMath math="c" /> is the distance from the center to either focus, the distance between the foci is <InlineMath math="2c" />.

3. **Vertices** (<InlineMath math="A_1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_2" />) are the points where each branch comes closest to the center. They lie on the transverse axis.

4. **Transverse axis** is the line segment through the center whose endpoints are the two vertices. The foci lie on the line containing this segment.

5. **Asymptotes** are lines that are approached by the branches of the hyperbola as they extend to infinity. The hyperbola never touches its asymptotes, but the farther from the center, the closer the curve approaches the asymptote lines.

> The main difference between a hyperbola and an ellipse: a hyperbola has asymptotes and consists of two separate branches, while an ellipse is a closed curve without asymptotes.

## Equation of a Hyperbola

Take <InlineMath math="a > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="b > 0" />. The constant in the definition must satisfy <InlineMath math="0 < 2a < 2c" />, where <InlineMath math="2c" /> is the distance between the foci. The standard equation depends on the orientation of the branches and the position of the center. We will use two cases. The **positive squared term** decides the direction, so a positive <InlineMath math="x^2" /> term opens left and right while a positive <InlineMath math="y^2" /> term opens up and down.

## Properties of Hyperbolas

The three parameters of every hyperbola satisfy:

<BlockMath math="c^2 = a^2 + b^2" />

In this notation:
- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the distance from center to vertex (transverse semiaxis)
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the conjugate semiaxis that determines the asymptote slopes
- <InlineMath math="c" /> is the distance from center to focus

This relationship is different from an ellipse which uses <InlineMath math="c^2 = a^2 - b^2" />.

**Eccentricity** of a hyperbola is defined as:

<BlockMath math="e = \frac{c}{a}" />

The eccentricity of a hyperbola is always <InlineMath math="e > 1" /> because <InlineMath math="c^2 = a^2 + b^2" /> implies <InlineMath math="c > a" />. More precisely, <InlineMath math="e^2 = 1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}" />, so increasing <InlineMath math="e" /> increases the ratio <InlineMath math="b/a" /> that also determines the asymptote slopes. For comparison, a circle has <InlineMath math="e = 0" />, an ellipse has <InlineMath math="0 < e < 1" />, a parabola has <InlineMath math="e = 1" />, and a hyperbola has <InlineMath math="e > 1" />.

**Asymptote equations** for a hyperbola with center at <InlineMath math="(0,0)" />:

For a horizontal transverse axis:

<BlockMath math="y = \pm \frac{b}{a}x" />
  For a vertical transverse axis:

<BlockMath math="y = \pm \frac{a}{b}x" />


In the horizontal graph above, <InlineMath math="a=3" /> and <InlineMath math="b=2" />. Compare the upper right branch with its positive asymptote at <InlineMath math="x=5" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\frac{25}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}&amp;=1\\y^2&amp;=\frac{64}{9}\\y&amp;=\frac{8}{3}\end{aligned}" />

<BlockMath math="y_{\mathrm{asym}}=\frac{2}{3}\cdot5=\frac{10}{3},\qquad\frac{10}{3}-\frac{8}{3}=\frac{2}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

Thus **approaching an asymptote** does not mean lying on it. At this coordinate, the vertical gap between the curve and the line is still <InlineMath math="2/3" /> of a unit.


> The asymptotes describe the limiting directions of the two branches. As the distance from the center grows, each branch approaches an asymptote without intersecting it.

## Exercises

Use the vertices to find the transverse semiaxis, the foci to find the focal distance, and the **positive squared term** to identify the opening direction. When the center shifts, the asymptotes must also pass through that center.

1. Determine the equation of a hyperbola with center at <InlineMath math="(0,0)" />, vertices at <InlineMath math="(\pm 2, 0)" />, and foci at <InlineMath math="(\pm 3, 0)" />.

2. Given the hyperbola <InlineMath math="\frac{x^2}{25} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1" />. Determine the coordinates of the foci, eccentricity, and asymptote equations.

3. A hyperbola has its center at <InlineMath math="(1, -2)" />, horizontal transverse axis, <InlineMath math="a = 3" />, and <InlineMath math="b = 4" />. Determine the equation of the hyperbola.

4. Determine the asymptote equations of the hyperbola <InlineMath math="\frac{(y+1)^2}{4} - \frac{(x-2)^2}{9} = 1" />.
