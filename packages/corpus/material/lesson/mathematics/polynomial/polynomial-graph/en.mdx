## How Degree and Coefficients Shape a Polynomial Graph

The graph of a polynomial function provides a visual representation of how the function's value changes as the input value <InlineMath math="x" /> changes. Its shape depends on the <Highlight>degree and coefficients</Highlight> of the function.

## Point Plotting Method

One way to begin drawing a graph is to calculate **several points** <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> pairs that satisfy the function. The points guide the sketch, but a small table alone does not determine every feature of the graph. Roots, turning points, and end behavior provide additional checks.

**Steps**:

1.  Choose several different values for <InlineMath math="x" />.
2.  Calculate the value <InlineMath math="y = P(x)" /> for each chosen <InlineMath math="x" /> value.
3.  Create a table of <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> value pairs.
4.  Plot these points on the coordinate plane.
5.  Connect the points with a smooth and continuous curve.

## General Characteristics of Polynomial Graphs

Graphs of polynomial functions are always **smooth** (no sharp corners) and **continuous** (no jumps or breaks). Their general shape is heavily influenced by the **degree** of the polynomial.

A positive-degree polynomial has a nonzero leading coefficient. A nonzero constant polynomial has degree zero. The zero polynomial has the entire horizontal axis as its graph, but its degree is undefined under this convention.

- **Degree** <InlineMath math="0" />: <InlineMath math="P(x) = c" />. The graph is a horizontal line.
- **Degree** <InlineMath math="1" />: <InlineMath math="P(x) = ax + b" />. The graph is a straight (slanted) line.
- **Degree** <InlineMath math="2" />: <InlineMath math="P(x) = ax^2 + bx + c" />. The graph is a parabola.
- **Degree** <InlineMath math="3" />: <InlineMath math="P(x) = ax^3 + \dots" />. Its two ends point in opposite vertical directions, and it can have at most two turning points.
- **Degree** <InlineMath math="4" />: The graph can have up to three 'peaks' or 'valleys'.
- **Degree** <InlineMath math="5" />: The graph can have up to four 'peaks' or 'valleys'.

For a polynomial of degree <InlineMath math="n\ge1" />, there are at most <InlineMath math="n" /> intersections with the horizontal axis and at most <InlineMath math="n-1" /> turning points. These are upper bounds, not guaranteed counts. For example, <InlineMath math="x^3" /> has no turning point.

## End Behavior

The **leading term** controls end direction because the lower-degree terms become small relative to it. For positive degree:

<BlockMath math="P(x)=a_nx^n\left(1+\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_nx}+\cdots+\frac{a_0}{a_nx^n}\right),\quad x\ne0" />

Every additional fraction tends to zero as the input magnitude grows without bound. The bracket therefore tends to one, leaving the leading term to determine the direction. The pictures show finite windows. The infinite-end statements follow from this analysis.

| Degree | Leading coefficient sign | Left end | Right end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Even | Positive | Up | Up |
| Even | Negative | Down | Down |
| Odd | Positive | Down | Up |
| Odd | Negative | Up | Down |

### Using End Behavior

**End behavior** identifies the far-left and far-right directions. Near the origin, all coefficients still affect the shape, so the curve may turn several times before following its eventual end directions.

## Exercise

Consider

<BlockMath math="p(x)=-(x-2)^2(x+1)" />

Determine its **degree and leading coefficient**, real zeros, behavior at each zero, and end behavior. Then describe the key features a correct sketch must show.

### Worked Solution

The leading terms multiply to <InlineMath math="-x^3" />, so the polynomial has degree <InlineMath math="3" /> and leading coefficient <InlineMath math="-1" />.

The **factored form** gives the zeros directly:

- <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> has multiplicity <InlineMath math="1" />, so the graph crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis there.
- <InlineMath math="x=2" /> has multiplicity <InlineMath math="2" />, so the graph touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and turns there.

Because the degree is odd and the leading coefficient is negative,

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x\to-\infty \implies p(x)\to\infty" />
  <BlockMath math="x\to\infty \implies p(x)\to-\infty" />
</MathContainer>

As one additional checkpoint, <InlineMath math="p(0)=-4" />. A correct sketch therefore rises on the far left, crosses at <InlineMath math="(-1,0)" />, passes through <InlineMath math="(0,-4)" />, touches the axis at <InlineMath math="(2,0)" />, and falls on the far right.
