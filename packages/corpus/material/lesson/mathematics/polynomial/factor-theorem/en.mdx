The polynomial <InlineMath math="x^3 - 3x^2 + 4 = (x + 1)(x - 2)^2" /> shows both cases. The factor <InlineMath math="(x + 1)" /> appears once, so the graph crosses the axis at <InlineMath math="-1" />. The factor <InlineMath math="(x - 2)" /> appears twice, so the graph comes down to <InlineMath math="0" /> at <InlineMath math="x = 2" /> and rises again without crossing the axis.

When we divide a polynomial <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> by <InlineMath math="(x - c)" />, sometimes the remainder is zero. We know from the Remainder Theorem that if the remainder is zero, then <InlineMath math="P(c) = 0" />. So, what does it mean if <InlineMath math="P(c) = 0" />?

The value <InlineMath math="c" /> that causes <InlineMath math="P(c) = 0" /> is called a **zero** or a **root** of the polynomial <InlineMath math="P(x)" />. The Factor Theorem explains the close relationship between these zeros and the factors of the polynomial.

### Statement of the Factor Theorem

Let <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> be a polynomial and <InlineMath math="c" /> be a real number.

<InlineMath math="(x - c)" /> is a **factor** of <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> **if and only if** <InlineMath math="P(c) = 0" />.

The statement works in **both directions**. A known factor makes the product zero when you substitute its root. Conversely, a zero polynomial value means the division has no remainder.

| Known information | Conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="P(x)=(x-c)H(x)" /> | <InlineMath math="P(c)=(c-c)H(c)=0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="P(c)=0" /> | <InlineMath math="P(x)=(x-c)H(x)+0" /> |

### Connection to the Remainder Theorem

The Factor Theorem is <Highlight>a special case of the Remainder Theorem</Highlight>. Recall the division algorithm:

<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x - c) \cdot H(x) + S" />

And from the Remainder Theorem, we know <InlineMath math="S = P(c)" />.

<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x - c) \cdot H(x) + P(c)" />

- If <InlineMath math="(x - c)" /> is a factor, it means <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is divisible by <InlineMath math="(x - c)" />. This only happens if the remainder is zero. Thus, <InlineMath math="S = P(c) = 0" />.
- If <InlineMath math="P(c) = 0" />, then <InlineMath math="S = 0" />. The equation becomes <InlineMath math="P(x) = (x - c) \cdot H(x) + 0" />, or <InlineMath math="P(x) = (x - c) \cdot H(x)" />. Therefore, <InlineMath math="(x - c)" /> is a factor of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />.

  B <--> C["x - c is a factor of P(x)"]

## Using the Factor Theorem to Factor Polynomials

Each **verified root** gives one linear factor. Divide the polynomial by that factor, then factor the quotient. Division by a linear factor reduces the degree by one, making the next calculation simpler.

<Mermaid
  title={<>Steps for factoring with the factor theorem</>}
  description={<>Each confirmed zero gives one linear factor and lowers the degree of the quotient.</>}
  chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Try a candidate zero c"] --> B{"P(c) = 0?"}
  B -->|"no"| A
  B -->|"yes"| C["Divide P(x) by x - c with Horner"]
  C --> D{"Can the quotient still be factored?"}
  D -->|"yes"| A
  D -->|"no"| E["Multiply all factors to check"]`}
/>

Factorization depends on the number system. Over the reals, <InlineMath math="x^2+1" /> cannot be split into linear factors because <InlineMath math="x^2+1>0" />. Retain such factors and any required leading coefficient. For example:

<BlockMath math="2x^3-2x^2+2x-2=2(x-1)(x^2+1)" />

### Factoring a Polynomial

Let <InlineMath math="P(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 13x + 10" />. When <InlineMath math="x=1" />, the value of <InlineMath math="P(1)" /> equals the **sum of all coefficients and the constant**, <InlineMath math="1 + 2 - 13 + 10=0" />. Therefore, <InlineMath math="P(1)=0" />.

**Step 1**. Confirm Zero

Calculate <InlineMath math="P(1)" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(1) = (1)^3 + 2(1)^2 - 13(1) + 10" />
  <BlockMath math="P(1) = 1 + 2 - 13 + 10" />
  <BlockMath math="P(1) = 0" />
</MathContainer>

**Step 2**. Confirm Factor

Since <InlineMath math="P(1) = 0" />, <InlineMath math="(x - 1)" /> is a factor of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />.

**Step 3**. Divide Polynomial

We use **Horner's method** to divide <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> by <InlineMath math="(x - 1)" /> (<InlineMath math="c = 1" />).

<BlockMath
  math="
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
1 & 1 & 2 & -13 & 10 \\
  &   & 1 & 3 & -10 \\
\hline
  & 1 & 3 & -10 & \boxed{0} \\
\end{array}
"
/>

The quotient is <InlineMath math="H(x) = 1x^2 + 3x - 10 = x^2 + 3x - 10" />. The remainder is <InlineMath math="0" />, as expected.

So, <InlineMath math="P(x) = (x - 1)(x^2 + 3x - 10)" />.

**Step 4**. Factor the Quotient

Factor the quadratic polynomial <InlineMath math="H(x) = x^2 + 3x - 10" />.

Choose <InlineMath math="-2" /> and <InlineMath math="5" /> because their sum is <InlineMath math="3" /> and their product is <InlineMath math="-10" />.

<BlockMath math="x^2 + 3x - 10 = (x - 2)(x + 5)" />

**Step 5**. Complete Factorization

Combine all factors.

<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 5)" />

Check the factorization by substitution: <InlineMath math="P(-5) = -125 + 50 + 65 + 10 = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="P(2) = 8 + 8 - 26 + 10 = 0" />. The zeros <InlineMath math="-5" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="2" /> give three linear factors. Their product has degree three and leading coefficient <InlineMath math="1" />, like <InlineMath math="P(x)" />, so <InlineMath math="P(x) = (x + 5)(x - 1)(x - 2)" /> exactly.

Evaluate <InlineMath math="P(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 13x + 10" /> at the integers from <InlineMath math="-5" /> to 2. The bars are 0 exactly at <InlineMath math="-5" />, 1 and 2, the three zeros behind <InlineMath math="(x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 5)" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing the factor <InlineMath math="(x + 1)" /> for the zero <InlineMath math="1" /> | The zero <InlineMath math="c" /> gives the factor <InlineMath math="(x - c)" /> | Substitute <InlineMath math="c" /> into the factor, the result must be zero |
| Stopping after the first factor | The quotient may still factor | Every factor must be linear or have no real roots, so try to factor the quotient again |
| Treating a nonzero remainder as a factor | A remainder <InlineMath math="P(c) \ne 0" /> means <InlineMath math="(x - c)" /> is not a factor | Evaluate <InlineMath math="P(c)" /> before dividing |
| Dropping the leading coefficient | Without the <InlineMath math="2" />, the product is only half of <InlineMath math="2x^3 - 2x^2 + 2x - 2" /> | Expand the factorization and compare it with the original polynomial |

The strongest check is **expanding the factorization again**. The result must equal the original polynomial exactly.

### Answer Key

**Step 1**. Show <InlineMath math="P(-1) = 0" />

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} P(-1) &amp;= (-1)^3 - 2(-1)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}- 21(-1) - 18 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(-1) = -1 - 2(1) + 21 - 18" />
  <BlockMath math="P(-1) = -1 - 2 + 21 - 18" />
  <BlockMath math="P(-1) = -3 + 3 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Proven <InlineMath math="P(-1) = 0" />.

**Step 2**. Confirm Factor

Since <InlineMath math="P(-1) = 0" />, <InlineMath math="(x - (-1)) = (x + 1)" /> is **a factor** of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />.

**Step 3**. Divide Polynomial (**Horner's Method** with <InlineMath math="c = -1" />)

<BlockMath
  math="
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
-1 & 1 & -2 & -21 & -18 \\
   &   & -1 & 3 & 18 \\
\hline
   & 1 & -3 & -18 & \boxed{0} \\
\end{array}
"
/>

The quotient is <InlineMath math="H(x) = x^2 - 3x - 18" />.

So, <InlineMath math="P(x) = (x + 1)(x^2 - 3x - 18)" />.

**Step 4**. Factor the Quotient

Factor <InlineMath math="H(x) = x^2 - 3x - 18" />.

For this quotient, <InlineMath math="-6+3=-3" /> and <InlineMath math="(-6)(3)=-18" />.

<BlockMath math="x^2 - 3x - 18 = (x - 6)(x + 3)" />

**Step 5**. Complete Factorization

<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x + 1)(x - 6)(x + 3)" />

**Problem 2**. <InlineMath math="P(2) = 8 - 12 + 4 = 0" />, so <InlineMath math="(x - 2)" /> is a factor. The Horner method with <InlineMath math="c = 2" /> on the coefficients <InlineMath math="1, -3, 0, 4" /> gives <InlineMath math="1, -1, -2, 0" />, so

<BlockMath math="x^3 - 3x^2 + 4 = (x - 2)(x^2 - x - 2) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)" />

The factor <InlineMath math="(x - 2)" /> appears twice, so <InlineMath math="2" /> is a double zero, and the graph only touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis there. Check: <InlineMath math="(x^2 - 4x + 4)(x + 1) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 4" />.

**Problem 3**. Since <InlineMath math="(x + 2)" /> is a factor, <InlineMath math="P(-2) = 0" />:

<BlockMath math="-8 + 4k + 8 - 12 = 4k - 12 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = 3" />

For <InlineMath math="k = 3" />, the Horner method with <InlineMath math="c = -2" /> on the coefficients <InlineMath math="1, 3, -4, -12" /> gives <InlineMath math="1, 1, -6, 0" />, so the quotient is <InlineMath math="x^2 + x - 6 = (x + 3)(x - 2)" /> and <InlineMath math="x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 12 = (x + 2)(x + 3)(x - 2)" />. Check by expanding: <InlineMath math="(x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 5x + 6" />, and <InlineMath math="(x^2 + 5x + 6)(x - 2) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 12" />.
