## Multiplying Every Term by Every Term

Similar to the [operations of addition and subtraction of polynomials](/en/subjects/mathematics/polynomial/addition-subtraction-polynomial), the operation of multiplication on polynomials can also be understood through the basic concepts of number multiplication and the distributive property.

The main principle in multiplying two polynomials is: multiply <Highlight>each term in the first polynomial</Highlight> by each term in the second polynomial.

After performing all the multiplications between terms, the next step is to **combine (add or subtract) like terms** to simplify the result.

## Multiplication Methods

There are several ways to perform polynomial multiplication, but all are based on the **distributive property**. The horizontal method writes every partial product in one line, and the table method places them in a grid before you combine like terms.

### Horizontal Distribution Method

Multiply **every pair of terms**, then collect like terms. In the first product, carry the negative sign into every multiplication. Since <InlineMath math="(-5)(-1)=5" />, the final constant is positive.

**Example 1**

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(x-5)(x^2+3x-1)&amp;=x(x^2+3x-1)-5(x^2+3x-1) \\ &amp;=x^3+3x^2-x-5x^2-15x+5 \\ &amp;=x^3+(3-5)x^2+(-1-15)x+5 \\ &amp;=x^3-2x^2-16x+5\end{aligned}" />

**Example 2**:

Watch the negative terms in the second factor. For example, <InlineMath math="(-2x)(-5)=10x" />, so the linear terms combine as <InlineMath math="10x+14x=24x" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(x^2-2x+7)(2x-5)&amp;=x^2(2x-5)-2x(2x-5)+7(2x-5) \\ &amp;=2x^3-5x^2-4x^2+10x+14x-35 \\ &amp;=2x^3+(-5-4)x^2+(10+14)x-35 \\ &amp;=2x^3-9x^2+24x-35\end{aligned}" />

### Table Method with an Area Model

This method organizes the multiplication of each term using a table, similar to finding the area when multiplying two numbers.

For example, the multiplication <InlineMath math="16 \times 12" /> can be seen as the area of a rectangle with sides <InlineMath math="10+6" /> and <InlineMath math="10+2" />.

|        | <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| :----- | :-- | :-- |
| <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="100" /> | <InlineMath math="60" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="20" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |

Total area is <InlineMath math="100 + 60 + 20 + 12 = 192" />.

The same approach can be applied to polynomials.

The **four area pieces** explain the four table products. Each piece takes its width from one column and its height from one row. Adding these areas gives the area of the whole rectangle.

**Example 3**:

Find the product of <InlineMath math="(x + 6)(x + 2)" /> using the table method.

|                             |  <InlineMath math="x" />  | <InlineMath math="+6" /> |
| :-------------------------- | :-----------------------: | :------------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="+6x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="+2" /> | <InlineMath math="+2x" /> | <InlineMath math="+12" /> |

Now, sum all the results inside the table cells:

<BlockMath math="(x+6)(x+2)=x^2+6x+2x+12=x^2+8x+12" />

**Example 4**:

Find the product of <InlineMath math="(x - 5)(x^2 + 3x - 1)" /> using the table method.

|                             |  <InlineMath math="x^2" />  |  <InlineMath math="+3x" />  | <InlineMath math="-1" /> |
| :-------------------------- | :-------------------------: | :-------------------------: | :----------------------: |
| <InlineMath math="x" /> |  <InlineMath math="x^3" />  | <InlineMath math="+3x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="-x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-5" /> | <InlineMath math="-5x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="-15x" />  | <InlineMath math="+5" /> |

Sum all the results inside the table cells:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(x-5)(x^2+3x-1)&amp;=x^3+3x^2-x-5x^2-15x+5 \\ &amp;=x^3-2x^2-16x+5\end{aligned}" />

A polynomial table may also contain negative terms. In that case its cells represent **algebraic products**, not negative geometric areas.

Both methods give the same result. The table method arranges the partial products so that no term is omitted.

## Exercise

Expand and simplify <InlineMath math="(2x-3)(x^2+x-4)" />. Distribute **both terms** of the first factor, then combine the terms with the same power of <InlineMath math="x" />. A linear factor times a quadratic factor has degree three, so the result must contain no higher power than <InlineMath math="x^3" />.

### Worked Solution

Distribute **both terms** of the first factor across the second factor. Keep every **partial product** visible before combining like terms:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(2x-3)(x^2+x-4)&amp;=2x(x^2+x-4)-3(x^2+x-4) \\ &amp;=2x^3+2x^2-8x-3x^2-3x+12 \\ &amp;=2x^3-x^2-11x+12\end{aligned}" />

The check is structural: a linear factor times a quadratic factor can have degree <InlineMath math="3" />, and the leading product <InlineMath math="2x\cdot x^2=2x^3" /> matches the leading term of the result.
