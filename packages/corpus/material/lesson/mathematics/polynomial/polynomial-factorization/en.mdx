## Factoring into Linear Factors over the Complex Numbers

We have learned to factor polynomials, for example using the [Factor Theorem](/en/subjects/mathematics/polynomial/factor-theorem). However, sometimes the factorization result still leaves factors that are not linear (like quadratic factors) which cannot be factored further using real numbers.

**Complete Factorization** (or Complete Linear Factorization) is the process of factoring a polynomial <Highlight>into a product of linear factors</Highlight>, where these factors may involve complex numbers.

This concept is based on the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every polynomial of degree <InlineMath math="n\ge1" /> has exactly <InlineMath math="n" /> complex roots when multiplicities are counted. Real roots are included among those complex roots.

### Complete Factorization Property of Polynomials

If <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is a polynomial of degree <InlineMath math="n \ge 1" /> with leading coefficient <InlineMath math="a_n \neq 0" />, then there exist complex numbers <InlineMath math="c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n" /> (which are the roots of <InlineMath math="P(x)" />) such that:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;P(x) \\ &amp;= a_n(x - c_1)(x - c_2)\dots(x - c_n) \end{aligned}" />

This means that every polynomial of degree <InlineMath math="n" /> can be broken down into exactly <InlineMath math="n" /> linear factors <InlineMath math="(x - \text{root})" /> multiplied by its **leading coefficient**.

## Steps for Complete Factorization

Each root gives one linear factor. Find roots by **grouping or testing rational candidates**, then use division to reduce the degree. Solve any remaining quadratic with the quadratic formula, including when its discriminant is negative.

Keep the **leading coefficient** and any repeated factors. For example, the following polynomial has three roots when repetition is counted: one, one, and minus two.

<BlockMath math="2x^3-6x+4=2(x-1)^2(x+2)" />

### Using Complete Factorization

Find all **complex zeros** of <InlineMath math="P(x) = x^3 - x^2 + x - 1" /> and factor the polynomial completely.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**. Find Roots Try to factor <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> first.

Factor by grouping:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x^3 - x^2) + (x - 1)" />
<BlockMath math="P(x) = x^2(x - 1) + 1(x - 1)" />
<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x^2 + 1)(x - 1)" />
</MathContainer>

Now, find the roots by setting <InlineMath math="P(x) = 0" />:

<BlockMath math="(x^2 + 1)(x - 1) = 0" />

This gives two possibilities:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1" />
<BlockMath math="x^2+1=0\implies x^2=-1\implies x=\pm i" />
</MathContainer>

So, the complex roots are <InlineMath math="1, i, -i" />.

**Step 2**. Form Linear Factors

From the root <InlineMath math="1" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="(x - 1)" />.
From the root <InlineMath math="i" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="(x - i)" />.
From the root <InlineMath math="-i" />, the factor is <InlineMath math="(x - (-i)) = (x + i)" />.

**Step 3**. Write Complete Factorization

The leading coefficient of <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> is <InlineMath math="a_3 = 1" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(x) = 1 \cdot (x - 1)(x - i)(x + i)" />
<BlockMath math="P(x) = (x - 1)(x - i)(x + i)" />
</MathContainer>

## Exercise

Find all **complex zeros** of <InlineMath math="P(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + x + 5" />, then factor <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> completely. Start from the possible rational roots, test them with substitution, and divide out each factor you find until only a quadratic remains.

### Answer Key

**Step 1**. Find Rational Roots (Rational Zero Theorem)

<InlineMath math="a_0 = 5" />, factors <InlineMath math="p" />: <InlineMath math="\pm 1, \pm 5" />.
<InlineMath math="a_n = 1" />, factors <InlineMath math="q" />: <InlineMath math="\pm 1" />.
**Possible roots** <InlineMath math="p/q" />: <InlineMath math="\pm 1, \pm 5" />.
Test <InlineMath math="x = -1" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} P(-1) &amp;= (-1)^3-3(-1)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+(-1)+5 \\ &amp;= -1-3(1)-1+5 \\ &amp;= -1-3-1+5 \\ &amp;= 0 \end{aligned}" />

So, <InlineMath math="x = -1" /> is a root, and <InlineMath math="(x+1)" /> is a factor.

**Step 2**. Divide using Horner's Method (<InlineMath math="c = -1" />)

<BlockMath
math="
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
-1 & 1 & -3 & 1 & 5 \\
          &   & -1 & 4 & -5 \\
\hline
          & 1 & -4 & 5 & \boxed{0} \\
\end{array}
"
/>

Quotient <InlineMath math="H(x) = x^2 - 4x + 5" />.

<InlineMath math="P(x) = (x+1)(x^2 - 4x + 5)" />.

**Step 3**. Find the roots of the quotient. Solve <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 5 = 0" /> using the **quadratic formula**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)}" />
<BlockMath math="x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="x = \frac{4 \pm 2i}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="x = 2 \pm i" />
</MathContainer>

The other roots are <InlineMath math="2 + i" /> and <InlineMath math="2 - i" />.

**Step 4**. Collect all complex roots. The roots are <InlineMath math="-1, 2+i, 2-i" />.

**Step 5**. Complete Factorization (<InlineMath math="a_n = 1" />)

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;P(x) \\ &amp;=1\cdot(x-(-1)) \\ &amp;\quad{}\cdot(x-(2+i))(x-(2-i)) \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;P(x) \\ &amp;= (x + 1)(x - 2 - i)(x - 2 + i) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>
