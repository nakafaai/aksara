## Degree of a Monomial

The monomial degree rules below assume a <Highlight>nonzero coefficient</Highlight>. The zero polynomial is treated separately because it has no nonzero term.

Each monomial within a polynomial has a characteristic called **degree**. This degree is determined by the powers (exponents) of its variables. For one variable the degree is the exponent itself, and for several variables it is the sum of all the exponents in the term.

### Degree of a Single Variable Monomial

If a monomial has only one variable, like <InlineMath math="ax^n" />, its degree is the **exponent of that variable**, which is <InlineMath math="n" />.

<BlockMath math="\deg(ax^n)=n,\quad a\neq0" />

**Examples**:

- The monomial <InlineMath math="4x^5" /> has a degree of <InlineMath math="5" />.
- The monomial <InlineMath math="0.12x" /> (or <InlineMath math="0.12x^1" />) has a degree of <InlineMath math="1" />.

### Degree of a Multiple Variable Monomial

If a monomial has more than one variable, its degree is the **sum** of all the variable exponents.

<BlockMath math="\deg(cx_1^{p_1}x_2^{p_2}\cdots x_k^{p_k})=p_1+p_2+\cdots+p_k,\quad c\neq0" />

**Examples**:

- The monomial <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{4}x^2y^7" /> has a degree of <InlineMath math="2 + 7 = 9" />.
- The monomial <InlineMath math="2.17x^3yz^3" /> (remember <InlineMath math="y = y^1" />) has a degree of <InlineMath math="3 + 1 + 3 = 7" />.

### Degree of a Constant

What about a constant (a number without variables), like <InlineMath math="5" />? A non-zero constant is considered to have a **degree** <InlineMath math="0" />, because we can write it as <InlineMath math="5x^0" /> (since <InlineMath math="x^0 = 1" />).

Here is a summary of monomial degree examples in a table:

| Monomial                                | Degree | Explanation                                                       |
| :-------------------------------------- | :----- | :---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="4x^5" />              | <InlineMath math="5" /> | The exponent of <InlineMath math="x" /> is <InlineMath math="5" />.                     |
| <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{4}x^2y^7" /> | <InlineMath math="9" /> | Sum of exponents <InlineMath math="2+7=9" />.                     |
| <InlineMath math="0.12x" />             | <InlineMath math="1" /> | The exponent of <InlineMath math="x" /> is <InlineMath math="1" />.                     |
| <InlineMath math="2.17x^3yz^3" />       | <InlineMath math="7" /> | Sum of exponents <InlineMath math="3+1+3=7" />.                   |
| <InlineMath math="10" />                | <InlineMath math="0" /> | Non-zero constant. Can be written as <InlineMath math="10x^0" />. |

The degree shapes the whole graph. Put <InlineMath math="x = -2" /> into <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="x^2" />, <InlineMath math="x^3" /> and <InlineMath math="x^4" />: the odd powers give negative values and the even powers give positive values, and each step up in degree doubles the size. That is why graphs of odd degree leave the picture in opposite directions on the left and right, while graphs of even degree rise on both sides.

## Determining the Degree of a Polynomial

The degree of a polynomial is determined from the degrees of its non-zero terms.

**The degree of a polynomial** is the **highest degree** among all the terms (monomials) that make up the polynomial.

**Steps to determine the degree of a polynomial**:

1.  Identify all the terms (monomials) in the polynomial.
2.  Determine the degree of each term.
3.  Choose the highest degree among all the terms. That is the degree of the polynomial.

Combine <Highlight>like terms before finding the degree</Highlight>. High-power terms can cancel, so reading the largest exponent before simplifying can give the wrong degree. For example:

<BlockMath math="x^4-x^4+3x^2+1=3x^2+1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\deg(3x^2+1)=2" />

After simplification, the coefficient of the fourth power is zero. The highest remaining term has degree two.

**Example 1**:

Determine the degree of the following polynomial:

<BlockMath math="8x^3 - 36x^2 + 54x - 27" />

- The term <InlineMath math="8x^3" /> has degree <InlineMath math="3" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="-36x^2" /> has degree <InlineMath math="2" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="54x" /> (or <InlineMath math="54x^1" />) has degree <InlineMath math="1" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="-27" /> (constant) has degree <InlineMath math="0" />.

The highest degree among the terms is <InlineMath math="3" />. Therefore, the degree of this polynomial is <InlineMath math="3" />.

**Example 2**:

Determine the degree of the following polynomial:

<BlockMath math="5x^4y^2 + xy^2 - 2x^5y^6" />

- The term <InlineMath math="5x^4y^2" /> has degree <InlineMath math="4 + 2 = 6" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="xy^2" /> (or <InlineMath math="x^1y^2" />) has degree <InlineMath math="1 + 2 = 3" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="-2x^5y^6" /> has degree <InlineMath math="5 + 6 = 11" />.

The highest degree among the terms is <InlineMath math="11" />. Therefore, the degree of this polynomial is <InlineMath math="11" />.

**Example 3**:

Determine the degree of the following polynomial:

<BlockMath math="0.13x^3 + 1.56x^2 - 2.24x + 1.72" />

- The term <InlineMath math="0.13x^3" /> has degree <InlineMath math="3" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="1.56x^2" /> has degree <InlineMath math="2" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="-2.24x" /> has degree <InlineMath math="1" />.
- The term <InlineMath math="1.72" /> has degree <InlineMath math="0" />.

The highest degree is <InlineMath math="3" />. Therefore, the degree of this polynomial is <InlineMath math="3" />.

The cubic <InlineMath math="8x^3 - 36x^2 + 54x - 27" /> equals <InlineMath math="(2x - 3)^3" />. Expanding with the binomial pattern gives each coefficient: <InlineMath math="2^3 = 8" />, <InlineMath math="3 \cdot 2^2 \cdot (-3) = -36" />, <InlineMath math="3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-3)^2 = 54" /> and <InlineMath math="(-3)^3 = -27" />. The bar at degree 3 is not zero, so the degree is 3, and the triple root <InlineMath math="x = 1.5" /> sits where <InlineMath math="2x - 3 = 0" />.

### Degree of the Zero Polynomial

Is the degree of <InlineMath math="0" /> equal to <InlineMath math="0" />, since <InlineMath math="0" /> can be written as <InlineMath math="0x^0" />?

Generally in mathematics:

- **Non-zero** constants (like <InlineMath math="5, -27, 1.72" />) have a degree of <InlineMath math="0" />.
- The **zero polynomial** has no non-zero term. One common convention leaves its degree **undefined**. Another assigns the formal value **negative infinity** (<InlineMath math="-\infty" />), which keeps rules such as <InlineMath math="\deg(PQ)=\deg P+\deg Q" /> valid when <InlineMath math="P" />, <InlineMath math="Q" />, or both are the zero polynomial.

The exercises below use the first convention, so the zero polynomial has undefined degree. Non-zero constants have degree <InlineMath math="0" />. Every other polynomial takes the greatest degree among its non-zero terms.

## Exercise

Determine the degree of

<BlockMath math="P(x,y)=4x^3y^2-7x^2y^5+6" />

Then explain why the same **degree rule** cannot assign degree <InlineMath math="0" /> to the zero polynomial.

### Worked Solution

Find the **total degree of each non-zero term**:

- <InlineMath math="4x^3y^2" /> has degree <InlineMath math="3+2=5" />.
- <InlineMath math="-7x^2y^5" /> has degree <InlineMath math="2+5=7" />.
- <InlineMath math="6" /> has degree <InlineMath math="0" />.

The largest term degree is <InlineMath math="7" />, so <InlineMath math="\deg P=7" />.

The zero polynomial has no non-zero term whose degree could be chosen as the largest. Its degree is therefore undefined. Assigning it degree <InlineMath math="0" /> would make some polynomial-degree rules fail.

For example, take <InlineMath math="P(x)=x" /> and <InlineMath math="Q(x)=0" />. Assigning degree zero to the zero polynomial would make the two sides of the product rule unequal:

<BlockMath math="\underbrace{\deg(x\cdot0)}_{0}\ne\underbrace{\deg x+\deg0}_{1+0=1}" />
