## A Simplified Public Health Model

Limits can describe <Highlight>the long-term behavior of a mathematical model</Highlight>. In public health, a formula must be fitted to reliable data and validated before anyone uses it to guide a real decision. The formula below shows how to calculate a limit and check the valid domain of a model.

### Inspecting the Domain of the Model

This deliberately simplified formula is used to check how far the model stays meaningful:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;N(t) \\ &amp;= 285000 - \sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3} \end{aligned}" />

Here, <InlineMath math="N(t)" /> is interpreted as a modeled case count, while <InlineMath math="t" /> is a nonnegative input whose unit must be defined for the scenario being studied.

To test whether the formula remains meaningful in the long run, calculate:

Abbreviate the square-root term as <InlineMath math="R(t)" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R(t)=\sqrt{t^2-t+(190.68)^3}" />

  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{t\to\infty}N(t) \\ &amp;=\lim_{t\to\infty}(285000-R(t)) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

To calculate the limit exactly, factor <InlineMath math="t^2" /> out of the radical. For <InlineMath math="t>0" />, its square root is <InlineMath math="t" />:

<BlockMath math="R(t)=t\sqrt{1-\frac1t+\frac{(190.68)^3}{t^2}}" />

The final square-root factor approaches <InlineMath math="1" />, while <InlineMath math="t\to\infty" />. Thus <InlineMath math="R(t)\to\infty" /> and the **case-count formula becomes negative** for sufficiently large input:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t\to\infty}N(t)=-\infty" />

> A negative case count means the formula has been used outside the model's **valid domain**. A case count cannot be negative, so the formula is meaningful only while <InlineMath math="N(t) \geq 0" />. Read this simplified formula as a mathematical example of evaluating a formula, and keep it out of medical or policy decisions.

### Inputs That Produce a Nonnegative Result

We can calculate the condition <InlineMath math="N(t)\ge0" /> instead of just stating it. Both sides being squared below are nonnegative:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="C=(190.68)^3" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}N(t)\ge0&\iff\sqrt{t^2-t+C}\le285000\\&\iff t^2-t+C\le285000^2\\&\iff0\le t\le\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4(285000^2-C)}}2\end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="t\ge0" />, the upper bound is approximately <InlineMath math="284988.34" />. The example input <InlineMath math="282367" /> lies below it. This only checks for a **nonnegative output**, not agreement with data.

## Application in Economics

A marginal quantity measures the change produced by one extra unit. The limit turns that change into an **instantaneous rate**, which is what the marginal value means once the extra unit is made arbitrarily small.

### Marginal Cost Analysis

In a differentiable cost model, **marginal cost** is the instantaneous rate at which total cost changes with output. It is defined by the derivative:

**Mathematical definition using limits**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Marginal Cost} \\ &amp;= \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x} \end{aligned}" />

The quantities in this definition are:
- <InlineMath math="C(x)" /> = total production cost function for <InlineMath math="x \text{ units}" />
- <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> = small change in production quantity
- The limit gives the **instantaneous rate of change** of cost with respect to production

When output is counted in whole units, the exact additional cost of the next unit is <InlineMath math="C(x+1)-C(x)" />. The derivative <InlineMath math="C'(x)" /> often approximates that discrete change, but the two quantities are not automatically identical.

### Population Growth Model

Take positive parameters <InlineMath math="K>0" />, <InlineMath math="A>0" />, and <InlineMath math="r>0" />. An idealized logistic population model then has the form:

<BlockMath math="P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + Ae^{-rt}}" />

Since <InlineMath math="e^{-rt} \to 0" /> as <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" />, the model approaches its **carrying-capacity parameter**:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{K}{1+A(0)}=K" />

Here, <InlineMath math="K" /> is a parameter of the simplified model. A real environment's carrying capacity can change, so the limit does not claim that every measured population will settle permanently at one fixed value.

Take <InlineMath math="A=4" /> and introduce dimensionless coordinates <InlineMath math="u=rt" /> and <InlineMath math="q=P/K" />. The graph then shows the **fraction of carrying capacity reached**:

<BlockMath math="q(u)=\frac1{1+4e^{-u}}" />

At <InlineMath math="u=0" />, <InlineMath math="q=1/5" />. At <InlineMath math="u=\ln4" />, <InlineMath math="q=1/(1+4/4)=1/2" />. The curve approaches <InlineMath math="q=1" /> without reaching it at finite time.

## Application in Technology and Science

The filter example varies frequency, whereas the reaction example varies time. Identify the **variable being approached** before evaluating the limit so that its meaning is clear.

### Frequency Response of an Analog Filter

For a **normalized first-order analog low-pass model** with cutoff angular frequency <InlineMath math="\omega_c>0" />, the magnitude response is:

<BlockMath math="|H(i\omega)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega/\omega_c)^2}}" />

Its limiting values make the idealized behavior explicit:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{\omega \to 0}|H(i\omega)|=1" />
<BlockMath math="\lim_{\omega \to \infty}|H(i\omega)|=0" />
</MathContainer>

This analog model passes very low frequencies with gain approaching one and increasingly attenuates high frequencies. At the cutoff frequency, substitution gives:

<BlockMath math="|H(i\omega_c)|=\frac1{\sqrt{1+1^2}}=\frac1{\sqrt2}" />

### Chemical Reaction Rate

For an **idealized first-order consumption** of reactant <InlineMath math="A" />, the integrated rate law is:

<BlockMath math="[A](t)=[A]_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0" />

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty}[A](t)=[A]_0\lim_{t \to \infty}e^{-kt}=0" />

This conclusion belongs to that irreversible first-order model. A reversible reaction at equilibrium generally requires a different model and need not have zero reactant concentration.

## Calculating Cases at a Vaccination Target

Let <InlineMath math="t" /> be the number of vaccinated people. The following model calculates the number of **remaining positive cases**, <InlineMath math="N(t)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;N(t) \\ &amp;= 285000 - \sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3} \end{aligned}" />

We will calculate <InlineMath math="N(282{,}367)" /> for a vaccination target of <InlineMath math="282{,}367 \text{ people}" />.

### Data and Assumptions

The calculation uses these assumptions:

- Total population is <InlineMath math="576{,}260 \text{ people}" />.
- Children make up <InlineMath math="21\%" /> of the population and adults make up <InlineMath math="79\%" />.
- The **positive-case proportion** is <InlineMath math="30\%" /> in each age group.
- In this fictional scenario, eligible adults are older than <InlineMath math="18 \text{ years}" /> and outside the positive-case group.

These assumptions give the following population counts:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Children} &amp;= 0.21 \times 576{,}260 \\ &amp;= 121{,}014.6 \\ &amp;\approx 121{,}015 \text{ people} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Adults} = 0.79 \times 576{,}260 \\ &amp;= 455{,}245.4 \approx 455{,}245 \text{ people} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Total positive cases} \\ &amp;= 0.3 \times 576{,}260 = 172{,}878 \text{ people} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Positive cases among adults} \\ &amp;= 0.79 \times 0.30 \times 576{,}260 \\  &amp;= 136{,}573.62 \\ &amp;\approx 136{,}574 \text{ people} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Eligible adults} \\ &amp;= 0.79 \times 0.70 \times 576{,}260 \\  &amp;= 318{,}671.78 \\ &amp;\approx 318{,}672 \text{ people} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Using the unrounded eligible count, the target represents:

<BlockMath math="\frac{282{,}367}{318{,}671.78}\times 100\% \approx 88.6\%" />

The target is about <InlineMath math="88.6\%" /> of the eligible adults in this calculation. This simplified formula only shows how to evaluate the function at that target. It cannot determine a real vaccination target.

### Calculation

To calculate the model's case count, we use <InlineMath math="t=282{,}367" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R_0 &amp;=(282367)^2-282367 +(190.68)^3 \end{aligned}" />

  <BlockMath math="N(282367)=285000-\sqrt{R_0}" />
</MathContainer>

**Step 1** calculates the constant term.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;(190.68)^3 \\ &amp;= 190.68 \times 190.68 \times 190.68 \\ &amp;\approx 6{,}932{,}907.88 \end{aligned}" />

The displayed value is rounded to two decimal places. The next calculation retains the unrounded value internally.

**Step 2** calculates the square of the vaccination count.

<BlockMath math="(282{,}367)^2 = 79{,}731{,}122{,}689" />

**Step 3** combines the values under the square root.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;79731122689-282367 \\ &amp;\quad{}+6932907.88 \\ &amp;=79737773229.88 \end{aligned}" />

**Step 4** gives the final result.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="R_0\approx79737773229.88" />

  <BlockMath math="N(282367)=285000-\sqrt{R_0}" />
  <BlockMath math="\approx 285{,}000 - 282{,}378.78" />
  <BlockMath math="= 2{,}621.22 \approx 2{,}621 \text{ people}" />
</MathContainer>

### Meaning of the Result

If <InlineMath math="282{,}367" /> people are vaccinated, the supplied formula gives approximately <InlineMath math="2{,}621" /> remaining cases. This result comes only from the simplified formula. Real data is still required for a forecast or a decision about health-resource allocation.

The **initial values do not agree**: the formula gives <InlineMath math="N(0)\approx282367" />, while the population assumptions give <InlineMath math="172878" /> initial cases. This example therefore practices evaluating and questioning a model. The formula result cannot be treated as a forecast for the stated population.

## Limits of the Formula and Model

The value <InlineMath math="2{,}621 \text{ cases}" /> answers the mathematical question for the supplied formula. A real decision would still require independently validated evidence about:

1. Current and projected **hospital capacity**
2. Available **medical personnel** and workload
3. **Resource allocation** under several credible scenarios
4. Uncertainty and a truthful **public communication strategy**

> A limit describes the behavior of a formula. Real data is still needed to determine whether the formula matches actual conditions. State the model's assumptions, valid domain, units, and uncertainty when explaining the calculated result.

## Exercises

Each problem gives a situation and asks for the **value a quantity approaches**. Identify the quantity first, then evaluate the limit.

1. A pharmaceutical company models cumulative vaccine production with <InlineMath math="P(t) = 50000(1 - e^{-0.1t})" /> for <InlineMath math="t \geq 0" />. Determine the long-term production ceiling predicted by the model.

2. The modeled cumulative reach of a social-media message is <InlineMath math="I(t) = \frac{100000t}{t + 50}" /> for <InlineMath math="t \geq 0" />. Calculate the limit when <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" /> and explain the long-run reach predicted by the model.

3. The total cost model for producing <InlineMath math="x" /> masks is <InlineMath math="C(x) = 1000 + 5x + 0.01x^2" />. Determine the marginal-cost function using the limit definition, then compare it with the exact cost of increasing output from <InlineMath math="x" /> to <InlineMath math="x+1" />.

### The Cumulative Production Limit

The model's **long-term ceiling** is obtained by calculating the limit when <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} 50000(1 - e^{-0.1t})" />
<BlockMath math="= 50000 \lim_{t \to \infty} (1 - e^{-0.1t})" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= 50000(1 - 0) \\ &amp;= 50000 \text{ vaccine units} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The modeled cumulative production approaches <InlineMath math="50{,}000" /> vaccine units. It does not equal that value at any finite <InlineMath math="t" /> because <InlineMath math="e^{-0.1t}>0" /> for finite <InlineMath math="t" />.

### The Message Reach Limit

Divide the numerator and denominator by <InlineMath math="t" /> to identify the **term that approaches zero**. This division is valid for <InlineMath math="t>0" />, as required near this limit.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} I(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000t}{t + 50}" />
<BlockMath math="= \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000t}{t(1 + \frac{50}{t})} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000}{1 + \frac{50}{t}}" />
<BlockMath math="= \frac{100000}{1 + 0} = 100000 \text{ people}" />
</MathContainer>

Within the model, cumulative reach approaches <InlineMath math="100{,}000 \text{ people}" /> as time grows. That number is the model's saturation level. A real message may reach a different audience because its conditions can change.

### Marginal Cost and One Whole Unit

Marginal cost is the derivative of the cost function, which can be calculated using the limit definition:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\text{Marginal Cost} \\ &amp;= \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**Detailed steps**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} C(x + \Delta x) &amp;= 1000 + 5(x + \Delta x) \\ &amp;\quad{}+ 0.01(x + \Delta x)^2 \end{aligned}" />

We expand:

<BlockMath math="(x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2" />

Then we substitute:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;C(x + \Delta x) \\ &amp;= 1000 + 5x \\ &amp;\quad{}+ 5\Delta x + 0.01x^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ 0.02x\Delta x + 0.01(\Delta x)^2 \end{aligned}" />

So,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;C(x + \Delta x) - C(x) \\ &amp;= 5\Delta x \\ &amp;\quad{}+ 0.02x\Delta x + 0.01(\Delta x)^2 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x} \\ &amp;= 5 + 0.02x + 0.01\Delta x \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (5 + 0.02x + 0.01\Delta x) \\ &amp;= 5 + 0.02x \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Thus, the marginal-cost function is <InlineMath math="C'(x)=5+0.02x" /> currency units per output unit.

The exact cost of the next whole unit is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="C(x+1)-C(x)" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;=1000 +5(x+1) \\ &amp;\quad{}+0.01(x+1)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}-(1000+5x+0.01x^2) \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="=5.01+0.02x" />
</MathContainer>

The derivative underestimates this particular one-unit increment by <InlineMath math="0.01" /> currency unit. That comparison shows why marginal cost is an instantaneous rate and only an approximation to a discrete next-unit cost.
