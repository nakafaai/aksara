## A Simplified Public Health Model

Limits can describe the long-term behavior of a mathematical model. In public health, a formula must be fitted to reliable data and validated before anyone uses it to guide a real decision. The formula below shows how to calculate a limit and check the valid domain of a model.

### Inspecting the Domain of the Model

Consider this deliberately simplified function:

<BlockMath math="N(t) = 285000 - \sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3}" />

Here, <InlineMath math="N(t)" /> is interpreted as a modeled case count, while <InlineMath math="t" /> is a nonnegative input whose unit must be defined for the scenario being studied.

To test whether the formula remains meaningful in the long run, calculate:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} N(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \left(285000 - \sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3}\right)" />

**Long-term behavior:**

When <InlineMath math="t" /> is very large, the term <InlineMath math="t^2" /> will dominate inside the root because:
- <InlineMath math="t^2" /> grows faster than <InlineMath math="t" /> and the constant <InlineMath math="(190.68)^3" />
- For <InlineMath math="t \gg 1" />, <InlineMath math="t^2 - t + (190.68)^3 \approx t^2" />

So,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3} \approx \sqrt{t^2} = |t| = t \quad \text{(for } t > 0\text{)}" />
<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} N(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} (285000 - t) = -\infty" />
</MathContainer>

> A negative case count means the formula has been used outside the model's **valid domain**. A case count cannot be negative, so the formula is meaningful only while <InlineMath math="N(t) \geq 0" />. This simplified formula is a mathematical example, not an epidemiological forecast. It must not be used as medical or policy guidance.

## Application in Economics

### Marginal Cost Analysis

In a differentiable cost model, **marginal cost** is the instantaneous rate at which total cost changes with output. It is defined by the derivative:

**Mathematical definition using limits:**

<BlockMath math="\text{Marginal Cost} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x}" />

The quantities in this definition are:
- <InlineMath math="C(x)" /> = total production cost function for <InlineMath math="x \text{ units}" />
- <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> = small change in production quantity
- The limit gives the **instantaneous rate of change** of cost with respect to production

When output is counted in whole units, the exact additional cost of the next unit is <InlineMath math="C(x+1)-C(x)" />. The derivative <InlineMath math="C'(x)" /> often approximates that discrete change, but the two quantities are not automatically identical.

### Population Growth Model

An idealized logistic population model can be written as:

<BlockMath math="P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + Ae^{-rt}}, \qquad K > 0,\ A > 0,\ r > 0" />

Since <InlineMath math="e^{-rt} \to 0" /> as <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" />, the model approaches its **carrying-capacity parameter**:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \frac{K}{1+A(0)}=K" />

Here, <InlineMath math="K" /> is a parameter of the simplified model. A real environment's carrying capacity can change, so the limit does not claim that every measured population will settle permanently at one fixed value.

## Application in Technology and Science

### Digital Signal Analysis

For a **normalized first-order low-pass model** with cutoff frequency <InlineMath math="\omega_c > 0" />, the magnitude response is:

<BlockMath math="|H(i\omega)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega/\omega_c)^2}}" />

Its limiting values make the idealized behavior explicit:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{\omega \to 0}|H(i\omega)|=1" />
<BlockMath math="\lim_{\omega \to \infty}|H(i\omega)|=0" />
</MathContainer>

This model passes very low frequencies with unit gain and increasingly attenuates high frequencies. It describes one kind of mathematical filter. Other digital filters can follow different rules.

### Chemical Reaction Rate

For an **idealized first-order consumption** of reactant <InlineMath math="A" />, the integrated rate law is:

<BlockMath math="[A](t)=[A]_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0" />

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty}[A](t)=[A]_0\lim_{t \to \infty}e^{-kt}=0" />

This conclusion belongs to that irreversible first-order model. A reversible reaction at equilibrium generally requires a different model and need not have zero reactant concentration.

## Calculating Cases at a Vaccination Target

Let <InlineMath math="t" /> be the number of vaccinated people. The following model calculates the number of remaining positive cases, <InlineMath math="N(t)" />:

<BlockMath math="N(t) = 285000 - \sqrt{t^2 - t + (190.68)^3}" />

We will calculate <InlineMath math="N(282{,}367)" /> for a vaccination target of <InlineMath math="282{,}367 \text{ people}" />.

### Data and Assumptions

The calculation uses these assumptions:

- Total population is <InlineMath math="576{,}260 \text{ people}" />.
- Children make up <InlineMath math="21\%" /> of the population and adults make up <InlineMath math="79\%" />.
- The positive-case proportion is <InlineMath math="30\%" /> in each age group.
- Only adults outside the positive-case group are eligible for vaccination.

These assumptions give the following population counts:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Children} = 0.21 \times 576{,}260 = 121{,}014.6 \approx 121{,}015 \text{ people}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Adults} = 0.79 \times 576{,}260 = 455{,}245.4 \approx 455{,}245 \text{ people}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Total positive cases} = 0.3 \times 576{,}260 = 172{,}878 \text{ people}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Positive cases among adults} = 0.79 \times 0.30 \times 576{,}260 = 136{,}573.62 \approx 136{,}574 \text{ people}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Eligible adults} = 0.79 \times 0.70 \times 576{,}260 = 318{,}671.78 \approx 318{,}672 \text{ people}" />
</MathContainer>

Using the unrounded eligible count, the target represents:

<BlockMath math="\frac{282{,}367}{318{,}671.78}\times 100\% \approx 88.6\%" />

The target is about <InlineMath math="88.6\%" /> of the eligible adults in this calculation. This simplified formula only shows how to evaluate the function at that target. It cannot determine a real vaccination target.

### Calculation

To calculate the model's case count, we use <InlineMath math="t=282{,}367" />:

<BlockMath math="N(282{,}367) = 285{,}000 - \sqrt{(282{,}367)^2 - 282{,}367 + (190.68)^3}" />

**Step** <InlineMath math="1" /> calculates the constant term.

<BlockMath math="(190.68)^3 = 190.68 \times 190.68 \times 190.68 = 6{,}932{,}907.88" />

The displayed value is rounded to two decimal places. The next calculation retains the unrounded value internally.

**Step** <InlineMath math="2" /> calculates the square of the vaccination count.

<BlockMath math="(282{,}367)^2 = 79{,}731{,}122{,}689" />

**Step** <InlineMath math="3" /> combines the values under the square root.

<BlockMath math="79{,}731{,}122{,}689 - 282{,}367 + 6{,}932{,}907.88 = 79{,}737{,}773{,}229.88" />

**Step** <InlineMath math="4" /> gives the final result.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="N(282{,}367) = 285{,}000 - \sqrt{79{,}737{,}773{,}229.88}" />
<BlockMath math="= 285{,}000 - 282{,}378.78" />
<BlockMath math="= 2{,}621.22 \approx 2{,}621 \text{ people}" />
</MathContainer>

### Meaning of the Result

If <InlineMath math="282{,}367" /> people are vaccinated, the supplied formula gives approximately <InlineMath math="2{,}621" /> remaining cases. This result comes only from the simplified formula. Real data is still required for a forecast or a decision about health-resource allocation.

## Limits of the Formula and Model

The value <InlineMath math="2{,}621 \text{ cases}" /> answers the mathematical question for the supplied formula. A real decision would still require independently validated evidence about:

1. Current and projected **hospital capacity**
2. Available **medical personnel** and workload
3. **Resource allocation** under several credible scenarios
4. Uncertainty and a truthful **public communication strategy**

> A limit describes the behavior of a formula. Real data is still needed to determine whether the formula matches actual conditions. State the model's assumptions, valid domain, units, and uncertainty when explaining the calculated result.

## Exercises

1. A pharmaceutical company models cumulative vaccine production with <InlineMath math="P(t) = 50000(1 - e^{-0.1t})" /> for <InlineMath math="t \geq 0" />. Determine the long-term production ceiling predicted by the model.

2. The modeled cumulative reach of a social-media message is <InlineMath math="I(t) = \frac{100000t}{t + 50}" /> for <InlineMath math="t \geq 0" />. Calculate the limit when <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" /> and explain the long-run reach predicted by the model.

3. The total cost model for producing <InlineMath math="x" /> masks is <InlineMath math="C(x) = 1000 + 5x + 0.01x^2" />. Determine the marginal-cost function using the limit definition, then compare it with the exact cost of increasing output from <InlineMath math="x" /> to <InlineMath math="x+1" />.

### Worked Solutions

1. **Solution:**

   The model's long-term ceiling is obtained by calculating the limit when <InlineMath math="t \to \infty" />:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} 50000(1 - e^{-0.1t})" />
   <BlockMath math="= 50000 \lim_{t \to \infty} (1 - e^{-0.1t})" />
   <BlockMath math="= 50000(1 - 0) = 50000 \text{ vaccine units}" />
   </MathContainer>

   The modeled cumulative production approaches <InlineMath math="50{,}000" /> vaccine units. It does not equal that value at any finite <InlineMath math="t" /> because <InlineMath math="e^{-0.1t}>0" /> for finite <InlineMath math="t" />.

2. **Solution:**

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="\lim_{t \to \infty} I(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000t}{t + 50}" />
   <BlockMath math="= \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000t}{t(1 + \frac{50}{t})} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{100000}{1 + \frac{50}{t}}" />
   <BlockMath math="= \frac{100000}{1 + 0} = 100000 \text{ people}" />
   </MathContainer>

   Within the model, cumulative reach approaches <InlineMath math="100{,}000 \text{ people}" /> as time grows. That number is the model's saturation level. A real message may reach a different audience because its conditions can change.

3. **Solution:**

   Marginal cost is the derivative of the cost function, which can be calculated using the limit definition:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="\text{Marginal Cost} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x}" />
   </MathContainer>

   **Detailed steps:**

   <BlockMath math="C(x + \Delta x) = 1000 + 5(x + \Delta x) + 0.01(x + \Delta x)^2" />

   We expand:

   <BlockMath math="(x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2" />

   Then we substitute:

   <BlockMath math="C(x + \Delta x) = 1000 + 5x + 5\Delta x + 0.01x^2 + 0.02x\Delta x + 0.01(\Delta x)^2" />

   So,

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="C(x + \Delta x) - C(x) = 5\Delta x + 0.02x\Delta x + 0.01(\Delta x)^2" />
   <BlockMath math="\frac{C(x + \Delta x) - C(x)}{\Delta x} = 5 + 0.02x + 0.01\Delta x" />
   <BlockMath math="\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} (5 + 0.02x + 0.01\Delta x) = 5 + 0.02x" />
   </MathContainer>

   Thus, the marginal-cost function is <InlineMath math="C'(x)=5+0.02x" /> currency units per output unit.

   The exact cost of the next whole unit is:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="C(x+1)-C(x)" />
   <BlockMath math="=1000+5(x+1)+0.01(x+1)^2-(1000+5x+0.01x^2)" />
   <BlockMath math="=5.01+0.02x" />
   </MathContainer>

   The derivative underestimates this particular one-unit increment by <InlineMath math="0.01" /> currency unit. That comparison shows why marginal cost is an instantaneous rate and only an approximation to a discrete next-unit cost.
