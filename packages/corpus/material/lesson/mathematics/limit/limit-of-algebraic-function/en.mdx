## Choosing a Method from the Algebraic Expression

For an **algebraic limit**, the form of the expression determines which operation is justified. <Highlight>A polynomial can be evaluated by direct substitution</Highlight>. A rational or radical expression may instead contain a removable factor, a domain boundary, or a vertical asymptote that must be analyzed first.

Many examples of algebraic functions can be formed using addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and powers with rational exponents. Examples include the polynomial <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 3x - 2" /> and the rational function <InlineMath math="g(x) = \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}" />. We will use substitution as a diagnostic step, then factor, rationalize, or check one-sided behavior when substitution does not settle the limit.

## Properties of Algebraic Limits

An algebraic limit can be reduced to **limits of simpler terms**. Applying the same rules repeatedly means working term by term, and each step handles one simple part of the whole expression.

### Limits of Polynomial Functions

For polynomial functions that are **continuous** at all points, calculating limits is very simple. We can directly **substitute** the approaching value.

Let <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7" />, then:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7)" />

Since polynomial functions are continuous at all points, we can substitute directly:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= 2^4 - 5(2^3) + 2^2 - 7 \\ &amp;= 16 - 40 + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= -27 \end{aligned}" />

### Limits of Rational Functions

Rational functions have the form <InlineMath math="\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}" /> where <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> and <InlineMath math="Q(x)" /> are polynomials. Direct substitution tells us which method to try next:

- **If the denominator is not zero**: Use direct substitution like polynomial functions.

- **If the denominator is zero**: Check the numerator, signs, and one-sided behavior. Only <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" /> is an indeterminate form that may be resolved by algebraic simplification. A nonzero numerator over zero is not indeterminate.

## Handling Indeterminate Forms

When **direct substitution** yields the form <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />, we need to use special techniques. Factoring and rationalizing both change the expression until the common factor that produces the zero in the denominator cancels, and then substitution works again.

### Factoring Technique

A **common factor** can be canceled only at inputs where that factor is nonzero. The simplified expression agrees with the original near the limit point, even if the original function is undefined at that point.

**Example**: Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}" />

Direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />. Factor:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x^2 - 4 = (x + 2)(x - 2)" />
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x + 2)(x - 2)}{x - 2}" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="x" /> approaches <InlineMath math="2" /> (not equal to <InlineMath math="2" />), we can cancel the factor <InlineMath math="(x - 2)" />:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}= \lim_{x \to 2} (x + 2) = 2 + 2 = 4" />

### Rationalization Technique

For limits involving radical forms, we often need to rationalize. Direct substitution yields an indeterminate form.

**Example**: Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 1}{x - 1}" />

Direct substitution:

The numerator <InlineMath math="\sqrt1-1=0" /> and denominator <InlineMath math="1-1=0" /> both vanish. The indeterminate form <InlineMath math="0/0" /> does not yet give the limit.

We rationalize by multiplying with the **conjugate** <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x} + 1" />. The purpose is to eliminate the radical form in the numerator using the formula <InlineMath math="(a-b)(a+b) = a^2 - b^2" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 1}{x - 1} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x} + 1}{\sqrt{x} + 1}" />
<BlockMath math="= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(\sqrt{x} - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)}{(x - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(\sqrt{x})^2 - 1^2}{(x - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)} \\ &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x - 1}{(x - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="x \neq 1" /> (approaching <InlineMath math="1" />), we can cancel <InlineMath math="(x - 1)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x} + 1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1} + 1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{1 + 1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2} \end{aligned}" />

## Application of Limit Properties to Algebraic Functions

The limit rules can be applied one after another until **direct substitution** produces a defined value. Each step uses one rule, and the expression becomes simpler at every stage.

### Factoring the Numerator and Denominator

**Example**: Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x^2 + 2x - 15}" />

Direct substitution gives the indeterminate form <InlineMath math="\frac00" />: both numerator and denominator are zero.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="3^2-9=9-9=0" />

  <BlockMath math="3^2+2(3)-15=9+6-15=0" />
</MathContainer>

Factor both. For <InlineMath math="x^2 + 2x - 15" />, we find two numbers that when multiplied give <InlineMath math="-15" /> and when added give <InlineMath math="2" />. Those numbers are <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="-3" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + 2x - 15 \\ &amp;= x^2 + 5x - 3x - 15 \\ &amp;= x(x + 5) - 3(x + 5) \\ &amp;= (x - 3)(x + 5) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{(x - 3)(x + 5)} = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x + 3}{x + 5}" />
</MathContainer>

Substitute <InlineMath math="x = 3" />: <InlineMath math="\frac{3 + 3}{3 + 5} = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}" />

> If direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac{a}{0}" /> with <InlineMath math="a \neq 0" />, that result alone is not enough. Check the sign and both one-sided limits. For example:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2^-}\frac{1}{x-2}=-\infty" />
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2^+}\frac{1}{x-2}=+\infty" />
</MathContainer>

Because the one-sided limits differ, <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 2}\frac{1}{x-2}" /> does not exist as a two-sided limit. By contrast, if substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />, first factor, rationalize, or otherwise simplify the expression.

Watch the **denominator sign**. To the left of <InlineMath math="2" />, the negative denominator approaches zero and the function tends to <InlineMath math="-\infty" />. To the right, the denominator is positive and the function tends to <InlineMath math="+\infty" />. Do not connect the branches across the asymptote.

## Continuity and Limits

A function <InlineMath math="f" /> is said to be **continuous** at <InlineMath math="x = c" /> if:

1. <InlineMath math="f(c)" /> exists (is defined)
2. <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to c} f(x)" /> exists
3. <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)" />

Polynomial functions are continuous at every real number. A rational function is continuous at each point in its domain, namely wherever its denominator is nonzero.

For example, <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)^2" /> has no hole and no jump at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />. The limit 0 equals the function value <InlineMath math="f(1) = 0" />, which is exactly what continuity at 1 means.

## Exercises

Each problem asks for a limit of an algebraic expression. Identify the form first, because a zero over zero result needs **factoring or rationalising**.

1. Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7)" />

2. Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 6x + 8}{x^2 + 16x + 28}" />

3. Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{x - 1}" />

4. Determine whether the function <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 1" /> is continuous at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />

5. Calculate <InlineMath math="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x}" />

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. Since this is a polynomial function that is **continuous at all points**, we can substitute directly:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7) \\ &amp;= 2^4 - 5(2^3) + 2^2 - 7 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= 16 - 5(8) + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= 16 - 40 + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= -27 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 2**. Since this is a rational function with a non-zero denominator at <InlineMath math="x = 2" />, use **direct substitution**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 6x + 8}{x^2 + 16x + 28} \\ &amp;= \frac{2^2 - 6(2) + 8}{2^2 + 16(2) + 28} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="= \frac{4 - 12 + 8}{4 + 32 + 28} = \frac{0}{64} = 0" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 3**. **Direct substitution** gives <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />. Factor the numerator:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)^2" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)^2}{x - 1} &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} (x - 1) \\ &amp;= 1 - 1 \\ &amp;= 0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 4**. To check continuity at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />, check three conditions:

| Condition | Check |
| --- | --- |
| The function value exists | <InlineMath math="f(1)=1^2-2(1)+1=0" /> |
| The limit exists | <InlineMath math="\lim_{x\to1}(x^2-2x+1)=1-2+1=0" /> |
| The limit equals the function value | <InlineMath math="\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=0=f(1)" /> |

Since all three continuity conditions are satisfied, the function is **continuous** at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />.

**Problem 5**. Direct substitution: <InlineMath math="\frac{\sqrt{0 + 4} - 2}{0} = \frac{2 - 2}{0} = \frac{0}{0}" /> (indeterminate form).

Use rationalization by multiplying with the **conjugate** <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x + 4} + 2" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{x + 4})^2 - 2^2}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \\ &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x + 4) - 4}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \\ &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0 + 4} + 2} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{\sqrt{4} + 2} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2 + 2} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{4} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Compare Problem 3: <InlineMath math="\frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{x - 1}" /> simplifies to <InlineMath math="x - 1" />, but the graph loses the point at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />. It is the line <InlineMath math="y = x - 1" /> with a hole at <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" />, and the limit is the height of the hole, 0.
