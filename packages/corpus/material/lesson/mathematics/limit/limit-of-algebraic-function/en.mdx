## Picking an Algebraic Method

For an **algebraic limit**, the structure of the expression decides which step is valid. <Highlight>A polynomial can be evaluated by direct substitution</Highlight>. A rational or radical expression may instead hide a removable factor, a domain boundary, or an asymptote that must be analyzed first.

Algebraic expressions are built from addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and rational powers, such as these polynomial and rational examples:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 3x - 2" />
  <BlockMath math="g(x) = \frac{x + 1}{x - 2}" />
</MathContainer>

Use direct substitution first as a diagnostic check, then factor, rationalize, or inspect one-sided limits whenever substitution does not settle the answer.

## Properties of Algebraic Limits

An algebraic limit breaks down into **limits of simpler terms**. Applying the limit laws term by term lets each step handle one elementary part of the expression.

### Polynomial Limits

Because polynomial functions are **continuous** everywhere, substitute the target value directly. Take the polynomial:

<BlockMath math="f(x) = x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7" />

Evaluating its limit as <InlineMath math="x \to 2" /> replaces <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="2" /> directly:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7)" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= 2^4 - 5(2^3) + 2^2 - 7 \\ &amp;= 16 - 40 + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= -27 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

### Rational Limits

Rational functions take the quotient form <InlineMath math="\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}" /> where <InlineMath math="P(x)" /> and <InlineMath math="Q(x)" /> are polynomials. Testing the denominator reveals which path applies:

- **Nonzero denominator**: Evaluate directly just like a polynomial.
- **Zero denominator**: Check the numerator, signs, and one-sided behavior. Only <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" /> is an indeterminate form that algebraic simplification can resolve. A nonzero numerator over zero signals infinite behavior.

## Handling Indeterminate Forms

When **direct substitution** produces <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />, the numerator and denominator share a hidden zero factor. Factoring polynomials and rationalizing radicals both reveal and cancel that shared factor so direct substitution works again.

### Factoring Technique

A **common factor** cancels only for inputs where that factor is nonzero, so the simplified expression agrees with the original everywhere near the target point. Evaluate:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}" />

Direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />, so factor the numerator first:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 4 = (x + 2)(x - 2)" />
  <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x + 2)(x - 2)}{x - 2}" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="x" /> approaches <InlineMath math="2" /> with <InlineMath math="x \neq 2" />, cancel <InlineMath math="(x - 2)" /> and substitute:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}= \lim_{x \to 2} (x + 2) = 2 + 2 = 4" />

### Rationalizing Radicals

Limits containing square roots often require multiplying by a **conjugate** to clear the radical. Evaluate:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 1}{x - 1}" />

Direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="0" /> in both numerator and denominator, forming the indeterminate ratio <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x} + 1" /> using the difference of squares:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2" />
  <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 1}{x - 1} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x} + 1}{\sqrt{x} + 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(\sqrt{x})^2 - 1^2}{(x - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x - 1}{(x - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 1)}" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="x \neq 1" />, cancel <InlineMath math="(x - 1)" /> and evaluate:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x} + 1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1} + 1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2} \end{aligned}" />

## Combined Limits and Poles

Apply factoring and cancellation step by step until **direct substitution** yields a defined value. Evaluate:

<BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x^2 + 2x - 15}" />

Direct substitution gives <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" /> because both numerator and denominator vanish at <InlineMath math="x = 3" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="3^2 - 9 = 9 - 9 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="3^2 + 2(3) - 15 = 9 + 6 - 15 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Factor both quadratics using <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="-3" /> for the denominator:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x^2 + 2x - 15 \\ &amp;= x^2 + 5x - 3x - 15 \\ &amp;= (x - 3)(x + 5) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Cancel <InlineMath math="(x - 3)" /> and substitute <InlineMath math="x = 3" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{(x - 3)(x + 5)} = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x + 3}{x + 5}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{3 + 3}{3 + 5} = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}" />
</MathContainer>

> If direct substitution produces <InlineMath math="\frac{a}{0}" /> with <InlineMath math="a \neq 0" />, the ratio is not indeterminate. Inspect the denominator sign from both sides to determine the one-sided infinite limits.

## Continuity and Limits

A function <InlineMath math="f" /> is **continuous** at <InlineMath math="x = c" /> when three conditions hold together:

1. The function value <InlineMath math="f(c)" /> is defined.
2. The two-sided limit exists:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to c} f(x)" />

3. The limit matches the function value:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)" />

Polynomials are continuous at every real number, while rational functions are continuous wherever their denominator is nonzero. Consider the quadratic function:

<BlockMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)^2" />

At <InlineMath math="x = 1" />, there is neither a hole nor a jump, and the limit <InlineMath math="0" /> matches <InlineMath math="f(1) = 0" />.

## Checking Algebraic Limits

Identify the form of each expression before computing, because <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" /> requires **factoring or rationalizing**.

1. Evaluate the polynomial limit:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7)" />

2. Evaluate the rational limit:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 6x + 8}{x^2 + 16x + 28}" />

3. Evaluate the indeterminate rational limit:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{x - 1}" />

4. Check whether the polynomial below satisfies continuity at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />:

   <BlockMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 1" />

5. Evaluate the radical limit:

   <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x}" />

Check your work against the worked solutions below.

**Problem 1**. Because this polynomial is **continuous everywhere**, substitute directly:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{x \to 2} (x^4 - 5x^3 + x^2 - 7) \\ &amp;= 2^4 - 5(2^3) + 2^2 - 7 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= 16 - 5(8) + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= 16 - 40 + 4 - 7 \\ &amp;= -27 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 2**. Because the denominator is nonzero at <InlineMath math="x = 2" />, use **direct substitution**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 6x + 8}{x^2 + 16x + 28} \\ &amp;= \frac{2^2 - 6(2) + 8}{2^2 + 16(2) + 28} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{4 - 12 + 8}{4 + 32 + 28} = \frac{0}{64} = 0" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 3**. **Direct substitution** yields <InlineMath math="\frac{0}{0}" />, so factor the numerator:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)^2" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)^2}{x - 1} &amp;= \lim_{x \to 1} (x - 1) \\ &amp;= 1 - 1 \\ &amp;= 0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 4**. Verify all three continuity conditions at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />:

| Condition | Check |
| --- | --- |
| The function value exists | <InlineMath math="f(1)=1^2-2(1)+1=0" /> |
| The limit exists | <InlineMath math="\lim_{x\to1}(x^2-2x+1)=1-2+1=0" /> |
| The limit equals the function value | <InlineMath math="\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=0=f(1)" /> |

Because all three conditions hold, the function is **continuous** at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />.

**Problem 5**. Direct substitution produces an indeterminate ratio:

<BlockMath math="\frac{\sqrt{0 + 4} - 2}{0} = \frac{2 - 2}{0} = \frac{0}{0}" />

Rationalize by multiplying with the **conjugate** <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x + 4} + 2" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{x + 4})^2 - 2^2}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \\ &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x + 4) - 4}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} \\ &amp;= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0 + 4} + 2} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{2 + 2} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{4} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>
