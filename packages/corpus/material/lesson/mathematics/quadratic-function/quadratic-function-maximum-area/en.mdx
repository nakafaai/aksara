## Turning a constraint into a maximum area

An optimization problem asks for the best value that is still allowed by a constraint. For example, a fixed length of fencing can enclose many rectangles, but those rectangles do not all have the same area. A quadratic function lets us compare every permitted rectangle in one model.

The important move is not to memorize a formula for a particular fence. It is to express one dimension in terms of the other, substitute that relationship into the area formula, and then interpret the vertex of the resulting parabola.

## Why a downward-opening quadratic has a maximum

A quadratic function has the form

<BlockMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a\neq0" />

When <InlineMath math="a<0" />, its graph opens downward. The vertex is therefore the highest point of the graph. If the vertex lies inside the feasible domain of the problem, its function value is the maximum.

### Locating the vertex

For <InlineMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c" />, the vertex coordinates are found from

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{b}{2a}" />
  <BlockMath math="y_v=f(x_v)" />
</MathContainer>

The equivalent discriminant form is

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\Delta=b^2-4ac" />
  <BlockMath math="y_v=-\frac{\Delta}{4a}" />
</MathContainer>

The algebra identifies the vertex, but the context decides whether that vertex is usable. Lengths cannot be negative, a production count may need to be an integer, and time cannot fall outside the interval described by the model.

## Worked example: a rectangular chicken coop

A farmer has <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular chicken coop. What dimensions produce the greatest possible area?

1. **Name the dimensions.** Let the length be <InlineMath math="p\text{ m}" /> and the width be <InlineMath math="l\text{ m}" />.
2. **Translate the perimeter constraint.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="2p+2l=20" />
     <BlockMath math="p+l=10" />
     <BlockMath math="p=10-l" />
   </MathContainer>

3. **Build an area function in one variable.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="A=p\cdot l" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=(10-l)l" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=10l-l^2" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=-l^2+10l" />
   </MathContainer>

4. **State the feasible domain.** Both side lengths must be positive, so <InlineMath math="0<l<10" />. The coefficient <InlineMath math="a=-1" /> is negative, which means the area graph opens downward.
5. **Locate the vertex.**

   <BlockMath math="l_v=-\frac{b}{2a}=-\frac{10}{2(-1)}=\frac{-10}{-2}=5" />

6. **Recover the other dimension and evaluate the area.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="p=10-l=10-5=5" />
     <BlockMath math="A(5)=5\cdot5=25" />
   </MathContainer>

7. **Check and interpret the result.** The vertex lies inside <InlineMath math="0<l<10" />. At the boundary values, the rectangle collapses and the area approaches <InlineMath math="0" />, so the interior vertex gives the global maximum.

The greatest area is therefore <InlineMath math="25\text{ m}^2" />, obtained from a <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}\times5\text{ m}" /> square.

## Reading the model at a glance

| Part of the problem | Mathematical statement | Meaning |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ------- |
| Fixed perimeter | <InlineMath math="2p+2l=20" /> | Every permitted rectangle uses all <InlineMath math="20\text{ m}" /> of fencing. |
| One-variable model | <InlineMath math="A(l)=-l^2+10l" /> | Each permitted width has exactly one corresponding area. |
| Feasible domain | <InlineMath math="0<l<10" /> | Both side lengths remain positive. |
| Vertex | <InlineMath math="(5,25)" /> | Width <InlineMath math="5\text{ m}" /> produces area <InlineMath math="25\text{ m}^2" />. |

## Other applications

### Production profit

Suppose the profit from selling <InlineMath math="x" /> toys, measured in thousands of rupiah, is

<BlockMath math="P(x)=-2x^2+100x" />

Because <InlineMath math="a=-2<0" />, the model has a maximum:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{100}{2(-2)}=25" />
  <BlockMath math="P(25)=-2(25)^2+100(25)=-2(625)+2500=1250" />
</MathContainer>

The model predicts a maximum profit of <InlineMath math="\text{IDR }1\,250\,000" /> when <InlineMath math="25" /> toys are sold. Here the vertex is already an integer, so no rounding comparison is needed.

### Projectile height

Suppose a toy rocket's height after <InlineMath math="t\text{ s}" /> is

<BlockMath math="h(t)=-5t^2+40t" />

The physically meaningful interval follows from the times at which the height is zero:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="-5t^2+40t=-5t(t-8)=0" />
  <BlockMath math="t=0\text{ s}\quad\text{or}\quad t=8\text{ s}" />
</MathContainer>

The model therefore starts at ground level when <InlineMath math="t=0" /> and returns to ground level when <InlineMath math="t=8" />, so its physical time domain is <InlineMath math="0\le t\le8" />. Within that interval, the maximum occurs at

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="t_v=-\frac{40}{2(-5)}=\frac{-40}{-10}=4\text{ s}" />
  <BlockMath math="h(4)=-5(4)^2+40(4)=-80+160=80\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

The rocket reaches a maximum height of <InlineMath math="80\text{ m}" /> after <InlineMath math="4\text{ s}" />.

## Practice

A rectangle has perimeter <InlineMath math="60\text{ cm}" />. Determine the length and width that maximize its area, and find the maximum area.

### Worked solution

1. Let the length be <InlineMath math="p\text{ cm}" /> and the width be <InlineMath math="l\text{ cm}" />.
2. Convert the perimeter constraint into an expression for the length.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="2p+2l=60" />
     <BlockMath math="p+l=30" />
     <BlockMath math="p=30-l" />
   </MathContainer>

3. Substitute into the area formula.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=p\cdot l" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=(30-l)l" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=30l-l^2" />
     <BlockMath math="A(l)=-l^2+30l" />
   </MathContainer>

4. The feasible domain is <InlineMath math="0<l<30" />, and <InlineMath math="a=-1<0" />, so the vertex gives the maximum.
5. Find the width at the vertex.

   <BlockMath math="l_v=-\frac{30}{2(-1)}=\frac{-30}{-2}=15\text{ cm}" />

6. Find the corresponding length and area.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="p=30-l=30-15=15\text{ cm}" />
     <BlockMath math="A(15)=15\cdot15=225\text{ cm}^2" />
   </MathContainer>

The maximum area is <InlineMath math="225\text{ cm}^2" />, produced by a <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}\times15\text{ cm}" /> square.
