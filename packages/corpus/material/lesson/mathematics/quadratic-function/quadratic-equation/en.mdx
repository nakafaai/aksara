## Equations of Degree Two

A quadratic equation is a **polynomial equation in one variable**: it contains <InlineMath math="x^2" /> but no higher power of <InlineMath math="x" />. The general form of a quadratic equation is:

<BlockMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" />

In this form, <InlineMath math="a, b, c" /> are real numbers and <InlineMath math="a \neq 0" />.

### Squares and Areas

A square with side length <InlineMath math="x" /> has area <InlineMath math="x\cdot x=x^2" />. Doubling the side makes the area **four times as large**, not twice as large. For example, a side of 2 meters gives an area of 4 square meters, while a side of 4 meters gives 16 square meters.

## How to Solve Quadratic Equations

The form of the equation determines the **most convenient method**. Each method below works when its calculation conditions are met. Factorisation is quickest when the factors are whole numbers, the formula always works, and [completing the square](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-perfect-square) also reveals the vertex.

### Factorization

The factorization method involves breaking down the quadratic equation into a product of two linear factors. For example:

Split the middle term into <InlineMath math="-4x+x" /> so both groups have **a common factor**. Once the product equals zero, at least one factor must be zero:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}2x^2-3x-2&amp;=2x^2-4x+x-2\\&amp;=2x(x-2)+(x-2)\\&amp;=(2x+1)(x-2)\end{aligned}" />

From the factored form above, we can get the solutions:

- If <InlineMath math="2x + 1 = 0" />, then <InlineMath math="x = -\frac{1}{2}" />
- If <InlineMath math="x - 2 = 0" />, then <InlineMath math="x = 2" />

The roots of the quadratic equation are <InlineMath math="x = -\frac{1}{2}" /> or <InlineMath math="x = 2" />.

### Completing the Square

Completing the square rewrites the quadratic expression as a **squared binomial** plus a constant.

Example:

<BlockMath math="2x^2 - 3x - 2 = 0" />

We divide all terms by <InlineMath math="2" />:

<BlockMath math="x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x - 1 = 0" />

Move the constant to the right side:

<BlockMath math="x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x = 1" />

Add <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{-3/2}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{16}" /> to both sides:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x + \frac{9}{16} = 1 + \frac{9}{16}" />
  <BlockMath math="\left(x - \frac{3}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{16 + 9}{16} = \frac{25}{16}" />
  <BlockMath math="\left(x - \frac{3}{4}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^2" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x - \frac{3}{4} = \pm \frac{5}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{3}{4} \pm \frac{5}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = 2 \text{ or } x = -\frac{1}{2}" />
</MathContainer>

### Using the Quadratic Formula

For the equation <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" />, the roots can be determined using the **quadratic formula**:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}" />

Example:

<BlockMath math="2x^2 - 3x - 2 = 0" />

With <InlineMath math="a = 2" />, <InlineMath math="b = -3" />, and <InlineMath math="c = -2" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{-(-3) \pm \sqrt{(-3)^2 - 4(2)(-2)}}{2(2)}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{3 \pm 5}{4}" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x = \frac{3 + 5}{4} = 2 \\ &amp;\text{or} \\ &amp;x = \frac{3 - 5}{4} = -\frac{1}{2} \end{aligned}" />

## Formulating Problems as Quadratic Equations

Word problems hide the equation inside a description, so translate each sentence into a quantity first. Only once the equation stands on its own do you choose a solving method.

Quadratic equations model quantities whose relationships contain **products or squares**. The following examples turn those relationships into equations.

### Reading Room Problem

Four reading corners of the same size are created in a room measuring <InlineMath math="4 \text{ m} \times 6 \text{ m}" />. If each corner is a square with side length <InlineMath math="x \text{ meters}" />, then the remaining area of the room for arranging seats is:

Subtract the area of the four corners from the total area.
<BlockMath math="4 \times 6 - 4x^2 = 24 - 4x^2" />

  heightLabel={<InlineMath math="4 \text{ m}" />}
  widthLabel={<InlineMath math="6 \text{ m}" />}
/>

If the remaining area must be **20 square meters**, the area model becomes an equation. The four drawn squares show the arrangement schematically. Calculate their side length from the area requirement:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}24-4x^2&amp;=20\\4x^2&amp;=4\\x^2&amp;=1\\x&amp;=\pm1\end{aligned}" />

A side length must be positive, so <InlineMath math="x=1" /> meter. The two squares along the 4-meter wall must not overlap, requiring <InlineMath math="2x\le4" />. One meter satisfies this restriction.

### Problem of Multiplying Two Numbers

The **product of two numbers** is <InlineMath math="63" /> and their sum is <InlineMath math="16" />. We can solve this using a quadratic equation.

Say the two numbers are <InlineMath math="p" /> and <InlineMath math="q" />, then:

- <InlineMath math="p + q = 16" />, so <InlineMath math="q = 16 - p" />
- <InlineMath math="p \times q = 63" />

Substituting the value of <InlineMath math="q" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="p(16 - p) = 63" />
  <BlockMath math="16p - p^2 = 63" />
  <BlockMath math="-p^2 + 16p - 63 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="p^2 - 16p + 63 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Factor the quadratic equation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="p^2 - 16p + 63 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="(p - 7)(p - 9) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="p = 7 \text{ or } p = 9" />
</MathContainer>

The two numbers are <InlineMath math="7" /> and <InlineMath math="9" />.

### Vehicle Speed Problem

A vehicle travels a distance of <InlineMath math="320 \text{ km}" /> at a certain speed. If the vehicle travels <InlineMath math="24 \text{ km/h}" /> faster, its **travel time is reduced** by <InlineMath math="3 \text{ hours}" />. We can find the initial speed using a quadratic equation.

Say the initial speed is <InlineMath math="v \text{ km/h}" /> and the initial travel time is <InlineMath math="t \text{ hours}" />, then:

- <InlineMath math="v \times t = 320" /> (distance <InlineMath math="=" /> speed <InlineMath math="\times" /> time)
- <InlineMath math="(v + 24) \times (t - 3) = 320" /> (second condition)

From the first equation: <InlineMath math="t = \frac{320}{v}" />

Substituting into the second equation:

<BlockMath math="(v + 24) \times \left(\frac{320}{v} - 3\right) = 320" />

Multiply by <InlineMath math="v" /> and simplify:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(v + 24)(320 - 3v) = 320v" />
  <BlockMath math="320v-3v^2+7680-72v=320v" />
  <BlockMath math="-3v^2-72v+7680=0" />
  <BlockMath math="v^2 + 24v - 2560 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="(v - 40)(v + 64) = 0" />
</MathContainer>

A negative speed is not meaningful in this context. Therefore, the initial speed is <InlineMath math="v = 40 \text{ km/h}" />. The initial trip takes <InlineMath math="8 \text{ hours}" />. At <InlineMath math="64 \text{ km/h}" />, the trip takes <InlineMath math="5 \text{ hours}" />, exactly <InlineMath math="3 \text{ hours}" /> less.

## Common Misconceptions About Quadratic Equations

Some common misconceptions include:

1. Identifying the addition operation <InlineMath math="x + 3" /> as <InlineMath math="3x" />.

   **Concrete example**: If a room is <InlineMath math="x \text{ meters}" /> long and gains <InlineMath math="3 \text{ meters}" />, its new length is <InlineMath math="(x + 3) \text{ meters}" />. The expression <InlineMath math="3x" /> would multiply the original length by three.

2. Labeling an equation as quadratic solely because it contains the term <InlineMath math="x^2" />, without considering the equation's overall form.

   A quadratic equation <Highlight>is a polynomial equation of the form</Highlight> <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" /> with <InlineMath math="a \neq 0" />.

## Forms of Quadratic Equations

Distinguish an equation **as written** from the form obtained after clearing denominators. Multiplying by a denominator must preserve the restriction that the denominator cannot be zero.

| Original equation | After clearing denominators | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="\frac1x+2x+4=0" /> | <InlineMath math="2x^2+4x+1=0,\ x\ne0" /> | Rational, reducible to a quadratic for permitted x values |
| <InlineMath math="\frac1{x-5}+\frac1{x-3}=x^2-4" /> | <InlineMath math="2x-8=(x^2-4)(x-5)(x-3),\ x\ne3,5" /> | Rational, leads to a degree-four equation |
| <InlineMath math="3x^2+2x-1=0" /> | <InlineMath math="a=3,\ b=2,\ c=-1" /> | Already a quadratic equation |
| <InlineMath math="x^2+\frac1x+3=0" /> | <InlineMath math="x^3+3x+1=0,\ x\ne0" /> | Rational, leads to a cubic equation |

## Practice Problems

These problems mix several shapes, so decide for each equation whether it factors, whether the formula is needed, or whether a perfect square is already there. Write the chosen method down before you calculate.

**Identifying Quadratic Equations**

Determine whether the following mathematical equations are quadratic equations:

1. <InlineMath math="x^3 + x^2 + x = 0" />
2. <InlineMath math="3x^2 + 2x - 1 = 0" />
3. <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{x} + 5x = 0" />
4. <InlineMath math="x^2 + \frac{1}{x} + 3 = 0" />

**Expanding Products**

Expand the following equations:

1. <InlineMath math="(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0" />
2. <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{1}{3}x - 4\right)(x + 9) = 0" />
3. <InlineMath math="(2x - 8)(x + 5) = 0" />

### Answer Key

For classification, inspect the **highest power and any denominators**. To expand, multiply each term in the first factor by each term in the second. To find roots, use a product equal to zero.

**Classification problem 1**: <InlineMath math="x^3 + x^2 + x = 0" />

**Answer**: Not a quadratic equation, because it has the highest power of <InlineMath math="3" /> (<InlineMath math="x^3" />). This is a cubic equation.

**Classification problem 2**: <InlineMath math="3x^2 + 2x - 1 = 0" />

**Answer**: Quadratic equation, because it is in the form <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" /> with <InlineMath math="a = 3" />, <InlineMath math="b = 2" />, and <InlineMath math="c = -1" />.

**Classification problem 3**: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{x} + 5x = 0" />

**Answer**: Not a quadratic equation, because it contains the term <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{x}" />. As written, it is rational. For <InlineMath math="x\ne0" />, multiplying by x gives <InlineMath math="1+5x^2=0" />.

**Classification problem 4**: <InlineMath math="x^2 + \frac{1}{x} + 3 = 0" />

**Answer**: Not a quadratic equation, because it contains the term <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{x}" />. This is a rational equation.

**Expansion problem 1**: <InlineMath math="(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0" />

**Answer**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So, the product of the two factors is <InlineMath math="x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0" />

**Expansion problem 2**: <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{1}{3}x - 4\right)(x + 9) = 0" />

**Answer**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\left(\frac{1}{3}x - 4\right)(x + 9) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{1}{3}x \cdot x + \frac{1}{3}x \cdot 9 - 4 \cdot x - 4 \cdot 9 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{1}{3}x^2 + 3x - 4x - 36 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{1}{3}x^2 - x - 36 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Multiply all terms by <InlineMath math="3" /> to simplify:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 3x - 108 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So, the product of the two factors is <InlineMath math="x^2 - 3x - 108 = 0" /> or <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}x^2 - x - 36 = 0" />

**Expansion problem 3**: <InlineMath math="(2x - 8)(x + 5) = 0" />

**Answer**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(2x - 8)(x + 5) = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="2x \cdot x + 2x \cdot 5 - 8 \cdot x - 8 \cdot 5 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="2x^2 + 10x - 8x - 40 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="2x^2 + 2x - 40 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Factoring <InlineMath math="2x - 8" /> as <InlineMath math="2(x - 4)" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="2(x - 4)(x + 5) = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So, the product of the two factors is <InlineMath math="2x^2 + 2x - 40 = 0" />
