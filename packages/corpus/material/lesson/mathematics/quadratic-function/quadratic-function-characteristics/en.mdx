## Shape of Quadratic Function Graphs

A parabola has an <Highlight>axis of symmetry and a vertex</Highlight>. For <InlineMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c" /> with <InlineMath math="a\ne0" />, the sign of the leading coefficient determines the opening direction. Its magnitude determines curvature, while all three coefficients locate the graph.

## Influence of the Leading Coefficient on Graph Shape

The **sign of the leading coefficient** decides whether the parabola opens upward or downward. Its magnitude then decides how narrow or wide the parabola becomes, because a larger magnitude pulls both branches closer to the axis of symmetry.

### When the Leading Coefficient Is Positive

If <InlineMath math="a > 0" />, the graph of the quadratic function will open upward. This means the graph has a minimum point.

Examples of functions with <InlineMath math="a > 0" />:

- <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2" />, the simplest example with <InlineMath math="a = 1" />
- <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2x^2 + 1" />, an example with <InlineMath math="a = 2" />

The sign of <InlineMath math="a" /> <Highlight>controls the opening direction</Highlight>. Its magnitude controls the width: compared with <InlineMath math="y=x^2" />, the graph is narrower when <InlineMath math="|a|>1" /> and wider when <InlineMath math="0<|a|<1" />.

### When the Leading Coefficient Is Negative

If <InlineMath math="a < 0" />, the graph of the quadratic function will **open downward**. This means the graph has a maximum point.

Examples of functions with <InlineMath math="a < 0" />:

- <InlineMath math="f(x) = -x^2" /> with <InlineMath math="a = -1" />
- <InlineMath math="f(x) = -3x^2 - 12x - 15" /> with <InlineMath math="a = -3" />

For the second example, complete the square to find the maximum. Multiplying a nonnegative square by a negative number gives a nonpositive result.

<BlockMath math="-3x^2-12x-15=-3(x+2)^2-3\le-3" />

### Why the Leading Coefficient Cannot Be Zero

If <InlineMath math="a=0" />, the <Highlight>quadratic term disappears</Highlight>. The expression <InlineMath math="bx+c" /> is linear when <InlineMath math="b\ne0" /> and constant when <InlineMath math="b=0" />. For example, <InlineMath math="2x+3" /> gives a sloping line, while <InlineMath math="3" /> gives a horizontal line.


For a straight line, increasing the input by one always changes the output by the same linear coefficient. There is no turning point as there is on a parabola.

<BlockMath math="f(x+1)-f(x)=b(x+1)+c-(bx+c)=b" />

## Key Points and Lines on the Parabola

Use the <Highlight>vertex, symmetry axis, and intercepts</Highlight> to locate the parabola. Not every parabola crosses the horizontal axis. The leading coefficient still determines the width, especially when several features coincide at one point.

### Vertex

Completing the square reveals the <Highlight>extreme value</Highlight> because a square is nonnegative. For <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2-2x-3" />, the minimum occurs when <InlineMath math="x=1" />:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=(x-1)^2-4\ge-4" />

### Axis of Symmetry

Two points at the <Highlight>same distance from the symmetry axis</Highlight> have equal height. For <InlineMath math="f(x)=(x-1)^2-4" />, use distance <InlineMath math="t" /> from the line <InlineMath math="x=1" />:

<BlockMath math="f(1-t)=(-t)^2-4=t^2-4=f(1+t)" />

### Vertical Axis Intercept

For the vertical intercept, use an <Highlight>input of zero</Highlight>. Every variable term vanishes. For <InlineMath math="f(x)=2x^2-4x+5" />:

<BlockMath math="f(0)=2(0)^2-4(0)+5=5\quad\Rightarrow\quad(0,5)" />

### Horizontal Axis Intercepts

For horizontal intercepts, find where the <Highlight>function value is zero</Highlight>. Factoring <InlineMath math="x^2-2x-3" /> gives two roots. A negative discriminant would mean no real horizontal-axis intercepts:

<BlockMath math="(x-3)(x+1)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x\in\{-1,3\}" />

## Drawing Quadratic Function Graphs

The examples below draw one upward opening and one downward opening parabola. Each **graphing step** is written out, so the shape can be compared with the formula.

### Upward Opening Parabola Example

Graph the function <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x - 3" />:

**Step 1**: Coefficient <InlineMath math="a = 1 > 0" />, so the parabola opens upward.
**Step 2**: Vertex:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-2}{2 \cdot 1} = 1" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(1) &amp;= 1^2 - 2 \cdot 1 - 3 \\ &amp;= 1 - 2 - 3 \\ &amp;= -4 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

So the vertex is at <InlineMath math="(1, -4)" />.

**Step 3**: <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept:

<BlockMath math="f(0) = 0^2 - 2 \cdot 0 - 3 = -3" />

So the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept is at <InlineMath math="(0, -3)" />.

**Step 4**: Intercepts on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis: <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" /> or <InlineMath math="x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0" />

Using the quadratic formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x &amp;= \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \\ &amp;= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 12}}{2} \\ &amp;= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{16}}{2} \\ &amp;= \frac{2 \pm 4}{2} \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;x = \frac{2 + 4}{2} = 3 \\ &amp;\text{or} \\ &amp;x = \frac{2 - 4}{2} = -1 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

So the intercepts on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis are <InlineMath math="(-1, 0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(3, 0)" />.

**Step 5**: Calculate some additional points:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(-2) &amp;= (-2)^2 - 2 \cdot (-2) - 3 \\ &amp;= 4 + 4 - 3 \\ &amp;= 5 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(2) &amp;= 2^2 - 2 \cdot 2 - 3 \\ &amp;= 4 - 4 - 3 \\ &amp;= -3 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

### Downward Opening Parabola Example

Graph the function <InlineMath math="f(x) = -x^2" />:

**Step 1**: Coefficient <InlineMath math="a = -1 < 0" />, so the parabola opens downward.
**Step 2**: Vertex:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{0}{2 \cdot (-1)} = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="f(0) = -(0)^2 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So the vertex is at <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />.

**Step 3**: <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept:

<BlockMath math="f(0) = 0" />

So the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept is at <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />.

**Step 4**: Intercepts on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis: <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" /> or <InlineMath math="-x^2 = 0" />

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="x = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So the <InlineMath math="x" />-intercept is at <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />.

**Step 5**: Calculate some additional points:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="f(-2) = -((-2)^2) = -4" />
  <BlockMath math="f(-1) = -((-1)^2) = -1" />
  <BlockMath math="f(1) = -(1^2) = -1" />
  <BlockMath math="f(2) = -(2^2) = -4" />
</MathContainer>

## Opening Direction Vertex and Range

This table assumes a <Highlight>domain of all real numbers</Highlight>. On a restricted domain, the extreme value might not be reached. Write the vertex as <InlineMath math="(x_v,y_v)" />, where <InlineMath math="y_v=f(x_v)" />.

| Condition                    | Opening  | Vertex value | Range                                  |
| ---------------------------- | -------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="a > 0" />  | Upward   | Minimum      | <InlineMath math="[y_v,\infty)" />    |
| <InlineMath math="a < 0" />  | Downward | Maximum      | <InlineMath math="(-\infty,y_v]" />   |

## Practice Problem

This problem runs the whole checklist on one function, so a single answer has to name every feature.

Analyze the graph of <InlineMath math="g(x)=-2(x+1)^2+8" />. Find its **opening direction**,  vertex, symmetry axis, intercepts, and range.

### Worked Solution

The function is already in **vertex form** <InlineMath math="a(x-h)^2+k" />. Here <InlineMath math="a=-2" />, <InlineMath math="h=-1" />, and <InlineMath math="k=8" />.

- Because <InlineMath math="a<0" />, the parabola opens downward.
- The vertex is <InlineMath math="(-1,8)" />, so its maximum value is <InlineMath math="8" />.
- The symmetry axis is <InlineMath math="x=-1" />.

For the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis intercept, set <InlineMath math="x=0" />:

<BlockMath math="g(0)=-2(0+1)^2+8=6" />

The graph crosses the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis at <InlineMath math="(0,6)" />. For the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis intercepts, solve <InlineMath math="g(x)=0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="-2(x+1)^2+8=0" />
  <BlockMath math="(x+1)^2=4" />
  <BlockMath math="x+1=\pm2" />
  <BlockMath math="x=-3\quad\text{or}\quad x=1" />
</MathContainer>

The intercepts are <InlineMath math="(-3,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,0)" />. Since the parabola opens downward from the maximum <InlineMath math="8" />, its range is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,8]" />.
