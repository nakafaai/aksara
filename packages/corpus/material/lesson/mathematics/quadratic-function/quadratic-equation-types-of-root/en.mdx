## Reading the Nature of the Roots from the Discriminant

The nature of the roots of a quadratic equation <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" /> with real coefficients and <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" /> is decided by the **discriminant** <InlineMath math="D = b^2 - 4ac" />. You only need to compute <InlineMath math="D" /> and read its sign, without solving the equation all the way:

| Discriminant | Nature of the roots | Graph of <InlineMath math="y = ax^2 + bx + c" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="D > 0" /> | Two different real roots | Crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at two points |
| <InlineMath math="D = 0" /> | One repeated real root | Touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at its vertex |
| <InlineMath math="D < 0" /> | Two complex, nonreal roots | Never meets the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis |

The discriminant is the part under the square root in the [quadratic formula](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-formula):

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}" />

The <InlineMath math="\pm" /> sign gives two different values when <InlineMath math="\sqrt{D}" /> is positive, one value when <InlineMath math="\sqrt{D} = 0" />, and imaginary numbers when <InlineMath math="D" /> is negative.

## Two Different Real Roots

If <InlineMath math="D > 0" />, the equation has **two different real roots**:

<BlockMath math="x_1 = \frac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a}, \qquad x_2 = \frac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}" />

For <InlineMath math="x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0" />, the discriminant is <InlineMath math="D = (-5)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 6 = 1" />, so <InlineMath math="x = \frac{5 \pm 1}{2}" />, that is <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />. Check by factoring: <InlineMath math="(x - 2)(x - 3) = x^2 - 5x + 6" />.

For an equation with integer coefficients, <InlineMath math="D" /> also tells you **whether the roots are rational**. If <InlineMath math="D" /> is a perfect square such as <InlineMath math="1" />, the roots are rational and the equation factors. If <InlineMath math="D > 0" /> is not a perfect square, the roots are irrational. For example, <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 1 = 0" /> has <InlineMath math="D = 12" />, so <InlineMath math="x = \frac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt{3}" />.

## One Repeated Real Root

If <InlineMath math="D = 0" />, the <InlineMath math="\pm" /> sign changes nothing, so the equation has **one repeated real root**:

<BlockMath math="x_1 = x_2 = -\frac{b}{2a}" />

For <InlineMath math="x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0" />, the discriminant is <InlineMath math="(-6)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 9 = 0" /> and the root is <InlineMath math="x = \frac{6}{2} = 3" />. Check: the left side is the perfect square <InlineMath math="(x - 3)^2" />, so the graph touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis exactly at the vertex <InlineMath math="(3, 0)" />.

The condition <InlineMath math="D = 0" /> often finds a parameter. Suppose <InlineMath math="x^2 + (m - 2)x + 4 = 0" /> must have a repeated root:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="D = (m - 2)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 4 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="(m - 2)^2 = 16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m - 2 = \pm 4" />
  <BlockMath math="m = 6 \quad \text{or} \quad m = -2" />
</MathContainer>

## Two Complex Nonreal Roots

If <InlineMath math="D < 0" />, the square root of <InlineMath math="D" /> is not a real number. Both roots are **complex and nonreal**, and they always form a conjugate pair:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}, \qquad i^2 = -1" />

For <InlineMath math="x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0" />, the discriminant is <InlineMath math="2^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = -16" />, so <InlineMath math="x = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i" />. Check with the sum and product: <InlineMath math="(-1 + 2i) + (-1 - 2i) = -2 = -\frac{b}{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="(-1)^2 + 2^2 = 5 = \frac{c}{a}" />. The real part <InlineMath math="-1" /> equals the x-coordinate of the vertex of <InlineMath math="(x + 1)^2 + 4" />, the link at the heart of [imaginary roots of a quadratic equation](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-imaginary-root).

## Signs of the Roots from Their Sum and Product

The factored form <InlineMath math="a(x - x_1)(x - x_2)" /> must equal <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c" />. Match the coefficients of equal powers:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}a(x-x_1)(x-x_2)&amp;=a[x^2-(x_1+x_2)x+x_1x_2]\\b&amp;=-a(x_1+x_2)\\c&amp;=ax_1x_2\end{aligned}" />

So **the sum of the roots** is <InlineMath math="x_1 + x_2 = -\frac{b}{a}" /> and **the product of the roots** is <InlineMath math="x_1 x_2 = \frac{c}{a}" />. For real roots (<InlineMath math="D \ge 0" />), these two values decide the signs of the roots without computing them:

| Condition (with <InlineMath math="D \ge 0" />) | Signs of the roots |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="x_1 x_2 > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="x_1 + x_2 > 0" /> | Both positive |
| <InlineMath math="x_1 x_2 > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="x_1 + x_2 < 0" /> | Both negative |
| <InlineMath math="x_1 x_2 < 0" /> | Opposite signs |
| <InlineMath math="x_1 x_2 = 0" /> | One root is zero, the other is <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{a}" /> |

A positive product means both roots have the same sign, so **the sum decides that sign**.

For <InlineMath math="x^2 + x - 6 = 0" />, the product of the roots is <InlineMath math="\frac{c}{a} = -6 < 0" />, so the roots have opposite signs. The graph shows why: for <InlineMath math="a > 0" />, a negative <InlineMath math="f(0) = c" /> puts the parabola below the axis at <InlineMath math="x = 0" />, so it must cross the axis once on the left of the origin and once on the right. The case <InlineMath math="a < 0" /> with <InlineMath math="c > 0" /> is the same with the parabola flipped.

## Example of the Condition for Two Different Real Roots

Find the values of <InlineMath math="k" /> for which <InlineMath math="kx^2 + 4x + 1 = 0" /> has two different real roots.

There are **two conditions**. The equation must be quadratic, so <InlineMath math="k \ne 0" />, and the discriminant must be positive:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="D = 4^2 - 4 \cdot k \cdot 1 = 16 - 4k > 0" />
  <BlockMath math="k < 4" />
</MathContainer>

So <InlineMath math="k < 4" /> and <InlineMath math="k \ne 0" />. Check with <InlineMath math="k = 3" />: the equation <InlineMath math="3x^2 + 4x + 1 = (3x + 1)(x + 1)" /> has roots <InlineMath math="-\frac13" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" />. For <InlineMath math="k = 0" />, the equation becomes <InlineMath math="4x + 1 = 0" />, which has only one root, so that value must be removed.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Computing <InlineMath math="(-5)^2" /> as <InlineMath math="-25" /> | The square of a negative number is always positive | <InlineMath math="(-5)^2 = 25" />, so <InlineMath math="D" /> for <InlineMath math="x^2 - 5x + 6" /> is <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Forgetting the condition <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" /> | For <InlineMath math="a = 0" /> the equation is linear and classifying the roots from <InlineMath math="D" /> does not apply | Write the condition on the leading coefficient before computing <InlineMath math="D" /> |
| Calling <InlineMath math="D = 0" /> no roots | The root exists and is repeated, namely <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{2a}" /> | The graph touches the axis at its vertex |
| Deciding the signs from the sum alone | A positive sum can come from one large root and one negative root | Check the product first |
| Using the sign rules when <InlineMath math="D < 0" /> | Complex nonreal roots are neither positive nor negative | Make sure <InlineMath math="D \ge 0" /> first |

The quickest check after computing the roots is **to match their sum and product** with <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\frac{c}{a}" />.

## Practice Problems

Compute the discriminant first, then **decide the nature of the roots before solving the equation**.

1. Find the nature of the roots of <InlineMath math="3x^2 - 7x + 2 = 0" />, then solve it.

2. Find the nature of the roots of <InlineMath math="2x^2 - 4x + 5 = 0" />, then solve it and check with the sum and product of the roots.

3. Find the values of <InlineMath math="p" /> for which <InlineMath math="x^2 - 2px + 9 = 0" /> has a repeated root, then find the root.

4. Without solving the equation, find the signs of both roots of <InlineMath math="x^2 - 8x + 12 = 0" />.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. The discriminant is <InlineMath math="D = (-7)^2 - 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 49 - 24 = 25" />. Since <InlineMath math="D > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="25 = 5^2" />, the roots are **two different rational real numbers**:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{7 \pm 5}{6} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_1 = 2, \quad x_2 = \frac13" />

Check: <InlineMath math="(3x - 1)(x - 2) = 3x^2 - 7x + 2" />.

**Problem 2**. The discriminant is <InlineMath math="D = (-4)^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = -24" />, so the roots are **two complex nonreal numbers**. With <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-24} = 2i\sqrt{6}" />:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{4 \pm 2i\sqrt{6}}{4} = 1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\,i" />

Check: the sum is <InlineMath math="2 = -\frac{b}{a}" />, and the product is <InlineMath math="1 + \frac{6}{4} = \frac52 = \frac{c}{a}" />.

**Problem 3**. A repeated root means <InlineMath math="D = 0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="D = (-2p)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 9 = 4p^2 - 36 = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="p^2 = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = 3 \quad \text{or} \quad p = -3" />
</MathContainer>

The root is <InlineMath math="x = -\frac{b}{2a} = p" />, so it is <InlineMath math="3" /> for <InlineMath math="p = 3" /> and <InlineMath math="-3" /> for <InlineMath math="p = -3" />. Check: <InlineMath math="x^2 - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)^2" /> and <InlineMath math="x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2" />.

**Problem 4**. The discriminant is <InlineMath math="64 - 48 = 16 > 0" />, so both roots are real. The product is <InlineMath math="12 > 0" />, so the roots have the same sign, and the sum is <InlineMath math="8 > 0" />, so **both are positive**. Check: <InlineMath math="x^2 - 8x + 12 = (x - 2)(x - 6)" />, with roots <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="6" />.
