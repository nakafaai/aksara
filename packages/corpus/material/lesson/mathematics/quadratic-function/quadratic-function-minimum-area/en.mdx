## Turning a constraint into a minimum

Some optimization problems ask for the smallest possible value. A manufacturer may want the lowest cost, or a design may need the least total area under a fixed geometric condition. The objective changes, but the modeling process is the same: translate the constraint, build a function in one variable, and interpret its vertex.

Finding a minimum does not mean choosing zero automatically. The original problem determines which values are permitted, and the minimum must lie within that feasible domain.

## Why an upward opening quadratic has a minimum

A quadratic function has the form

<BlockMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a\neq0" />

When <InlineMath math="a>0" />, its graph opens upward. The vertex is therefore the lowest point of the graph. If the vertex lies inside the feasible domain, its function value is the minimum.

### Locating the vertex

For <InlineMath math="f(x)=ax^2+bx+c" />, use

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{b}{2a}" />
  <BlockMath math="y_v=f(x_v)" />
</MathContainer>

The equivalent discriminant form is

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\Delta=b^2-4ac" />
  <BlockMath math="y_v=-\frac{\Delta}{4a}" />
</MathContainer>

Always return to the context after calculating the vertex. Check the domain, units, and whether the variable must be an integer.

## Minimum Area from One Wire and Two Squares

A <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}" /> wire is cut into two pieces. Each piece is bent into a square. Where should the wire be cut so that the sum of the two square areas is as small as possible?

1. **Name the side lengths.** Let the first square have side <InlineMath math="x\text{ cm}" /> and the second have side <InlineMath math="y\text{ cm}" />.
2. **Translate the wire-length constraint.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="4x+4y=40" />
     <BlockMath math="x+y=10" />
     <BlockMath math="y=10-x" />
   </MathContainer>

3. **Build the total-area function.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="A=x^2+y^2" />
     <BlockMath math="A(x)=x^2+(10-x)^2" />
     <BlockMath math="A(x)=x^2+(100-20x+x^2)" />
     <BlockMath math="A(x)=2x^2-20x+100" />
   </MathContainer>

4. **State the feasible domain.** Both squares must have positive side lengths, so <InlineMath math="0<x<10" />. Because <InlineMath math="a=2>0" />, the graph opens upward.
5. **Locate the vertex.**

   <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{-20}{2(2)}=\frac{20}{4}=5" />

6. **Recover the second side length and evaluate the total area.**

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="y=10-x=10-5=5" />
     <BlockMath math="A(5)=5^2+5^2=50" />
   </MathContainer>

7. **Interpret the cut.** Each square needs perimeter <InlineMath math="4(5)=20\text{ cm}" />, so the wire is cut into two <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" /> pieces. The full <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}" /> wire is still used. Only the sum of the enclosed areas is minimized.

    description={
      <>
        The graph is shown for <InlineMath math="0\le x\le10" />. Within the feasible interval <InlineMath math="0<x<10" />, it reaches its lowest point at <InlineMath math="(5,50)" />.

The minimum total area is <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^2" />, obtained when both squares have side length <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />.

## Constraint Model Domain and Vertex

| Part of the problem | Mathematical statement | Meaning |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ------- |
| Fixed wire length | <InlineMath math="4x+4y=40" /> | Both square perimeters use all <InlineMath math="40\text{ cm}" /> of wire. |
| One-variable model | <InlineMath math="A(x)=2x^2-20x+100" /> | Each permitted first side length determines one total area. |
| Feasible domain | <InlineMath math="0<x<10" /> | Both squares retain positive side lengths. |
| Vertex | <InlineMath math="(5,50)" /> | Equal side lengths produce the smallest total area. |

## Another Application with Production Cost

Suppose the cost of producing <InlineMath math="x" /> items, measured in thousands of rupiah, is

<BlockMath math="C(x)=3x^2-60x+500" />

Because <InlineMath math="a=3>0" />, the model has a minimum:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{-60}{2(3)}=10" />
  <BlockMath math="C(10)=3(10)^2-60(10)+500=300-600+500=200" />
</MathContainer>

The model predicts a minimum cost of <InlineMath math="\text{IDR }200\,000" /> at <InlineMath math="10" /> items. A real production decision would also check whether the model's stated domain and operational constraints allow that quantity.

## Practice

The sum of two positive numbers is <InlineMath math="16" />. Determine the two numbers that minimize the sum of their squares, and find that minimum sum.

### Worked solution

1. Let the first number be <InlineMath math="x" /> and the second be <InlineMath math="y" />.
2. Translate the sum constraint.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="x+y=16" />
     <BlockMath math="y=16-x" />
   </MathContainer>

3. Build the objective function.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="S=x^2+y^2" />
     <BlockMath math="S(x)=x^2+(16-x)^2" />
     <BlockMath math="S(x)=x^2+(256-32x+x^2)" />
     <BlockMath math="S(x)=2x^2-32x+256" />
   </MathContainer>

4. The feasible domain is <InlineMath math="0<x<16" />, and <InlineMath math="a=2>0" />, so the vertex gives the minimum.
5. Find the first number at the vertex.

   <BlockMath math="x_v=-\frac{-32}{2(2)}=\frac{32}{4}=8" />

6. Find the second number and evaluate the objective.

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="y=16-x=16-8=8" />
     <BlockMath math="S(8)=8^2+8^2=128" />
   </MathContainer>

The two numbers are <InlineMath math="8" /> and <InlineMath math="8" />, and the minimum sum of their squares is <InlineMath math="128" />.
