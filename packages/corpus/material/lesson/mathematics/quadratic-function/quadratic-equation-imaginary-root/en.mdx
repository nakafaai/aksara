## Imaginary Roots Appear When the Discriminant Is Negative

A quadratic equation <InlineMath math="ax^2 + bx + c = 0" /> with real coefficients has **imaginary roots** (nonreal roots) exactly when its discriminant is negative, <InlineMath math="D = b^2 - 4ac < 0" />. The two roots are

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}" />

Here <InlineMath math="i" /> is the imaginary unit with <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" />. The roots always form **a conjugate pair**: they share the real part <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{2a}" />, and their imaginary parts have opposite signs. On the graph, the parabola <InlineMath math="y = ax^2 + bx + c" /> never meets the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis.

For <InlineMath math="c = 3" />, the parabola crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at <InlineMath math="-3" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" />. For <InlineMath math="c = 5" />, the parabola lifts above the axis, and its two roots become <InlineMath math="-2 \pm i" />.

## The Imaginary Unit i

The equation <InlineMath math="x^2 + 1 = 0" /> asks for <InlineMath math="x^2 = -1" />. The square of a real number is **never negative**, so no real number solves it. That is why the number system is extended with the imaginary unit <InlineMath math="i" />, which satisfies <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" />.

With <InlineMath math="i" />, you can write the square root of a negative number. For <InlineMath math="k > 0" />, <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-k} = i\sqrt{k}" />, for example <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-4} = 2i" /> and <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-23} = i\sqrt{23}" />. A number of the form <InlineMath math="z = p + qi" /> with real <InlineMath math="p" /> and <InlineMath math="q" /> is a [complex number](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/complex-number-concept). The number <InlineMath math="2 + 5i" /> has real part <InlineMath math="2" />, while <InlineMath math="3i" /> is purely imaginary.

## The Root Formula When the Discriminant Is Negative

Start from the [quadratic formula](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-formula):

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}" />

If <InlineMath math="D < 0" />, write <InlineMath math="D = -|D|" />. Its square root becomes an imaginary number, and the formula turns into

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sqrt{D} = \sqrt{-|D|} = i\sqrt{|D|}" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a} = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}\,i" />
</MathContainer>

The last form separates the **real part** <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{2a}" /> from the **imaginary part** <InlineMath math="\pm\frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}" />. You can read both parts straight from the coefficients, without rearranging the equation.

## Example When the Leading Coefficient Is One

Solve <InlineMath math="x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0" />.

The coefficients are <InlineMath math="a = 1" />, <InlineMath math="b = 4" />, and <InlineMath math="c = 5" />. Compute the discriminant first:

<BlockMath math="D = 4^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 16 - 20 = -4" />

Since <InlineMath math="D < 0" />, **both roots are imaginary**. Substitute into the formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2 \cdot 1} = \frac{-4 \pm 2i}{2}" />
  <BlockMath math="x_1 = -2 + i, \qquad x_2 = -2 - i" />
</MathContainer>

Check <InlineMath math="x_1" /> by substituting it into the equation:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(-2+i)^2+4(-2+i)+5&amp;=4-4i+i^2-8+4i+5\\&amp;=1+i^2=1-1=0\end{aligned}" />

The root <InlineMath math="x_2" /> satisfies the equation too, because the conjugate of a root of a polynomial with real coefficients is always a root as well.

## Example When the Leading Coefficient Is Not One

Solve <InlineMath math="2x^2 + x + 3 = 0" />.

Here <InlineMath math="a = 2" />, <InlineMath math="b = 1" />, and <InlineMath math="c = 3" />, so

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="D = 1^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 1 - 24 = -23" />
  <BlockMath math="x = \frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt{23}}{2 \cdot 2} = -\frac{1}{4} \pm \frac{\sqrt{23}}{4}\,i" />
</MathContainer>

Divide **both parts** by <InlineMath math="2a = 4" />, not only the real part. Check with the sum and product of the roots. The sum is <InlineMath math="-\frac14 - \frac14 = -\frac12" />, equal to <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{a}" />. The product is <InlineMath math="\left(-\frac14\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{23}}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{16} + \frac{23}{16} = \frac{3}{2}" />, equal to <InlineMath math="\frac{c}{a}" />.

## The Vertex Holds the Imaginary Roots

Write the function in vertex form, <InlineMath math="f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k" />. The equation <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" /> gives <InlineMath math="(x - h)^2 = -\frac{k}{a}" />. If <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="k" /> have the same sign, the right side is negative, the parabola misses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, and

<BlockMath math="x = h \pm i\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}" />

So **the real part of the roots is the x-coordinate of the vertex**, and the imaginary part depends on <InlineMath math="\frac{k}{a}" />, the height of the vertex divided by <InlineMath math="a" />.

For <InlineMath math="2x^2 + x + 3" />, the vertex has <InlineMath math="h = -\frac14" /> and <InlineMath math="k = \frac{23}{8}" />. The vertex rule gives <InlineMath math="\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{23}{16}} = \frac{\sqrt{23}}{4}" />, so the roots are <InlineMath math="-\frac14 \pm \frac{\sqrt{23}}{4}\,i" />. The real part <InlineMath math="-\frac14" /> is the x-coordinate of the vertex, and <InlineMath math="\frac{\sqrt{23}}{4}" /> is the imaginary part, the same as the quadratic formula gives.

## Why the Roots Always Come in Conjugate Pairs

In the formula <InlineMath math="x = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}\,i" />, the <InlineMath math="\pm" /> sign changes only **the imaginary part**. The two roots have the form <InlineMath math="u + vi" /> and <InlineMath math="u - vi" />, and such roots are [conjugate complex numbers](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/conjugate-complex-numbers).

So the sum of the roots is always real, <InlineMath math="2u = -\frac{b}{a}" />, and so is their product, <InlineMath math="u^2 + v^2 = \frac{c}{a}" />. Another consequence: a quadratic with real coefficients **cannot** have exactly one imaginary root. If <InlineMath math="3 + 2i" /> is a root, then <InlineMath math="3 - 2i" /> is a root too.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-4} = -2" /> | <InlineMath math="(-2)^2 = 4" />, not <InlineMath math="-4" /> | <InlineMath math="(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4" />, so <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-4} = 2i" /> |
| Dividing only the real part by <InlineMath math="2a" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{-4 \pm 2i}{2}" /> turns into <InlineMath math="-2 \pm 2i" /> | Divide every term: <InlineMath math="-2 \pm i" /> |
| Taking <InlineMath math="i^2 = 1" /> | The imaginary unit is defined by <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" /> | Substitute the root into the equation, the result must be <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Dropping the minus sign in <InlineMath math="-b" /> | For <InlineMath math="x^2 - 6x + 13 = 0" />, the real part is <InlineMath math="3" />, not <InlineMath math="-3" /> | The real part equals the x-coordinate of the vertex |
| Thinking the graph crosses the axis at the real part | A parabola with <InlineMath math="D < 0" /> never touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis | Evaluate the function at the vertex, the result is not zero |

The quickest check uses **the sum and product of the roots**: the sum must be <InlineMath math="-\frac{b}{a}" /> and the product <InlineMath math="\frac{c}{a}" />.

## Practice Problems

Compute the discriminant first, then write the roots as **the real part plus or minus the imaginary part**.

1. Solve <InlineMath math="x^2 - 6x + 13 = 0" />.

2. Solve <InlineMath math="2x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0" /> over the complex numbers.

3. Find every value of <InlineMath math="k" /> for which <InlineMath math="x^2 + kx + 9 = 0" /> has imaginary roots.

4. Write a quadratic equation with integer coefficients whose roots are <InlineMath math="1 + 2i" /> and <InlineMath math="1 - 2i" />.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. Compute **the discriminant first**. It is <InlineMath math="D = (-6)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 13 = 36 - 52 = -16" />, so <InlineMath math="\sqrt{D} = 4i" />:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{6 \pm 4i}{2} = 3 \pm 2i" />

Check: the sum is <InlineMath math="6 = -\frac{b}{a}" />, and the product is <InlineMath math="3^2 + 2^2 = 13 = \frac{c}{a}" />. The vertex of <InlineMath math="(x - 3)^2 + 4" /> is <InlineMath math="(3, 4)" />, which fits the vertex rule <InlineMath math="3 \pm i\sqrt{4}" />.

**Problem 2**. The discriminant is <InlineMath math="D = 2^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = -36" />, so <InlineMath math="\sqrt{D} = 6i" />:

<BlockMath math="x = \frac{-2 \pm 6i}{4} = -\frac12 \pm \frac32 i" />

Check: the sum is <InlineMath math="-1 = -\frac{b}{a}" />, and the product is <InlineMath math="\frac14 + \frac94 = \frac52 = \frac{c}{a}" />.

**Problem 3**. Imaginary roots appear exactly when the discriminant is negative:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="D = k^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 9 = k^2 - 36 < 0" />
  <BlockMath math="k^2 < 36 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -6 < k < 6" />
</MathContainer>

Check two values. For <InlineMath math="k = 0" />, the equation <InlineMath math="x^2 + 9 = 0" /> gives <InlineMath math="x = \pm 3i" />. For <InlineMath math="k = 6" />, <InlineMath math="D = 0" /> and the root is the repeated real number <InlineMath math="-3" />, so the endpoint <InlineMath math="6" /> is not included.

**Problem 4**. Use the sum and product of the roots:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x_1 + x_2 = (1 + 2i) + (1 - 2i) = 2" />
  <BlockMath math="x_1 x_2 = 1^2 - (2i)^2 = 1 + 4 = 5" />
  <BlockMath math="x^2 - 2x + 5 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the discriminant is <InlineMath math="4 - 20 = -16" />, and the quadratic formula gives <InlineMath math="\frac{2 \pm 4i}{2} = 1 \pm 2i" />. Any nonzero integer multiple of this equation works too, for example <InlineMath math="2x^2 - 4x + 10 = 0" />.
