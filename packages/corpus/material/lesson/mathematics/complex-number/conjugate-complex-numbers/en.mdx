## The Conjugate Flips the Sign of the Imaginary Part

The **conjugate** of a complex number <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" /> is <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = x - iy" />: the real part stays, and **the sign of the imaginary part flips**. In the complex plane, <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" /> is the reflection of <InlineMath math="z" /> across the real axis.

<BlockMath math="z = x + iy \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar{z} = x - iy" />

The product of a number and its conjugate is always real, <InlineMath math="z\bar{z} = x^2 + y^2" />. With that property, a complex denominator can be turned into a real number, so you can **divide complex numbers**.

## Examples of Finding the Conjugate

Write **the imaginary component with its sign** before you flip it. A real number does not change because its imaginary component is zero, while a purely imaginary number turns into its opposite.

| Number | Real component | Imaginary component | Conjugate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="2+i" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2-i" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2=2+0i" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="2-0i=2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3-2i" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="-2" /> | <InlineMath math="3-(-2)i=3+2i" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3i=0+3i" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="0-3i=-3i" /> |

The number <InlineMath math="3 - 2i" /> is the one most often wrong. The imaginary part of <InlineMath math="3 - 2i" /> is <InlineMath math="-2" />, so flipping its sign gives <InlineMath math="+2" />, and the conjugate is <InlineMath math="3 + 2i" />.

A reflection keeps lengths, so <InlineMath math="|\bar{z}| = |z|" />. Only the direction turns over: if <InlineMath math="z" /> makes the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> with the positive real axis, <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" /> makes the angle <InlineMath math="-\theta" />. For <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 2i" />, both have [modulus](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/modulus-argument-complex-numbers) <InlineMath math="\sqrt{13}" />, and their arguments are about <InlineMath math="33.7^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="-33.7^\circ" />. This rule of opposite arguments holds for <InlineMath math="z \ne 0" />, up to whole turns of <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />: for a negative real number such as <InlineMath math="-2" />, the arguments of <InlineMath math="z" /> and <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" /> both equal <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />.

## Adding and Multiplying with the Conjugate

When you add <InlineMath math="z" /> and <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" />, **the imaginary parts cancel**. When you subtract them, the real parts cancel:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}z+\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)+(x-iy)=2x=2\operatorname{Re}(z)\\z-\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)-(x-iy)=2iy=2i\operatorname{Im}(z)\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 2i" />, the sum is <InlineMath math="6 = 2\operatorname{Re}(z)" /> and the difference is <InlineMath math="4i = 2i\operatorname{Im}(z)" />. In the product, the cross terms cancel, and the result is always **real and nonnegative**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}z\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)(x-iy)\\&amp;=x^2-ixy+ixy-i^2y^2\\&amp;=x^2+y^2=|z|^2\end{aligned}" />

Check with <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 2i" />: <InlineMath math="(3 + 2i)(3 - 2i) = 9 - 4i^2 = 9 + 4 = 13" />, the same as <InlineMath math="|z|^2 = 3^2 + 2^2" />.

## Dividing Complex Numbers with the Conjugate

To divide by a complex number, multiply the numerator and the denominator by **the conjugate of the denominator**. The denominator becomes the real number <InlineMath math="|z|^2" />, so the result can be written as <InlineMath math="a + bi" />.

Compute <InlineMath math="\frac{3 + 4i}{1 - 2i}" />. The conjugate of the denominator is <InlineMath math="1 + 2i" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{3 + 4i}{1 - 2i} \cdot \frac{1 + 2i}{1 + 2i} = \frac{(3 + 4i)(1 + 2i)}{1^2 + 2^2}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{3 + 6i + 4i + 8i^2}{5} = \frac{-5 + 10i}{5} = -1 + 2i" />
</MathContainer>

Check by multiplying back: <InlineMath math="(-1 + 2i)(1 - 2i) = -1 + 2i + 2i - 4i^2 = 3 + 4i" />, the original numerator. The same method also computes the [inverse of a complex number](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/inverse-complex-numbers), because <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}" /> for <InlineMath math="z \ne 0" />.

## When a Number Equals Its Conjugate

Suppose <InlineMath math="z = \bar{z}" />, that is <InlineMath math="x + iy = x - iy" />. Subtract <InlineMath math="x" /> from both sides to get <InlineMath math="iy = -iy" />, or <InlineMath math="2iy = 0" />. Since <InlineMath math="i \ne 0" />, it follows that <InlineMath math="y = 0" />.

So a complex number equals its conjugate **if and only if it is real**. Geometrically, only points on the real axis stay where they are when reflected across the real axis.

## Rules for Conjugates in Operations

For addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and inverses, the conjugate **can be taken before or after the operation** with the same result. The rules below come from operating on the real and imaginary components. The examples use <InlineMath math="z_1 = 1 + i" /> and <InlineMath math="z_2 = 2 - i" />, and the denominator in division and inverses must be nonzero.

| Operation | Identity | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sum | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1+z_2}=\bar z_1+\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{(1+i)+(2-i)}=\bar3=3=(1-i)+(2+i)" /> |
| Difference | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1-z_2}=\bar z_1-\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{-1+2i}=-1-2i=(1-i)-(2+i)" /> |
| Product | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1z_2}=\bar z_1\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{3+i}=3-i=(1-i)(2+i)" /> |
| Quotient | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1/z_2}=\bar z_1/\bar z_2,\ z_2\ne0" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{(1+3i)/5}=(1-3i)/5=(1-i)/(2+i)" /> |
| Inverse | <InlineMath math="\overline{z^{-1}}=(\bar z)^{-1},\ z\ne0" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{1/(1+i)}=(1+i)/2=1/(1-i)" /> |
| Double conjugate | <InlineMath math="\overline{\bar z}=z" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{\overline{1+i}}=\overline{1-i}=1+i" /> |

The product rule is proved by expanding both sides:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\overline{z_1z_2}&amp;=(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)-i(x_1y_2+y_1x_2)\\&amp;=(x_1-iy_1)(x_2-iy_2)=\bar z_1\bar z_2\end{aligned}" />

These rules explain why the [imaginary roots of a quadratic equation](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-imaginary-root) with real coefficients always come in pairs. If <InlineMath math="az^2 + bz + c = 0" /> with real <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, <InlineMath math="c" />, then <InlineMath math="a\bar{z}^2 + b\bar{z} + c = \overline{az^2 + bz + c} = \bar{0} = 0" />, so <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" /> is a root too.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flipping the sign of the real part too | <InlineMath math="\overline{3 + 2i}" /> is not <InlineMath math="-3 - 2i" /> | Only the term with <InlineMath math="i" /> changes sign |
| Taking <InlineMath math="3 - 2i" /> as its own conjugate | Its imaginary part is <InlineMath math="-2" />, so the sign becomes <InlineMath math="+2" /> | The reflection must land on the other side of the real axis |
| Flipping the sign of the <InlineMath math="i^2" /> term, for example writing the conjugate of <InlineMath math="2 + i^2" /> as <InlineMath math="2 - i^2 = 3" /> | <InlineMath math="2 + i^2" /> is really the real number <InlineMath math="1" /> | Replace <InlineMath math="i^2" /> with <InlineMath math="-1" /> first |
| Multiplying by the conjugate of the numerator when dividing | The denominator stays complex | Multiply by the conjugate of the denominator so it becomes <InlineMath math="\lvert z \rvert^2" /> |
| Writing <InlineMath math="z\bar{z} = x^2 - y^2" /> | <InlineMath math="-i^2 y^2 = +y^2" /> | <InlineMath math="z\bar{z}" /> must equal the squared modulus |

The quickest check after dividing is **multiplying the result by the denominator**. The product must give back the numerator.

## Practice Problems

Write every number as <InlineMath math="x + iy" /> first, then **check the result by multiplying**.

1. Find the conjugate of <InlineMath math="2 + i^2" /> and of <InlineMath math="1 + \frac{1}{i}" />.

2. Compute <InlineMath math="\frac{2 - i}{3 + 4i}" /> in the form <InlineMath math="a + bi" />.

3. Find the complex number <InlineMath math="z" /> that satisfies <InlineMath math="z + 2\bar{z} = 9 - 2i" />.

4. For <InlineMath math="z = 5 - 12i" />, compute <InlineMath math="z\bar{z}" /> and use it to find <InlineMath math="|z|" />.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. **Simplify first**. Since <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" />, the number <InlineMath math="2 + i^2 = 1" /> is real, so its conjugate is <InlineMath math="1" /> too. For the second number, multiply <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{i}" /> by <InlineMath math="\frac{-i}{-i}" />:

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{i} = \frac{-i}{-i^2} = \frac{-i}{1} = -i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 + \frac{1}{i} = 1 - i" />

So its conjugate is <InlineMath math="1 + i" />. Check: <InlineMath math="i \cdot (-i) = -i^2 = 1" />, so <InlineMath math="-i" /> really is the reciprocal of <InlineMath math="i" />.

**Problem 2**. Multiply by the conjugate of the denominator, <InlineMath math="3 - 4i" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{(2 - i)(3 - 4i)}{3^2 + 4^2} = \frac{6 - 8i - 3i + 4i^2}{25}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{2 - 11i}{25} = \frac{2}{25} - \frac{11}{25}i" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{2}{25} - \frac{11}{25}i\right)(3 + 4i) = \frac{6 + 8i - 33i - 44i^2}{25} = \frac{50 - 25i}{25} = 2 - i" />.

**Problem 3**. Write <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" />, so <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = x - iy" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(x + iy) + 2(x - iy) = 3x - iy" />
  <BlockMath math="3x - iy = 9 - 2i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 3, \quad y = 2" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> are real, the real parts and the imaginary parts of both sides must match. So <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 2i" />. Check: <InlineMath math="(3 + 2i) + 2(3 - 2i) = 3 + 2i + 6 - 4i = 9 - 2i" />.

**Problem 4**. Multiply by the conjugate:

<BlockMath math="(5 - 12i)(5 + 12i) = 25 - 144i^2 = 25 + 144 = 169" />

Since <InlineMath math="z\bar{z} = |z|^2" />, the modulus is <InlineMath math="|z| = \sqrt{169} = 13" />. Check with the modulus formula: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{5^2 + (-12)^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13" />.
