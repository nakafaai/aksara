## Changing the Sign of the Imaginary Part

Every complex number <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" /> has a **conjugate**, denoted by <InlineMath math="\bar{z}" />.

It is obtained by <Highlight>reversing the sign of the imaginary part</Highlight>. The real part keeps its value and its sign, so only the term multiplied by <InlineMath math="i" /> changes.

## Definition of the Complex Conjugate

If <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" /> is a complex number, with <InlineMath math="x" /> as the real part and <InlineMath math="y" /> as the imaginary part, then its conjugate is:

<BlockMath math="\bar{z} = x - iy" />

This means the **real part** (<InlineMath math="x" />) stays the same, while the sign of the imaginary part (<InlineMath math="y" />) is flipped (positive becomes negative, negative becomes positive).

## Applying the Conjugate Rule to Different Forms

Write down the **imaginary component with its sign** before reversing that sign. A real number stays unchanged because its imaginary component is zero. A purely imaginary number becomes its own negative.

| Number | Real component | Imaginary component | Conjugate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="2+i" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2-i" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2=2+0i" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="2-0i=2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3-2i" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="-2" /> | <InlineMath math="3-(-2)i=3+2i" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3i=0+3i" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="0-3i=-3i" /> |

## Equality with the Conjugate

Is it possible for a complex number <InlineMath math="z=x+iy" /> to be equal to its conjugate <InlineMath math="\bar{z}=x-iy" />? If so, what is the condition?

**Derivation**:

Start with <InlineMath math="z = \bar{z}" />:

<BlockMath math="x+iy = x-iy" />

This can only happen if <InlineMath math="iy = -iy" />, which means <InlineMath math="2iy = 0" />.

Since <InlineMath math="i \neq 0" />, it must be that <InlineMath math="y=0" />.

So, a complex number is equal to its conjugate if and only if its **imaginary part is zero**, or in other words, if the complex number is **real**.

## Properties of Conjugate Operations

These rules follow from **operations on real and imaginary components**. Compare them using the same example numbers. For division and inverses, the denominator must be nonzero.

| Operation | Identity | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sum | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1+z_2}=\bar z_1+\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{(1+i)+(2-i)}=\bar3=3=(1-i)+(2+i)" /> |
| Difference | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1-z_2}=\bar z_1-\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{-1+2i}=-1-2i=(1-i)-(2+i)" /> |
| Product | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1z_2}=\bar z_1\bar z_2" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{3+i}=3-i=(1-i)(2+i)" /> |
| Quotient | <InlineMath math="\overline{z_1/z_2}=\bar z_1/\bar z_2,\ z_2\ne0" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{(1+3i)/5}=(1-3i)/5=(1-i)/(2+i)" /> |
| Inverse | <InlineMath math="\overline{z^{-1}}=(\bar z)^{-1},\ z\ne0" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{1/(1+i)}=(1+i)/2=1/(1-i)" /> |
| Double conjugate | <InlineMath math="\overline{\bar z}=z" /> | <InlineMath math="\overline{\overline{1+i}}=\overline{1-i}=1+i" /> |

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\overline{z_1z_2}&amp;=(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)-i(x_1y_2+y_1x_2)\\&amp;=(x_1-iy_1)(x_2-iy_2)=\bar z_1\bar z_2\end{aligned}" />

### Recovering Components and the Modulus

When you add a conjugate pair, the **imaginary components cancel**. When you subtract the pair, the real components cancel instead:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}z+\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)+(x-iy)=2x=2\operatorname{Re}(z)\\z-\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)-(x-iy)=2iy=2i\operatorname{Im}(z)\end{aligned}" />

In the product, the cross terms also cancel. The result is always **real and nonnegative** because it is a sum of two squares:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}z\bar z&amp;=(x+iy)(x-iy)\\&amp;=x^2-ixy+ixy-i^2y^2\\&amp;=x^2+y^2=|z|^2\end{aligned}" />

## Exercise

Find the conjugate of each complex number. Two of the three contain a power of <InlineMath math="i" /> or a division by <InlineMath math="i" />, so **simplify the expression first** and read the conjugate from the simplified form.

1. <InlineMath math="2+i^2" />
2. <InlineMath math="1+\frac{1}{i}" />
3. <InlineMath math="1+2i" />

### Worked Solutions

1. Simplify the complex number:

   <InlineMath math="z = 2+i^2 = 2+(-1) = 1" />. Since <InlineMath math="z=1" /> is
   a real number (<InlineMath math="1+0i" />
   ),

   its conjugate is <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = 1" />.

2. Simplify first:

   Rationalize the denominator by multiplying by <InlineMath math="-i" />:

   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{i} &amp;= \frac{1}{i} \times \frac{-i}{-i} \\ &amp;= \frac{-i}{-i^2} \\ &amp;= \frac{-i}{-(-1)} \\ &amp;= \frac{-i}{1} \\ &amp;= -i \end{aligned}" />

   So, <InlineMath math="z = 1 + \frac{1}{i} = 1 - i" />.

   Its conjugate is <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = 1 - (-i) = 1 + i" />.

3. <InlineMath math="z = 1+2i" />.

   Apply the **imaginary sign change**: <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = 1 - 2i" />.
