## The Need for Complex Numbers

Consider the quadratic equation <InlineMath math="x^2 - 1 = 0" />. Factoring gives <InlineMath math="(x-1)(x+1) = 0" />, so its two solutions, <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-1" />, are real numbers.

The equation <InlineMath math="x^2 + 1 = 0" /> is different. It requires <InlineMath math="x^2 = -1" />, but the square of every real number is nonnegative. The equation therefore has no real solution.

Complex numbers <Highlight>extend the real number system</Highlight> so that equations such as this one can be solved.

## Imaginary Numbers

The **imaginary unit**, denoted by <InlineMath math="i" />, is defined by the equation

<BlockMath math="i^2 = -1" />

With the principal square root convention, this is also written as

<BlockMath math="\sqrt{-1}=i" />

For a nonnegative real number <InlineMath math="a" />, the principal square root of <InlineMath math="-a" /> is <InlineMath math="i\sqrt{a}" />. For example:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sqrt{-4} = i\sqrt{4} = 2i" />
  <BlockMath math="\sqrt{-9} = i\sqrt{9} = 3i" />
</MathContainer>

Numbers like <InlineMath math="2i" /> and <InlineMath math="3i" /> are called **purely imaginary numbers**.

The **principal square root** is one value, but a quadratic equation may have two solutions. Both of the following values satisfy the original equation:

<BlockMath math="i^2+1=-1+1=0,\qquad(-i)^2+1=i^2+1=0" />

Multiplying by <InlineMath math="i" /> turns a number a quarter turn around the origin. Start at <InlineMath math="1" />: one turn gives <InlineMath math="i" />, a second gives <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" />, a third gives <InlineMath math="-i" />, and a fourth brings you back to <InlineMath math="1" />. That is why <InlineMath math="i^2 = -1" /> is no contradiction: two quarter turns point the opposite way.

## General Form

Complex numbers are generally written in the form <InlineMath math="z = a + bi" />, where:

- <InlineMath math="a=\operatorname{Re}(z)" /> is the **real part**.
- <InlineMath math="b=\operatorname{Im}(z)" /> is the **imaginary part**.
- <InlineMath math="i" /> is the imaginary unit (<InlineMath math="\sqrt{-1}" />
  ).

Both <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are real numbers. The expression <InlineMath math="bi" /> is the imaginary term, while its coefficient <InlineMath math="b" /> is the imaginary part.

Every complex number <InlineMath math="a + bi" /> is a point in the plane: the real part <InlineMath math="a" /> across and the imaginary part <InlineMath math="b" /> up. Real numbers such as <InlineMath math="4" /> sit on the horizontal axis, and pure imaginary numbers such as <InlineMath math="2i" /> sit on the vertical axis.

### Identifying Real and Imaginary Parts

Read the **coefficient of the imaginary unit**, not the entire imaginary term. Thus the imaginary part of five minus four times the imaginary unit is minus four, not minus four times the imaginary unit.

| Number | Real-plus-imaginary form | Real part | Imaginary part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="2+3i" /> | <InlineMath math="2+3i" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5-4i" /> | <InlineMath math="5+(-4)i" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="-4" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\sqrt2" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt2+0i" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt2" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-7i" /> | <InlineMath math="0+(-7)i" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="-7" /> |

Read the parts of <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 2i" /> as two steps: <InlineMath math="3" /> along the real axis, then <InlineMath math="2" /> up. <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Re}(z) = 3" /> is the horizontal step, <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Im}(z) = 2" /> is the vertical step. The imaginary part is the number <InlineMath math="2" />, not <InlineMath math="2i" />.

## Exercise

Determine the **real and imaginary parts** of each complex number below. Three of these expressions hide their value inside a square root or a power of <InlineMath math="i" />, so simplify each one first and only then name the two parts.

1. <InlineMath math="2 + \sqrt{(-2)^2}" />
2. <InlineMath math="2 + i^2" />
3. <InlineMath math="1 + \sqrt{-9}" />
4. <InlineMath math="1 + 2i" />

### Worked Solutions

**Simplify first**, then read both parts from the standard form. The principal square root of a positive number is nonnegative. Therefore, the square root of the square of minus two is two, not minus two.

| Problem | Simplified expression | Real part | Imaginary part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2+\sqrt{(-2)^2}=2+\sqrt4=2+2=4" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="2+i^2=2-1=1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="1+\sqrt{-9}=1+3i" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="1+2i" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |
