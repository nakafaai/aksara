## The Argument of a Product and a Quotient

When two nonzero complex numbers are multiplied, **their arguments add**, and when they are divided, the arguments subtract. For the principal argument, that sum or difference can leave the range <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" />, so add or subtract one full turn when needed:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2) = \operatorname{Arg} z_1 + \operatorname{Arg} z_2 + 360^\circ k" />
  <BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \operatorname{Arg} z_1 - \operatorname{Arg} z_2 + 360^\circ k" />
</MathContainer>

The integer <InlineMath math="k" /> is always <InlineMath math="-1" />, <InlineMath math="0" />, or <InlineMath math="1" />, because the sum or difference of two angles in the principal range lies between <InlineMath math="-360^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />. For example, <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(i) = 90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(1 + i) = 45^\circ" /> give <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\big(i(1 + i)\big) = 135^\circ" />, and indeed <InlineMath math="i(1 + i) = -1 + i" />.

The moduli are multiplied or divided as well, but a positive modulus does not change a direction. That is why the argument of a product depends only on the arguments of its two factors.

## Why the Angles Add

Write both numbers in polar form, <InlineMath math="z_1 = r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)" /> and <InlineMath math="z_2 = r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)" />. When you multiply them and group the real and imaginary terms, **the angle sum identities** for cosine and sine appear:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\frac{z_1z_2}{r_1r_2}&amp;=(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\\&amp;\quad+i(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\\&amp;=\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)+i\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{aligned}" />

The product has modulus <InlineMath math="r_1 r_2" /> and argument <InlineMath math="\theta_1 + \theta_2" />, which matches [multiplication of complex numbers](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/multiplication-complex-numbers) in polar form. Division undoes multiplication: if <InlineMath math="w = \frac{z_1}{z_2}" />, then <InlineMath math="w z_2 = z_1" />, so <InlineMath math="\arg w + \arg z_2 = \arg z_1" />, and the argument of <InlineMath math="w" /> is the difference of the two arguments.

## When the Sum of Angles Leaves the Principal Range

**Example 1**. Let <InlineMath math="z_1 = -1 + i" /> with <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_1 = 135^\circ" />, and <InlineMath math="z_2 = -1 + \sqrt{3}i" /> with <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_2 = 120^\circ" />. The sum of the arguments passes <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, so subtract one turn:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2) = 135^\circ + 120^\circ - 360^\circ = -105^\circ" />

Check by multiplying directly:

<BlockMath math="(-1 + i)(-1 + \sqrt{3}i) = (1 - \sqrt{3}) - (1 + \sqrt{3})i \approx -0.73 - 2.73i" />

Both components are negative, so **the product lies in quadrant III**, where the principal argument is negative. The reference angle is <InlineMath math="\arctan\frac{2.73}{0.73} \approx 75^\circ" />, and <InlineMath math="-180^\circ + 75^\circ = -105^\circ" />, which matches the formula. The modulus matches too: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2} \cdot 2 = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83" />.

**Example 2**. For the quotient <InlineMath math="\frac{-1 - i}{i}" />, the numerator has argument <InlineMath math="-135^\circ" /> and the denominator <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. Their difference is below <InlineMath math="-180^\circ" />, so add one turn:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{-1 - i}{i}\right) = -135^\circ - 90^\circ + 360^\circ = 135^\circ" />

Check: multiply the numerator and denominator by <InlineMath math="-i" />, so <InlineMath math="\frac{-1 - i}{i} = (-1 - i)(-i) = i + i^2 = -1 + i" />. The number <InlineMath math="-1 + i" /> does have principal argument <InlineMath math="135^\circ" />.

## The Argument of a Power and a Conjugate

A power is repeated multiplication, so **the argument of the <InlineMath math="n" />th power is <InlineMath math="n" /> times the argument**, brought back into the principal range:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(z^n) = n \operatorname{Arg} z + 360^\circ k" />

For <InlineMath math="(-1 + i)^3" />, the value <InlineMath math="3 \cdot 135^\circ = 405^\circ" /> minus one turn gives <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />. Check: <InlineMath math="(-1 + i)^2 = -2i" />, then <InlineMath math="-2i(-1 + i) = 2 + 2i" />, a number in quadrant I with argument <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />.

The [conjugate](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/conjugate-complex-numbers) <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = x - iy" /> is the mirror image of <InlineMath math="z" /> in the real axis, so its argument changes sign: <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\bar{z} = -\operatorname{Arg} z" />. The one exception is the negative real axis. The number <InlineMath math="-4" /> equals its own conjugate, and both have principal argument <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, because <InlineMath math="-180^\circ" /> is outside the range.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing the sum of arguments without adjusting it | <InlineMath math="135^\circ + 120^\circ = 255^\circ" /> lies outside the principal range | The final result must lie between <InlineMath math="-180^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> |
| Dividing the arguments when dividing the numbers | A quotient subtracts the arguments instead of dividing them | Multiply the result by the denominator and check its argument |
| Subtracting in the wrong order | <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\frac{z_1}{z_2}" /> uses the numerator's argument minus the denominator's | For <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{i} = -i" />, the argument is <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" />, not <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> |
| Thinking the modulus affects the direction | A positive modulus only changes the length of the vector | The argument of <InlineMath math="2z" /> equals the argument of <InlineMath math="z" /> |
| Writing <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\bar{z} = -180^\circ" /> for a negative real number | The left end of the range is excluded | A negative real number equals its own conjugate |

The most reliable check is **computing the resulting number directly**, then reading its quadrant. The quadrant confirms the direction of the argument. Then check that the value lies in <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" />, because <InlineMath math="225^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="-135^\circ" /> both point into quadrant III.

## Exercises

The first two problems practice the adjustment after a product and a quotient, the third uses a power, and the fourth uses a conjugate. **Compute the sum, difference, or multiple first**, then bring it back into <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" />.

1. Given <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_1 = 150^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_2 = 100^\circ" />, find <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2)" />.

2. Given <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_1 = -170^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z_2 = 40^\circ" />, find <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)" />.

3. Find <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\big((1 + i)^6\big)" /> and check it by computing the power.

4. Find the principal argument of the conjugate of <InlineMath math="z = -\sqrt{3} + i" />.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. The sum of the arguments passes <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, so subtract one turn:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2) = 150^\circ + 100^\circ - 360^\circ = -110^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="-110^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="250^\circ" /> give the same direction, because <InlineMath math="\cos(-110^\circ) = \cos 250^\circ \approx -0.342" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin(-110^\circ) = \sin 250^\circ \approx -0.940" />. **Only <InlineMath math="-110^\circ" /> lies in the principal range**.

**Problem 2**. The difference of the arguments is below <InlineMath math="-180^\circ" />, so add one turn:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = -170^\circ - 40^\circ + 360^\circ = 150^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="150^\circ + 40^\circ = 190^\circ" />, and <InlineMath math="190^\circ - 360^\circ = -170^\circ" />, so **the quotient times the denominator returns to the direction of <InlineMath math="z_1" />**.

**Problem 3**. The argument of <InlineMath math="1 + i" /> is <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, so <InlineMath math="6 \cdot 45^\circ = 270^\circ" />, then subtract one turn:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\big((1 + i)^6\big) = 270^\circ - 360^\circ = -90^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="(1 + i)^2 = 2i" />, so <InlineMath math="(1 + i)^6 = (2i)^3 = 8i^3 = -8i" />. The number <InlineMath math="-8i" /> lies on the negative imaginary axis, so **its argument is indeed <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" />**.

**Problem 4**. The point <InlineMath math="(-\sqrt{3}, 1)" /> lies in quadrant II with <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Arg} z = 150^\circ" />. The conjugate mirrors the point in the real axis:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Arg}\bar{z} = -150^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="\bar{z} = -\sqrt{3} - i" /> lies in quadrant III, with reference angle <InlineMath math="30^\circ" /> and argument <InlineMath math="-180^\circ + 30^\circ = -150^\circ" />, so **the mirror image flips the sign of the argument**.
