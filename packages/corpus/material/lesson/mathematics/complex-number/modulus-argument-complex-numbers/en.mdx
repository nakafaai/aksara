## The Length and Direction of a Complex Number

A complex number <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" /> is drawn as the point <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> on the complex plane, or as a vector from the origin to that point. The vector has a length and a direction. Its length is the **modulus** <InlineMath math="|z|" />, and its direction is the **argument** <InlineMath math="\arg z" />, the angle from the positive real axis to the vector:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}" />
  <BlockMath math="\cos\theta = \frac{x}{|z|}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{y}{|z|}" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="z = 3 + 4i" />, the modulus is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5" />, and the argument is <InlineMath math="\arctan\frac{4}{3} \approx 53.13^\circ" />. The interactive graph draws that vector with its components <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 4" />, which form a right triangle with hypotenuse <InlineMath math="|z|" />.

The origin has modulus <InlineMath math="0" />, but **the argument of zero is undefined**, because the zero vector has no direction.

## Finding the Modulus with the Pythagorean Theorem

The modulus is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs <InlineMath math="|x|" /> and <InlineMath math="|y|" />, so **the modulus is always zero or positive**. A negative component is squared together with its sign, so the sign disappears:

| Number | Calculation | Modulus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="3 + 4i" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25}" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-1 - 2i" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{(-1)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{5}" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{5}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{5^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{25}" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-2i" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{0^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{4}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

The real number <InlineMath math="5" /> has modulus <InlineMath math="5" />, the same as its absolute value, so the modulus extends absolute value to the complex plane. Check <InlineMath math="-1 - 2i" />: its distance <InlineMath math="\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24" /> is larger than either component but less than their sum <InlineMath math="1 + 2 = 3" />, exactly like the hypotenuse of a triangle. The modulus of a product is the product of the moduli, <InlineMath math="|z_1 z_2| = |z_1|\,|z_2|" />, one of the [properties of the modulus](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/properties-modulus-complex-numbers).

## Reference Angle and Quadrant Rules

A calculator returns <InlineMath math="\arctan\frac{y}{x}" /> only between <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, so for points in quadrants II and III the result points the wrong way. First find the **acute reference angle** from the lengths of the two components, then use the signs of <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> to pick the quadrant:

<BlockMath math="\alpha = \arctan\left|\frac{y}{x}\right|, \qquad 0^\circ < \alpha < 90^\circ" />

| Quadrant | Component signs | Argument in <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | <InlineMath math="x > 0,\ y > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> |
| II | <InlineMath math="x < 0,\ y > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="180^\circ - \alpha" /> |
| III | <InlineMath math="x < 0,\ y < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="-180^\circ + \alpha" /> |
| IV | <InlineMath math="x > 0,\ y < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="-\alpha" /> |

Points on an axis need no quotient <InlineMath math="\frac{y}{x}" />. Their direction reads off directly: the positive real axis is <InlineMath math="0^\circ" />, the positive imaginary axis <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, the negative real axis <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, and the negative imaginary axis <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" />.

## Examples of Finding the Modulus and Argument

**Example 1**. For <InlineMath math="z = -\sqrt{3} + i" />, the point <InlineMath math="(-\sqrt{3}, 1)" /> lies in quadrant II, because <InlineMath math="x < 0" /> and <InlineMath math="y > 0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2, \qquad \alpha = \arctan\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = 30^\circ" />
  <BlockMath math="\arg z = 180^\circ - 30^\circ = 150^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check by rebuilding the components: <InlineMath math="2\cos 150^\circ = -\sqrt{3}" /> and <InlineMath math="2\sin 150^\circ = 1" />. Both match <InlineMath math="z" />, so **the angle and its quadrant are right**.

**Example 2**. For <InlineMath math="z = -1 - \sqrt{3}i" />, both components are negative, so the point lies in quadrant III:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2, \qquad \alpha = \arctan\sqrt{3} = 60^\circ" />
  <BlockMath math="\arg z = -180^\circ + 60^\circ = -120^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="2\cos(-120^\circ) = -1" /> and <InlineMath math="2\sin(-120^\circ) = -\sqrt{3}" />. If you only press <InlineMath math="\arctan\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{-1} = \arctan\sqrt{3}" /> on a calculator, you get <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />, the opposite direction, exactly half a turn away.

## The Polar Form from Modulus and Argument

The modulus <InlineMath math="r" /> and the argument <InlineMath math="\theta" /> give the **polar form** <InlineMath math="z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)" />, one of several [forms of a complex number](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/complex-number-form). To return to Cartesian form, multiply the modulus by the cosine and sine of the angle.

**Example 3**. The number <InlineMath math="z = 2 + 2i" /> lies in quadrant I with <InlineMath math="r = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="\theta = 45^\circ" />, so <InlineMath math="z = 2\sqrt{2}(\cos 45^\circ + i\sin 45^\circ)" />. Check: <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 2" /> for both components.

**Example 4**. The number <InlineMath math="z = 6(\cos 120^\circ + i\sin 120^\circ)" /> converts to Cartesian form:

<BlockMath math="z = 6\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + 6\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)i = -3 + 3\sqrt{3}i" />

Check the modulus: <InlineMath math="\sqrt{9 + 27} = \sqrt{36} = 6" />, and the point <InlineMath math="(-3, 3\sqrt{3})" /> lies in quadrant II, as the angle <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> requires. The angle in a polar form is usually chosen from <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" />, which gives the [principal argument](/en/subjects/mathematics/complex-number/principal-argument-complex-numbers).

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using <InlineMath math="\arctan\frac{y}{x}" /> without checking the quadrant | The result always lies between <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> | Rebuild <InlineMath math="r\cos\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="r\sin\theta" />, their signs must match |
| Writing a negative modulus | The modulus is a distance | A square root is always zero or positive |
| Computing the modulus as <InlineMath math="\lvert x \rvert + \lvert y \rvert" /> | That is the path along the axes, not the straight distance | For <InlineMath math="3 + 4i" />, the distance is <InlineMath math="5" />, not <InlineMath math="7" /> |
| Giving an argument for <InlineMath math="z = 0" /> | The zero vector has no direction | The argument is defined only for <InlineMath math="z \ne 0" /> |
| Mixing degrees and radians | A calculator can be set to the other unit | <InlineMath math="\arctan 1" /> must be <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> or <InlineMath math="\frac{\pi}{4}" /> |

The strongest check is **rebuilding the components from the modulus and argument**. If <InlineMath math="r\cos\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="r\sin\theta" /> return <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />, both of your answers are right.

## Exercises

The first problem lies in quadrant I, the second and third on an axis, and the fourth in quadrant IV with an angle that is not a special angle. **Locate the point first**, then find the modulus and the argument in <InlineMath math="(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]" />.

1. Find the modulus and argument of <InlineMath math="z = 2 + 2i" />.

2. Find the modulus and argument of <InlineMath math="z = -4" />.

3. Find the modulus and argument of <InlineMath math="z = -i" />.

4. Find the modulus and argument of <InlineMath math="z = 5 - 12i" />, with the argument rounded to two decimal places.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. The point <InlineMath math="(2, 2)" /> lies in quadrant I:

<BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{4 + 4} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad \arg z = \arctan 1 = 45^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2}\cos 45^\circ = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="2\sqrt{2}\sin 45^\circ = 2" />, so **both components come back intact**.

**Problem 2**. The number <InlineMath math="-4 = -4 + 0i" /> lies on the negative real axis, so no quotient <InlineMath math="\frac{y}{x}" /> is needed:

<BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{16} = 4, \qquad \arg z = 180^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="4\cos 180^\circ = -4" /> and <InlineMath math="4\sin 180^\circ = 0" />. The angle <InlineMath math="-180^\circ" /> points the same way, but **the left end of the interval is excluded**, so the answer is <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />.

**Problem 3**. The number <InlineMath math="-i = 0 - 1i" /> lies on the negative imaginary axis:

<BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{0 + 1} = 1, \qquad \arg z = -90^\circ" />

Check: <InlineMath math="\cos(-90^\circ) = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin(-90^\circ) = -1" />, so **the vector points straight down**.

**Problem 4**. The point <InlineMath math="(5, -12)" /> lies in quadrant IV, because <InlineMath math="x > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="y < 0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \arctan\frac{12}{5} \approx 67.38^\circ, \qquad \arg z \approx -67.38^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="13\cos(-67.38^\circ) \approx 5.00" /> and <InlineMath math="13\sin(-67.38^\circ) \approx -12.00" />, so **quadrant IV gives a negative argument**.
