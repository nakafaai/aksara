## Measures of Central Tendency

Measures of central tendency describe where a data set is centered. The median and mode describe that center in different ways.

## The Median as the Middle Value

The median is the **middle value of an ordered data set**.

**How to find the median:**

1. Sort the data from smallest to largest.
2. Find the middle position or positions.

- **If the number of data points is odd (<InlineMath math="n" /> is odd):**

  <BlockMath math="\text{Median} = \text{Value at position } \frac{n+1}{2}" />

  **Example with an odd number of values:**

  Suppose the scores of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ students}" /> are <InlineMath math="7, 8, 6, 9, 7" />.

  Sort: <InlineMath math="6, 7, 7, 8, 9" /> (there are <InlineMath math="5" /> data points, <InlineMath math="n=5" />)

  Middle position: <InlineMath math="(5+1)/2 = 3" />. The data at position <InlineMath math="3" /> is <InlineMath math="7" />.

  So, <InlineMath math="\text{Median} = 7" />.

- **If the number of data points is even (<InlineMath math="n" /> is even):**

  <BlockMath math="\text{Median} = \frac{\text{Value at position }(n/2) + \text{Value at position }((n/2)+1)}{2}" />

  **Example with an even number of values:**

  Suppose the scores of <InlineMath math="6 \text{ students}" /> are <InlineMath math="7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 10" />.

  Sort: <InlineMath math="6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10" /> (there are <InlineMath math="6" /> data points, <InlineMath math="n=6" />)

  Middle positions: Position <InlineMath math="6/2 = 3" /> and position <InlineMath math="(6/2) + 1 = 4" />.

  The two middle numbers are <InlineMath math="7" /> and <InlineMath math="8" />.

  Median is the average of <InlineMath math="7" /> and <InlineMath math="8" />: <InlineMath math="(7+8)/2 = 7.5" />.

  So, the median is <InlineMath math="7.5" />.

The median depends on the middle position in the ordered data. Extremely large or small values at the ends therefore have little effect on it.

## The Mode as the Most Frequent Value

The mode is the **value that appears most often**, meaning the value with the highest frequency.

**How to find the mode:**

1. Count how often each value appears.
2. Identify the value or values with the highest frequency.

**Example:**

Test scores:

<BlockMath math="7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 8, 7" />

- Score <InlineMath math="6" /> appears <InlineMath math="1 \text{ time}" />
- Score <InlineMath math="7" /> appears <InlineMath math="3 \text{ times}" />
- Score <InlineMath math="8" /> appears <InlineMath math="2 \text{ times}" />
- Score <InlineMath math="9" /> appears <InlineMath math="1 \text{ time}" />

The most frequent score is <InlineMath math="7" /> (appears <InlineMath math="3 \text{ times}" />).

So, the mode is <InlineMath math="7" />.

**Important notes about the mode:**

- If every value has the same frequency, no value is uniquely most frequent. Under the convention used here, the dataset is reported as having **no mode**.
- It can have **one mode** (unimodal).
- It can have **two modes** (bimodal), if there are two values with the same highest frequency.
- It can have **more than two modes** (multimodal).

A single isolated extreme value usually does not change the mode because its frequency is only one.

## Range Shows Data Spread

Besides locating the center with the median and mode, we may also want to know how **spread out** the data is. The **range** provides one quick measure.

The range is the **difference between the largest and smallest data values**.

<BlockMath math="\text{Range} = \text{Largest Data} - \text{Smallest Data}" />

**Example:**

Data: <InlineMath math="6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10" />

Largest data <InlineMath math="= 10" />

Smallest data <InlineMath math="= 6" />

Range <InlineMath math="= 10 - 6 = 4" />

The range shows the distance between the two endpoints, but it does not describe how values are distributed between them.

The median and mode describe the center of a data set, while the range describes its overall width.
