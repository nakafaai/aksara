## What Are Quartiles?

The median marks the middle of an ordered dataset at <InlineMath math="50\%" />. **Quartiles** extend that idea by marking three positions across the ordered data.

Those positions divide the distribution by rank into **four** parts, each representing about one quarter of the observations. With a finite dataset or tied values, the parts need not contain four visibly distinct sets of equal size.

There are three quartile boundaries:

1.  **Lower Quartile (<InlineMath math="Q_1" />):** The boundary at approximately the <InlineMath math="25\%" /> rank.
2.  **Middle Quartile (<InlineMath math="Q_2" />):** The **median**, at approximately the <InlineMath math="50\%" /> rank.
3.  **Upper Quartile (<InlineMath math="Q_3" />):** The boundary at approximately the <InlineMath math="75\%" /> rank.

Together, <InlineMath math="Q_1" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" />, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> describe the lower quarter, center, and upper quarter of an ordered distribution.

## How to Find the Position of Quartiles

How do we find the rank positions of <InlineMath math="Q_1" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" />, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> in ordered data?

Assume we have <InlineMath math="n" /> data points that we have sorted from smallest to largest.

Quartiles can be defined by several valid ranking conventions, so software packages may return slightly different values for the same small dataset. This lesson consistently uses the interpolated position <InlineMath math="p(n+1)" />, where <InlineMath math="p" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac14" />, <InlineMath math="\frac12" />, or <InlineMath math="\frac34" />. A position below <InlineMath math="1" /> is clamped to the minimum and a position above <InlineMath math="n" /> to the maximum. State the convention when comparing results.

### Lower Quartile

For the lower quartile:

<BlockMath math="\text{Position of } Q_1 = \text{Data point at }\frac{1}{4}(n+1)" />

- **If the result is a whole number**, for example <InlineMath math="5" />, then <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> is the value at position <InlineMath math="5" />.
- **If the result has a decimal**, for example <InlineMath math="5.25" />, then <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> lies between positions <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="6" />. We will calculate its interpolated value later; for now, we are only locating it.

**Example:**

Suppose we have <InlineMath math="20" /> data points (<InlineMath math="n=20" />).

The position of <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> is the data point at <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}(20+1)" />, which is data point <InlineMath math="\frac{21}{4}" /> or <InlineMath math="5.25" />.

This means <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> lies between positions <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="6" />.

### Middle Quartile

The middle quartile is the median:

<BlockMath math="\text{Position of } Q_2 = \text{Data point at }\frac{1}{2}(n+1)" />

The rules are the same as for <InlineMath math="Q_1" />:

- **If the result is a whole number**, say <InlineMath math="10" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" /> is the value of data point <InlineMath math="10" />.
- **If the result has a decimal**, say <InlineMath math="10.5" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" /> is between data points <InlineMath math="10" /> and <InlineMath math="11" />.

**Example (<InlineMath math="n=20" />):**

The position of <InlineMath math="Q_2" /> is the data point at <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}(20+1)" />, which is data point <InlineMath math="\frac{21}{2}" /> or <InlineMath math="10.5" />.

This means <InlineMath math="Q_2" /> (the median) is between data points <InlineMath math="10" /> and <InlineMath math="11" />.

### Upper Quartile

The formula is similar again:

<BlockMath math="\text{Position of } Q_3 = \text{Data point at }\frac{3}{4}(n+1)" />

The same interpolation rule applies:

- **If the result is a whole number**, say <InlineMath math="15" />, <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> is the value of data point <InlineMath math="15" />.
- **If the result has a decimal**, say <InlineMath math="15.75" />, <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> is between data points <InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="16" />.

**Example (<InlineMath math="n=20" />):**

The position of <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> is the data point at <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{4}(20+1)" />, which is data point <InlineMath math="\frac{63}{4}" /> or <InlineMath math="15.75" />.

This means <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> is between data points <InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="16" />.

## The Fourth Quartile

You might be wondering, "If there's <InlineMath math="Q_1" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" />, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" />, is there a <InlineMath math="Q_4" />?"

Technically, the concept of quartiles divides the data into four parts. <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> is the boundary for the first <InlineMath math="25\%" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" /> (the median) is the <InlineMath math="50\%" /> boundary, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> is the <InlineMath math="75\%" /> boundary. The final boundary, which encompasses <InlineMath math="100\%" /> of the data, is actually the **maximum value** of the dataset.

So, while we could refer to the maximum value as <InlineMath math="Q_4" />, in statistical analysis, we don't typically use the term <InlineMath math="Q_4" /> explicitly. The main focus is on <InlineMath math="Q_1" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" />, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" /> because they provide important information about the spread and center of the data in the lower, middle, and upper sections. The minimum value is sometimes called <InlineMath math="Q_0" />, but like <InlineMath math="Q_4" />, it's less commonly used than <InlineMath math="Q_1" />, <InlineMath math="Q_2" />, and <InlineMath math="Q_3" />.
