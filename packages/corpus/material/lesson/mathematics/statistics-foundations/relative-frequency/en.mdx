## Understanding Relative Frequency

Absolute frequency tells us **how many times** a value appears in a dataset.

For example, if we ask <InlineMath math="30 \text{ students}" /> about their phone brands, and <InlineMath math="10 \text{ students}" /> answer "Apple", then the frequency for the "Apple" brand is <InlineMath math="10" />.

**Relative frequency** places that count in relation to the total number of observations. It tells us what **proportion of the whole** belongs to the value or group.

It therefore answers a different question from absolute frequency: not only how many observations occur, but how large that count is relative to the dataset.

### Why Do We Need Relative Frequency?

Relative frequency serves two important purposes:

1. **Compare Proportions**: We can compare how large the proportion of one data group is compared to another group within the same dataset. For example, how large the proportion of Apple users is compared to Samsung users in one respondent group.
2. **Compare Between Different Groups**: We can compare the proportion of data from two groups with different total numbers. For example, comparing the percentage of students who like math in group A (total <InlineMath math="30 \text{ students}" />) with group B (total <InlineMath math="40 \text{ students}" />). It would be difficult with just regular frequency, but with relative frequency (percentage), the comparison becomes fair.

## Relative Frequency Formula

Divide the frequency of the group by the total frequency:

<BlockMath math="\text{Relative Frequency} = \frac{\text{Group Frequency}}{\text{Total Frequency}}" />

Where:

- **Group Frequency**: This is how many times the data or value in that group appears.
- **Total Frequency**: This is the total number of all data points we observed.

Relative Frequency is usually expressed in the form of:

- **Fraction**: The most basic form of division.
- **Decimal**: The result of the fraction division.
- **Percentage**: The decimal form multiplied by <InlineMath math="100\%" />. This is the most commonly used because it's easy to understand.

## Example of Calculating Relative Frequency

Consider the phone-brand data from the <InlineMath math="30 \text{ students}" /> above:

| Phone Brand | Frequency (Number of Students) |
| :---------- | :----------------------------- |
| Apple       | <InlineMath math="12" />       |
| Samsung     | <InlineMath math="10" />       |
| Xiaomi      | <InlineMath math="8" />        |
| **Total**   | <InlineMath math="30" />       |

Total frequency is <InlineMath math="12 + 10 + 8 = 30" /> students.

Calculate the relative frequency for each brand:

1. **Apple**:

   <BlockMath math="\text{Relative Frequency Apple} = \frac{12}{30}" />

   Relative Frequency Apple is <InlineMath math="0.4" />

   In percent: <InlineMath math="0.4 \times 100\% = 40\%" />

2. **Samsung**:

   <BlockMath math="\text{Relative Frequency Samsung} = \frac{10}{30}" />

   Relative Frequency Samsung is <InlineMath math="0.333\ldots" /> (we round it to <InlineMath math="0.33" />)

   In percent: <InlineMath math="0.33 \times 100\% = 33\%" />

3. **Xiaomi**:

   <BlockMath math="\text{Relative Frequency Xiaomi} = \frac{8}{30}" />

   Relative Frequency Xiaomi is <InlineMath math="0.266\ldots" /> (we round it to <InlineMath math="0.27" />)

   In percent: <InlineMath math="0.27 \times 100\% = 27\%" />

**Relative Frequency Table:**

| Phone Brand | Frequency                | Relative Frequency (Decimal) | Relative Frequency (Percent) |
| :---------- | :----------------------- | :--------------------------- | :--------------------------- |
| Apple       | <InlineMath math="12" /> | <InlineMath math="0.40" />   | <InlineMath math="40\%" />   |
| Samsung     | <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="0.33" />   | <InlineMath math="33\%" />   |
| Xiaomi      | <InlineMath math="8" />  | <InlineMath math="0.27" />   | <InlineMath math="27\%" />   |
| **Total**   | <InlineMath math="30" /> | <InlineMath math="1.00" />   | <InlineMath math="100\%" />  |

The sum of relative frequencies (in decimal form) should always be <InlineMath math="1" />, and in percent should be <InlineMath math="100\%" />. There might be slight differences due to rounding, but it should be very close to <InlineMath math="1" /> or <InlineMath math="100\%" />.

## Interpreting Relative Frequency

From the relative frequency table above, we can say:

- <InlineMath math="40\%" /> of the students in that group use Apple brand phones.
- About <InlineMath math="33\%" /> students use Samsung.
- The rest, about <InlineMath math="27\%" />, use Xiaomi.

The table shows at a glance how the <InlineMath math="30 \text{ students}" /> are distributed among the three brands.

Relative frequency makes absolute counts comparable by relating each count to the size of the complete dataset.
