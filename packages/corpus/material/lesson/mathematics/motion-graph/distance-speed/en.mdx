## The Gradient and Area of a Graph Tell the Story of a Motion

On a **distance-time graph**, the gradient is the **speed**. A horizontal line means the object is at rest, and a steeper line means it moves faster. On a **speed-time graph**, the gradient is the **acceleration**, and **the area under the graph is the distance** travelled. With these two ideas, you can read a whole journey from a single graph. In the models here, you follow a car's journey, find the area under a speed-time graph and compare the speed of each stage.

### Example Average Speed

Find **the average speed** of the car for the whole journey.

<BlockMath math="\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{120 + 120}{5} = 48\text{ km/h}" />

The average speed is lower than <InlineMath math="60\text{ km/h}" /> because the hour spent stopped is included in the total time. Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="s = 1" />, the distance is <InlineMath math="60\text{ km}" />, the same as <InlineMath math="60 \times 1" />.

### Example Distance and Acceleration

Find **the total distance**, **the acceleration** in the first <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds and **the deceleration** in the last <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Distance} = \tfrac{1}{2}(20 + 40) \times 20 = 600\text{ m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Acceleration} = \frac{20 - 0}{10} = 2\text{ m/s}^2" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Deceleration} = \frac{0 - 20}{10} = -2\text{ m/s}^2" />
</MathContainer>

The area under the graph is a trapezium with parallel sides <InlineMath math="20" /> and <InlineMath math="40" />, and height <InlineMath math="20" />. Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="s = 40" />, the distance is <InlineMath math="600" />, and at <InlineMath math="s = 10" />, the distance is <InlineMath math="100" />, the area of the first triangle.

## Common Mistakes about Graphs of Motion

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A horizontal line on a speed-time graph means the object is at rest. | A horizontal line on a speed-time graph means a steady speed. | Check the vertical axis of the graph. |
| The area under a distance-time graph is the distance. | Only the area under a speed-time graph gives distance. | The units of the area must be metres. |
| Average speed is the average of two speeds. | Average speed is total distance divided by total time. | Include time spent stopped in the total time. |
| A negative gradient on a speed-time graph means moving backwards. | A negative gradient means the speed is falling, a deceleration. | The speed is still positive above the time axis. |

The first mistake is the most common one in graph questions. **Read the label on the vertical axis first**.

## Practice on Graphs of Motion

These questions move from distance-time graphs to speed-time graphs. **Write the units** in every answer.

1. A bus travels <InlineMath math="90\text{ km}" /> in <InlineMath math="1.5" /> hours, stops for <InlineMath math="30" /> minutes, then travels another <InlineMath math="60\text{ km}" /> in <InlineMath math="1" /> hour. Find the average speed for the whole trip.

2. A motorcycle's speed-time graph is a straight line from <InlineMath math="(0, 4)" /> to <InlineMath math="(8, 20)" />, with speed in m/s and time in seconds. Find the acceleration and the distance in those <InlineMath math="8" /> seconds.

3. In the car example above, how far does the car travel in the first <InlineMath math="20" /> seconds?

4. Explain what a part of a distance-time graph that falls to the time axis means.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Total distance and total time, including the stop:

<BlockMath math="\frac{90 + 60}{1.5 + 0.5 + 1} = \frac{150}{3} = 50\text{ km/h}" />

Check: the average speed is lower than the speed of each moving stage, <InlineMath math="60\text{ km/h}" />, because the bus stopped for half an hour.

**Solution 2**: The acceleration is the gradient, and the distance is the area of a trapezium:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="a = \frac{20 - 4}{8} = 2\text{ m/s}^2" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Distance} = \tfrac{1}{2}(4 + 20) \times 8 = 96\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution 3**: The triangle for the first <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds plus the rectangle for the next <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds:

<BlockMath math="\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 20 + 10 \times 20 = 100 + 200 = 300\text{ m}" />

Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="s = 20" />, the distance is <InlineMath math="300" />.

**Solution 4**: A falling part means **the distance from the starting point is shrinking**, so the object is heading back to the start. When the graph touches the time axis, the object has arrived back at the starting point.
