## A Bus Trip Becomes a Graph

Write down how far something is from its starting point at every moment, then plot it with time across and distance up. That picture is a **distance-time graph**. One idea unlocks it: **the gradient of the line is the speed**. A steeper line means faster, a flat line means the object is standing still, and a line going down means it's heading back to the start. In the bus lab, you watch that graph draw itself while the bus drives.

Follow one town bus. It leaves the Terminal, stops at the Clinic, carries on to the Stadium, waits there, then drives straight back to the Terminal. Assume the bus keeps a **steady speed within each part** of the trip. A real bus speeds up and slows down gradually, but this assumption turns every part into one straight line.

| Part | Time (minutes) | What the bus does | Distance from the Terminal (km) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="OA" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="12" /> | drives to the Clinic | <InlineMath math="0" /> up to <InlineMath math="8" /> |
| <InlineMath math="AB" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> to <InlineMath math="18" /> | stops at the Clinic | stays at <InlineMath math="8" /> |
| <InlineMath math="BC" /> | <InlineMath math="18" /> to <InlineMath math="28" /> | drives to the Stadium | <InlineMath math="8" /> up to <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="CD" /> | <InlineMath math="28" /> to <InlineMath math="33" /> | waits at the Stadium | stays at <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="DE" /> | <InlineMath math="33" /> to <InlineMath math="48" /> | drives back to the Terminal | <InlineMath math="12" /> down to <InlineMath math="0" /> |

To draw the graph, **mark a point every time the bus changes what it's doing**, with time as the first coordinate and distance as the second. The points are <InlineMath math="O(0, 0)" />, <InlineMath math="A(12, 8)" />, <InlineMath math="B(18, 8)" />, <InlineMath math="C(28, 12)" />, <InlineMath math="D(33, 12)" />, and <InlineMath math="E(48, 0)" />. Join neighbouring points with straight lines, because the bus's speed doesn't change between them.

## Steeper Means Faster

On part <InlineMath math="OA" />, the bus's distance rises by <InlineMath math="8\text{ km}" /> in <InlineMath math="12" /> minutes. The rise divided by the time it takes is called the **gradient** of the line. On a distance-time graph, **the gradient is the speed**, because it tells you how many kilometres are added every minute.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{gradient of } OA = \frac{8 - 0}{12 - 0} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ km per minute}" />
<BlockMath math="\frac{2}{3}\text{ km per minute} \times 60 = 40\text{ km/h}" />
</MathContainer>

You multiply by <InlineMath math="60" /> because an hour has <InlineMath math="60" /> minutes. On part <InlineMath math="BC" />, the bus only rises <InlineMath math="4\text{ km}" /> in <InlineMath math="10" /> minutes, so the gradient gives <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4}{10} \times 60 = 24\text{ km/h}" />. Line <InlineMath math="BC" /> looks less steep than <InlineMath math="OA" />, and the bus really is slower there.

Two other line shapes often get misread: the flat line and the line that goes down.

### A Flat Stretch Is a Stop

From minute <InlineMath math="12" /> to minute <InlineMath math="18" />, the bus stays <InlineMath math="8\text{ km}" /> from the Terminal. The rise is zero, so the gradient of <InlineMath math="AB" /> is zero too:

<BlockMath math="\text{gradient of } AB = \frac{8 - 8}{18 - 12} = \frac{0}{6} = 0" />

**A speed of zero means the bus is standing still**, which fits a bus letting passengers off at the Clinic. The same thing happens on <InlineMath math="CD" /> at the Stadium.

Watch out for the other kind of graph. On a [graph of speed against time](/en/subjects/mathematics/graphs-of-motion/speed-time-graphs), a flat line means the object keeps moving at a steady speed. So before you interpret a flat line, **read the label on the vertical axis first**.

### Falling on the Way Back

On <InlineMath math="DE" />, the bus's distance from the Terminal drops from <InlineMath math="12\text{ km}" /> to <InlineMath math="0" /> in <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes. The gradient is negative:

<BlockMath math="\text{gradient of } DE = \frac{0 - 12}{48 - 33} = -\frac{12}{15} = -0.8\text{ km per minute}" />

The minus sign only shows direction: **the bus is moving towards the Terminal**. It doesn't mean the bus is slowing down. Speed has no direction, so the speed is the size of the gradient, <InlineMath math="0.8 \times 60 = 48\text{ km/h}" />. Line <InlineMath math="DE" /> is actually the steepest, so the bus is fastest on its way back.

## Average Speed of the Trip

The speed at one moment comes from the gradient of the part the bus is on. At minute <InlineMath math="20" />, the bus is on part <InlineMath math="BC" />, so its speed is <InlineMath math="24\text{ km/h}" />. For the whole trip, you want one number that sums it all up: total distance divided by total time. That number is the **average speed**:

<BlockMath math="\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}" />

The total distance counts every kilometre the bus covers, the trip back included. The total time counts every minute, the stops included.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{total distance} = 8 + 4 + 12 = 24\text{ km}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{average speed} = \frac{24\text{ km}}{48\text{ minutes}} = 0.5\text{ km per minute} = 30\text{ km/h}" />
</MathContainer>

Check it backwards: <InlineMath math="48" /> minutes is <InlineMath math="0.8" /> of an hour, and <InlineMath math="30 \times 0.8 = 24\text{ km}" />. Pick All in the speed lab and the same number appears there. Notice too that the bus ends up <InlineMath math="0\text{ km}" /> from the Terminal, yet the distance it travelled is still <InlineMath math="24\text{ km}" />.

A common slip is to average the five speeds of the parts:

<BlockMath math="\frac{40 + 0 + 24 + 0 + 48}{5} = 22.4\text{ km/h}" />

That answer is wrong because the five parts **don't last equally long**. Part <InlineMath math="DE" /> lasts <InlineMath math="15" /> minutes and <InlineMath math="CD" /> only <InlineMath math="5" />, yet this method gives them the same weight. Always go back to total distance over total time.

## When and Where They Meet

At minute <InlineMath math="18" />, just as the bus leaves the Clinic, a motorcyclist sets off from the Stadium towards the Terminal at a steady <InlineMath math="36\text{ km/h}" />. When and where does the motorcycle pass the bus? On the graph, **the point where the two lines cross** answers both at once, because there the two are in the same place at the same time.

Change the speeds to kilometres per minute first. On part <InlineMath math="BC" />, the bus covers <InlineMath math="24 \div 60 = 0.4" /> km per minute, and the motorcycle covers <InlineMath math="36 \div 60 = 0.6" /> km per minute. Say they meet <InlineMath math="t" /> minutes after minute <InlineMath math="18" />. The bus is then <InlineMath math="8 + 0.4t" /> km from the Terminal and the motorcycle is <InlineMath math="12 - 0.6t" /> km away. Set the two equal:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 8 + 0.4t &= 12 - 0.6t \\ t &= 4 \end{aligned}" />

So they meet at **minute <InlineMath math="22" />**. Their distances from the Terminal at that moment are:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{bus: } 8 + 0.4 \times 4 = 9.6\text{ km}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{motorcycle: } 12 - 0.6 \times 4 = 9.6\text{ km}" />
</MathContainer>

Both distances match, so the answer checks out. Minute <InlineMath math="22" /> is still inside part <InlineMath math="BC" />, which ends at minute <InlineMath math="28" />, so the bus equation you used really applies. There's a quicker way too: the gap between them is <InlineMath math="4\text{ km}" /> and shrinks by <InlineMath math="0.4 + 0.6 = 1" /> km every minute, so it closes in <InlineMath math="4" /> minutes.

Try these two problems on the same bus and motorcycle graph, then compare your answers.

1. At minute <InlineMath math="30" />, how far apart are the bus and the motorcycle?
2. A van leaves the Terminal at minute <InlineMath math="3" /> at a steady <InlineMath math="48\text{ km/h}" />, heading for the Stadium. When and where does the van catch up with the bus?

**Problem 1**. At minute <InlineMath math="30" />, the bus is waiting at the Stadium, <InlineMath math="12\text{ km}" /> from the Terminal. The motorcycle has been riding for <InlineMath math="12" /> minutes, so it's <InlineMath math="12 - 0.6 \times 12 = 4.8\text{ km}" /> from the Terminal. They're <InlineMath math="12 - 4.8 = 7.2\text{ km}" /> apart.

**Problem 2**. The van covers <InlineMath math="48 \div 60 = 0.8" /> km per minute. Try part <InlineMath math="OA" /> first, where the bus is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{3}t" /> km from the Terminal:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \tfrac{2}{3}t &= 0.8(t - 3) \\ t &= 18 \end{aligned}" />

Minute <InlineMath math="18" /> lies outside <InlineMath math="OA" />, which ends at minute <InlineMath math="12" />, so **the van doesn't catch the bus on its way to the Clinic**. At minute <InlineMath math="12" />, the van is only at <InlineMath math="0.8 \times 9 = 7.2\text{ km}" />, behind the bus. The bus then stays at <InlineMath math="8\text{ km}" /> until minute <InlineMath math="18" />. The van reaches <InlineMath math="8\text{ km}" /> at minute <InlineMath math="3 + 8 \div 0.8 = 13" />. So the van catches the bus at minute <InlineMath math="13" />, at the Clinic, while the bus is still stopped. On the graph, the van's line cuts the flat line <InlineMath math="AB" />.
