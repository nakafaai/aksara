## From a Trip to a Speed Graph

If you plot how fast something moves at every moment, you get a **speed-time graph**. This graph is for an object moving in one direction only, so its line never dips below the time axis.

> On a speed-time graph, the area under the line is the distance travelled and the gradient of the line is the acceleration.

Take a car waiting at a red light. When the light turns green, the car speeds up steadily from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="8" /> seconds. It then holds <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" /> for <InlineMath math="20" /> seconds, turns onto a main road, and speeds up again to <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds. At the next red light, it brakes steadily to a stop in <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds.

**Steadily** means the speed changes by the same amount every second, so each part of the story becomes one straight line. Put time on the horizontal axis and speed on the vertical axis. Mark the speed at each moment the motion changes: <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />, <InlineMath math="(8, 12)" />, <InlineMath math="(28, 12)" />, <InlineMath math="(38, 20)" />, and <InlineMath math="(48, 0)" />. The time <InlineMath math="28" /> comes from <InlineMath math="8 + 20" />, and <InlineMath math="38" /> from <InlineMath math="28 + 10" />. Join the points with straight lines.

To get a feel for these speeds, <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" /> equals <InlineMath math="43.2\text{ km/h}" />, and <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> equals <InlineMath math="72\text{ km/h}" />. ## Area under the Speed Line

Now the same car drives in the lab. As it moves, the region under its speed line fills with colour, and each part gets a number in metres.

Why should an area equal a distance? Start with the flat part, where the region is just a rectangle, then move on to the sloping parts.

### When the Speed Holds

From second <InlineMath math="8" /> to second <InlineMath math="28" />, the car holds <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" />. Every second it moves <InlineMath math="12" /> metres, so in <InlineMath math="20" /> seconds it moves:

<BlockMath math="\text{distance} = \text{speed} \times \text{time} = 12\text{ m/s} \times 20\text{ s} = 240\text{ m}" />

On the graph, the region under that flat line is a **rectangle** <InlineMath math="20" /> wide and <InlineMath math="12" /> tall, so its area is <InlineMath math="240" /> as well.

Look at the units: metres per second times seconds gives metres. **The unit of this area is a unit of distance**, whereas the area under a distance-time graph gives metres times seconds instead of distance.

### When the Speed Changes

When the speed changes steadily, the average speed over that part is halfway between the starting speed <InlineMath math="u" /> and the final speed <InlineMath math="v" />. The distance is that average speed times the time <InlineMath math="t" />, and the formula is exactly **the area of a trapezium**:

<BlockMath math="\text{distance} = \frac{u + v}{2} \times t" />

If one end is zero, the trapezium becomes a triangle. The car's other three parts work out like this:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{first part (triangle)} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 12 = 48\text{ m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{third part (trapezium)} = \tfrac{1}{2}(12 + 20) \times 10 = 160\text{ m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{fourth part (triangle)} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 20 = 100\text{ m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{total distance} = 48 + 240 + 160 + 100 = 548\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

Check the first part another way: its average speed is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{0 + 12}{2} = 6\text{ m/s}" />, and <InlineMath math="6 \times 8 = 48\text{ m}" />. The total of <InlineMath math="548\text{ m}" /> also matches the distance travelled in the lab. When the speed line is curved, its area comes from integration, as in [the kinematics of linear motion](/en/subjects/mathematics/kinematics-of-linear-motion/displacement-velocity-and-acceleration).

## How Fast the Speed Changes

How quickly does the speed change? Divide the change in speed by the time it takes. That quotient is called the **acceleration**, and on a speed-time graph it equals the gradient of the line:

<BlockMath math="a = \frac{v - u}{t}" />

Here <InlineMath math="u" /> is the starting speed, <InlineMath math="v" /> the final speed, and <InlineMath math="t" /> the time taken. On the car's first part:

<BlockMath math="a = \frac{12 - 0}{8} = 1.5\text{ m/s}^2" />

### Rising or Falling Speed

A rising line means the speed is increasing, and the acceleration is positive. A flat line means **the speed is steady and the acceleration is zero**. From second <InlineMath math="8" /> to second <InlineMath math="28" />, the car keeps moving <InlineMath math="12" /> metres every second. That's different from a flat line on a [graph of distance against time](/en/subjects/mathematics/graphs-of-motion/distance-time-graphs), which means the object is standing still.

A falling line means the speed is decreasing, and the acceleration is negative. Speed that keeps dropping like this is called **deceleration**. When the car brakes:

<BlockMath math="a = \frac{0 - 20}{10} = -2\text{ m/s}^2" />

Its deceleration is <InlineMath math="2\text{ m/s}^2" />. A falling line doesn't mean the car is reversing. The car keeps going forward until the line touches the time axis, and that's where it stops.

With a steady deceleration of <InlineMath math="6\text{ m/s}^2" />, the speed falls from <InlineMath math="12\text{ m/s}" /> to zero in <InlineMath math="2" /> seconds. The triangle under that falling line has area <InlineMath math="12\text{ m}" />, matching the stopping distance shown in the lab.

This table compares what the same line shape means on the two graphs. Most mistakes come from carrying the meaning from one graph over to the other.

| Line shape | On a distance-time graph | On a speed-time graph |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Rising | moving away from the start | speed increasing |
| Flat | standing still | steady speed |
| Falling | heading back to the start | speed decreasing |

### Units of Acceleration

You read <InlineMath math="\text{m/s}^2" /> as metres per second per second. An acceleration of <InlineMath math="4\text{ m/s}^2" /> means **the speed goes up by <InlineMath math="4\text{ m/s}" /> every second**. The train starting from rest has speeds of <InlineMath math="0, 4, 8, 12, 16" />, then <InlineMath math="20\text{ m/s}" /> at seconds <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />.

The units have to match before you divide. Say a car goes from rest to <InlineMath math="90\text{ km/h}" /> in <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds. Working out <InlineMath math="90 \div 10 = 9" /> mixes hours with seconds, so change the speed to m/s first:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="90\text{ km/h} = \frac{90 \times 1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = 25\text{ m/s}" />
<BlockMath math="a = \frac{25 - 0}{10} = 2.5\text{ m/s}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check: in <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds the speed rises by <InlineMath math="2.5 \times 10 = 25\text{ m/s}" />, which is <InlineMath math="90\text{ km/h}" />.

## From Lifts to Toll Plazas

These three problems get harder as they go. For each one, **sketch the speed-time graph first**, then work out its area or gradient.

1. A lift goes up from the ground floor. It speeds up steadily from rest to <InlineMath math="3\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="2" /> seconds, rises at a steady speed for <InlineMath math="6" /> seconds, then slows steadily to a stop in <InlineMath math="2" /> seconds. How many metres does it rise? If each floor is <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> tall, how many floors does it pass?
2. In a <InlineMath math="100\text{ m}" /> race, a runner speeds up steadily from rest for <InlineMath math="4" /> seconds to a speed <InlineMath math="v" />, then runs at a steady <InlineMath math="v" /> to the finish line. The race takes <InlineMath math="12" /> seconds. Find <InlineMath math="v" /> and the runner's acceleration in the first <InlineMath math="4" /> seconds.
3. A car is travelling at <InlineMath math="25\text{ m/s}" /> on a highway. Approaching a toll plaza, it slows steadily to <InlineMath math="5\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds, passes the gate at a steady <InlineMath math="5\text{ m/s}" /> for <InlineMath math="4" /> seconds, then speeds up steadily back to <InlineMath math="25\text{ m/s}" /> in <InlineMath math="16" /> seconds. How far does it travel in those <InlineMath math="30" /> seconds, and how many seconds does it lose compared with keeping up <InlineMath math="25\text{ m/s}" />?

**Problem 1**. The graph rises, stays flat, then falls, and the region under it is a trapezium. Its parallel sides are <InlineMath math="10" /> seconds along the bottom and <InlineMath math="6" /> seconds along the top, and its height is <InlineMath math="3" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{distance} = \tfrac{1}{2}(10 + 6) \times 3 = 24\text{ m}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{check by parts: } 3 + 18 + 3 = 24\text{ m}" />
</MathContainer>

The lift rises <InlineMath math="24\text{ m}" />, which is <InlineMath math="8" /> floors.

**Problem 2**. The final speed is unknown, so **write the area in terms of <InlineMath math="v" />**. The region is a triangle over <InlineMath math="4" /> seconds plus a rectangle over <InlineMath math="12 - 4 = 8" /> seconds, and its total area must be <InlineMath math="100" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times v + 8v = 100" />
<BlockMath math="10v = 100 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = 10\text{ m/s}" />
<BlockMath math="a = \frac{10 - 0}{4} = 2.5\text{ m/s}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check by substituting <InlineMath math="v = 10\text{ m/s}" /> into the triangle and rectangle areas:

<BlockMath math="\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 10 + 8 \times 10 = 20 + 80 = 100\text{ m}" />
