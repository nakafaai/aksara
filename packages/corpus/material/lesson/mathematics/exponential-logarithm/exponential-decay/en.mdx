## Definition of Exponential Decay

Exponential decay occurs when a positive quantity is multiplied by the same factor between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> during every equal interval. A discrete decay model has the form:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=C\,a^x" />

The quantities in this model are:

- <InlineMath math="C>0" />, the initial value
- <InlineMath math="0<a<1" />, the decay factor per interval
- <InlineMath math="x" />, the number of intervals

The factor describes the part that remains. For example, a <InlineMath math="15\%" /> decrease leaves <InlineMath math="85\%" />, so the decay factor is <InlineMath math="a=0.85" />.

## Reading an Exponential Decay Graph

As <InlineMath math="x" /> increases, <InlineMath math="a^x" /> becomes smaller. The graph falls quickly at first and then flattens because each decrease is the same proportion of a progressively smaller amount.

For <InlineMath math="f(x)=50\left(\frac12\right)^x" />, the initial value is <InlineMath math="50" /> and every interval leaves half of the previous value:

## Decay Models for Medicine Radioactivity and Bounces

### A Half Life Model

Suppose a hypothetical medicine starts at <InlineMath math="50\,\mu\text{g}" /> in the body and half of the remaining amount is eliminated every hour. If <InlineMath math="x" /> is measured in hours, then:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=50\left(\frac12\right)^x" />

The first values show that the model always halves the current amount:

- <InlineMath math="f(0)=50\,\mu\text{g}" />
- <InlineMath math="f(1)=25\,\mu\text{g}" />
- <InlineMath math="f(2)=12.5\,\mu\text{g}" />
- <InlineMath math="f(3)=6.25\,\mu\text{g}" />

After <InlineMath math="9" /> hours, <InlineMath math="f(9)=50\left(\frac12\right)^9\approx0.0977\,\mu\text{g}" />, which is below <InlineMath math="0.1\,\mu\text{g}" />.

### Radioactive Decay

If a sample loses the same fraction <InlineMath math="r" /> during each time interval, then the fraction remaining is <InlineMath math="1-r" />. For <InlineMath math="0<r<1" />:

<BlockMath math="M(t)=M_0(1-r)^t" />

Here, <InlineMath math="M_0" /> is the initial mass and <InlineMath math="t" /> counts equal time intervals. This model assumes the stated proportional rate remains constant over the interval being studied.

### Bounce Height

Suppose a ball is dropped from height <InlineMath math="h_0" /> and each rebound reaches the fixed proportion <InlineMath math="0<p<1" /> of the previous height. The height after rebound <InlineMath math="n" /> is:

<BlockMath math="h_n=h_0p^n" />

For <InlineMath math="h_0=300\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="p=\frac35" />:

- <InlineMath math="h_0=300\text{ cm}" />
- <InlineMath math="h_1=300\left(\frac35\right)=180\text{ cm}" />
- <InlineMath math="h_2=300\left(\frac35\right)^2=108\text{ cm}" />
- <InlineMath math="h_3=300\left(\frac35\right)^3=64.8\text{ cm}" />

The mathematical model never reaches zero. A practical stopping statement therefore needs a threshold, such as “below <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" />.”

## Exercises

1. A hypothetical substance starts at <InlineMath math="200\text{ mg}" /> and half of the remaining amount is eliminated every hour. How much remains after <InlineMath math="5" /> hours?

2. A radioactive sample has mass <InlineMath math="0.3\text{ kg}" /> at the start of an observation. It loses <InlineMath math="15\%" /> of its mass each hour. How much remains after <InlineMath math="8" /> hours?

3. A ball is dropped from <InlineMath math="3\text{ m}" /> and each rebound reaches <InlineMath math="\frac35" /> of the previous height.

   1. Draw the discrete rebound-height graph.
   2. If the ball is treated as stopped once its rebound is below <InlineMath math="1\text{ cm}" />, find the first rebound that meets the threshold.

### Worked Solutions

1. The decay factor is <InlineMath math="\frac12" />. After five hourly intervals:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="f(x)=200\left(\frac12\right)^x" />
     <BlockMath math="f(5)=200\left(\frac12\right)^5=200\left(\frac1{32}\right)=6.25" />
   </MathContainer>

   Therefore, <InlineMath math="6.25\text{ mg}" /> remains.

2. Losing <InlineMath math="15\%" /> leaves the factor <InlineMath math="0.85" />:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="M(t)=0.3(0.85)^t" />
     <BlockMath math="M(8)=0.3(0.85)^8\approx0.08175\text{ kg}" />
     <BlockMath math="0.08175\text{ kg}\approx81.75\text{ g}" />
   </MathContainer>

3. With height measured in centimetres, the model is:

## Model Boundaries

Exponential decay is used for phenomena such as radioactive decay, depreciation, cooling approximations, and elimination models. In every application, identify the interval, the factor that remains, and the conditions under which that factor can be treated as constant. The model works while those conditions hold. If the conditions change, the pattern may also change.
