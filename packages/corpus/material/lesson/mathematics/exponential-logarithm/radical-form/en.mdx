## Relationship Between Exponents and Radicals

**Fractional exponents and roots** describe the same operation. The decay model below uses a half-hour input to show why an exponent of one half is a square root.

Suppose <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> is the amount of a medicine remaining in the body <InlineMath math="x" /> hours after a dose. The initial amount is <InlineMath math="50" /> units, and the amount halves every hour:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=50(0.5)^x" />

Thirty minutes is half an hour, so the correct input is:

<BlockMath math="x=\frac{30}{60}=\frac12" />

The amount remaining after thirty minutes is therefore:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f\left(\frac12\right) &amp;=50(0.5)^{\frac12} \\ &amp;=50\sqrt{0.5} \\ &amp;\approx35.36 \end{aligned}" />

The equality <InlineMath math="(0.5)^{\frac12}=\sqrt{0.5}" /> connects exponent notation with radical notation. The model measures <InlineMath math="x" /> in hours, so <InlineMath math="x=30" /> means thirty hours.

## Definition of Radical Form

For <InlineMath math="a>0" />, <InlineMath math="m\in\mathbb{Z}" />, and an integer <InlineMath math="n\geq1" />, the real-valued definition is:

<BlockMath math="a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m=\sqrt[n]{a^m}" />

This equality converts a **fractional exponent** to a root, or a root to a fractional exponent, without changing the value. If <InlineMath math="n" /> is odd, negative real bases can also have real <InlineMath math="n" />th roots. If <InlineMath math="n" /> is even, a negative radicand has no real root.

A radical can be a length you can draw. The diagonal of a unit square has length <InlineMath math="\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}" />. Swing it down with a compass arc and it lands on the number line at <InlineMath math="\sqrt 2 = 2^{1/2}" />, a little past <InlineMath math="1.4" />.

## Simplifying Radical Forms

Simplify <InlineMath math="(2\sqrt{x})(3\sqrt[3]{x})" /> for <InlineMath math="x>0" />.

Write both roots as powers. **Multiply the coefficients** and **add the exponents** of factors with the same base:

<BlockMath
  math="\begin{align}
(2\sqrt{x})(3\sqrt[3]{x}) &= (2x^{\frac{1}{2}})(3x^{\frac{1}{3}}) \\
&= 2 \cdot 3 \cdot x^{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}} \\
&= 6x^{\frac{3+2}{6}} \\
&= 6x^{\frac{5}{6}} \\
&= 6\sqrt[6]{x^5}
\end{align}"
/>

So the equivalent simplified forms are <InlineMath math="6x^{\frac56}" /> and <InlineMath math="6\sqrt[6]{x^5}" />.

Simplifying <InlineMath math="\sqrt 8 = 2\sqrt 2" /> has a picture too. The diagonal of a <InlineMath math="2 \times 2" /> square is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt 8" />, and it is exactly twice the diagonal <InlineMath math="\sqrt 2" /> of the unit square inside it.

## A Common Invalid Shortcut

A root **does not distribute over addition**. In general:

<BlockMath math="\sqrt{a+b}\neq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}" />

One counterexample is enough to disprove the proposed identity. Take <InlineMath math="a=4" /> and <InlineMath math="b=9" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \sqrt{4+9} &=\sqrt{13} \\ \sqrt4+\sqrt9 &=2+3=5 \\ \sqrt{13} &\neq5 \end{aligned}" />

Choose perfect squares so that both expressions produce exact integer results:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5" />
  <BlockMath math="\sqrt{16}+\sqrt9=4+3=7" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="5\neq7" />, the two expressions cannot be interchangeable. Keep the sum inside the radical unless another valid algebraic step changes it.

This right triangle shows why <InlineMath math="\sqrt{a + b} \ne \sqrt a + \sqrt b" />. Its legs are <InlineMath math="\sqrt 9 = 3" /> and <InlineMath math="\sqrt{16} = 4" />, and its hypotenuse is <InlineMath math="\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5" />. The shortcut would claim <InlineMath math="3 + 4 = 7" />, but walking along the two legs is always longer than the straight hypotenuse.
