## Eight Rules from Exponent Laws

Each logarithm property follows from a corresponding exponent law, so every formula below can be derived from that law.

All the properties below use these conditions:

<BlockMath math="a>0\qquad a\neq1\qquad b>0\qquad c>0\qquad n\in\mathbb{R}" />

Whenever <InlineMath math="m" /> or <InlineMath math="b" /> is used as a logarithm base, that base must also be positive and not equal to one.

| Rule | Identity |
| ---- | -------- |
| Logarithm of the base | <InlineMath math="\log_a a=1" /> |
| Logarithm of one | <InlineMath math="\log_a 1=0" /> |
| Inverse relationship | <InlineMath math="\log_a(a^n)=n" /> |
| Product | <InlineMath math="\log_a(bc)=\log_a b+\log_a c" /> |
| Quotient | <InlineMath math="\log_a\left(\frac bc\right)=\log_a b-\log_a c" /> |
| Power | <InlineMath math="\log_a(b^n)=n\log_a b" /> |
| Change of base | <InlineMath math="\log_a b=\frac{\log_m b}{\log_m a}=\frac1{\log_b a}" /> |
| Chain | <InlineMath math="(\log_a b)(\log_b c)=\log_a c" /> |

The first three rules come directly from <InlineMath math="a^1=a" />, <InlineMath math="a^0=1" />, and the inverse relationship between logarithms and exponents. The next sections explain the rules that combine several values.

## Why the Rules Work

### Product Rule

Let:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x=\log_a b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b=a^x" />
  <BlockMath math="y=\log_a c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c=a^y" />
</MathContainer>

Multiplying <InlineMath math="b" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> adds their exponents:

<BlockMath math="bc=a^x a^y=a^{x+y}" />

Taking the base-<InlineMath math="a" /> logarithm gives:

<BlockMath math="\log_a(bc)=x+y=\log_a b+\log_a c" />

### Quotient Rule

Using the same substitutions, division subtracts the exponents:

<BlockMath math="\frac bc=\frac{a^x}{a^y}=a^{x-y}" />

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\log_a\left(\frac bc\right)=x-y=\log_a b-\log_a c" />

### Power Rule

Let <InlineMath math="y=\log_a b" />, so <InlineMath math="b=a^y" />. For any real <InlineMath math="n" />, the condition <InlineMath math="b>0" /> makes <InlineMath math="b^n" /> well-defined:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="b^n=(a^y)^n=a^{ny}" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_a(b^n)=ny=n\log_a b" />
</MathContainer>

This proof covers real exponents directly. Repeated multiplication would explain only positive integer exponents.

### Change of Base and Reciprocal Rule

Let <InlineMath math="x=\log_a b" />, so <InlineMath math="b=a^x" />. Take the logarithm of both sides using any valid base <InlineMath math="m" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_m b=\log_m(a^x)" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_m b=x\log_m a" />
  <BlockMath math="x=\frac{\log_m b}{\log_m a}" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="x=\log_a b" />, this proves the change-of-base formula. Choosing <InlineMath math="m=b" /> gives the reciprocal relationship:

<BlockMath math="\log_a b=\frac{\log_b b}{\log_b a}=\frac1{\log_b a}" />

### Chain Rule

After both logarithms are written with the same valid base, <InlineMath math="\log_m b" /> appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator, so the two factors cancel:

<BlockMath math="(\log_a b)(\log_b c)=\frac{\log_m b}{\log_m a}\cdot\frac{\log_m c}{\log_m b}=\frac{\log_m c}{\log_m a}=\log_a c" />

## Evaluating a Logarithm from Exponential Form

Calculate <InlineMath math="\log_5 125" />.

Because <InlineMath math="125=5^3" />, the power and inverse rules give:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\log_5 125=\log_5(5^3)" />
  <BlockMath math="\log_5(5^3)=3\log_5 5=3" />
</MathContainer>

The result also answers the original exponent question: <InlineMath math="5^3=125" />.

## Exercises

1. Simplify each expression.

    1. <InlineMath math="\log_9 81" />

    2. <InlineMath math="\log_2 64-\log_2 16" />

    3. <InlineMath math="\log_4(16^{10})" />

2. If <InlineMath math="\log_5 4=m" /> and <InlineMath math="\log_4 3=n" />, express <InlineMath math="\log_{12}100" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="m" /> and <InlineMath math="n" />.

3. A city has <InlineMath math="300{,}000" /> residents and is modeled as growing by <InlineMath math="6\%" /> at the end of each year. Under this fixed-rate model, after how many complete years will its population first reach at least <InlineMath math="1{,}000{,}000" />?

4. Dini deposits <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}\,2{,}000{,}000" /> in an account paying <InlineMath math="12\%" /> interest compounded annually. Assume no further deposits, withdrawals, fees, or rate changes. After how many complete years will the balance first reach at least <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}\,6{,}500{,}000" />?

### Worked Solutions

1. Simplify each expression.

   1. Since <InlineMath math="81=9^2" />:

      <MathContainer>
        <BlockMath math="\log_9 81=\log_9(9^2)" />
        <BlockMath math="\log_9(9^2)=2" />
      </MathContainer>

   2. Use the quotient rule:

      <MathContainer>
        <BlockMath math="\log_2 64-\log_2 16=\log_2\left(\frac{64}{16}\right)" />
        <BlockMath math="\log_2 4=2" />
      </MathContainer>

   3. Since <InlineMath math="16=4^2" />:

      <MathContainer>
        <BlockMath math="\log_4(16^{10})=\log_4((4^2)^{10})" />
        <BlockMath math="\log_4(4^{20})=20" />
      </MathContainer>

2. Use base <InlineMath math="4" /> for the numerator and denominator:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="\log_{12}100=\frac{\log_4 100}{\log_4 12}" />
     <BlockMath math="\log_4 100=\log_4(4\cdot25)=1+2\log_4 5" />
     <BlockMath math="\log_4 12=\log_4(4\cdot3)=1+n" />
   </MathContainer>

   The reciprocal rule gives <InlineMath math="\log_4 5=\frac1{\log_5 4}=\frac1m" />. Therefore:

   <BlockMath math="\log_{12}100=\frac{1+\frac2m}{1+n}" />

3. The fixed-rate model is:

   <BlockMath math="P(x)=300{,}000(1.06)^x" />

   Solve the continuous equation first:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="1{,}000{,}000=300{,}000(1.06)^x" />
     <BlockMath math="x=\frac{\ln(10/3)}{\ln(1.06)}\approx20.662354" />
   </MathContainer>

   The model updates after complete years, so check the neighboring integers:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="P(20)\approx962{,}141<1{,}000{,}000" />
     <BlockMath math="P(21)\approx1{,}019{,}869>1{,}000{,}000" />
   </MathContainer>

   The population first reaches the target after <InlineMath math="21\text{ complete years}" />. This result assumes that the stated growth rate stays fixed for all <InlineMath math="21" /> years. A changing growth rate would give a different answer.

4. The annual-compounding model is:

   <BlockMath math="B(x)=2{,}000{,}000(1.12)^x" />

   Solve the continuous equation:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="6{,}500{,}000=2{,}000{,}000(1.12)^x" />
     <BlockMath math="x=\frac{\ln(3.25)}{\ln(1.12)}\approx10.400324" />
   </MathContainer>

   Interest is credited only after each complete year, so compare years <InlineMath math="10" /> and <InlineMath math="11" />:

   <MathContainer>
     <BlockMath math="B(10)\approx6{,}211{,}696<6{,}500{,}000" />
     <BlockMath math="B(11)\approx6{,}957{,}100>6{,}500{,}000" />
   </MathContainer>

   The balance first reaches the target after <InlineMath math="11\text{ complete years}" />.
