## Definition of Exponential Growth

Exponential growth occurs when a positive quantity is multiplied by the same factor greater than <InlineMath math="1" /> during each equal interval. A growth model has the form:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=P_0a^x" />

where:

- <InlineMath math="P_0>0" /> is the initial value
- <InlineMath math="a>1" /> is the growth factor per interval
- <InlineMath math="x" /> is the number of intervals

For discrete events such as completed cell divisions, <InlineMath math="x" /> is a nonnegative integer. A continuous graph extends the same formula to real values of <InlineMath math="x" /> and should be understood as an interpolation of the model.

## Building a Model from a Table

### Idealized Bacterial Growth

Suppose a culture starts with <InlineMath math="30" /> bacteria and every bacterium divides into two every <InlineMath math="30\text{ minutes}" />. The model ignores deaths and resource limits so that we can focus on the repeated factor.

Let <InlineMath math="x" /> count completed thirty-minute intervals:

| Interval | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| -------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Bacteria | <InlineMath math="30" /> | <InlineMath math="60" /> | <InlineMath math="120" /> | <InlineMath math="240" /> | <InlineMath math="480" /> | <InlineMath math="960" /> |

Each value is the initial count multiplied by a power of <InlineMath math="2" />:

- <InlineMath math="f(0)=30\cdot2^0=30" />
- <InlineMath math="f(1)=30\cdot2^1=60" />
- <InlineMath math="f(2)=30\cdot2^2=120" />
- <InlineMath math="f(3)=30\cdot2^3=240" />
- <InlineMath math="f(4)=30\cdot2^4=480" />

Therefore, the model is:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=30\cdot2^x" />

The table contains discrete observations. The curve of <InlineMath math="f(x)=30\cdot2^x" /> shows how the same rule behaves between those integer inputs:

### Converting Time to Intervals

Five hours contain ten thirty-minute intervals:

<BlockMath math="x=\frac{5\cdot60}{30}=10" />

Now substitute the interval count into the model:

<BlockMath math="f(10)=30\cdot2^{10}=30\cdot1{,}024=30{,}720" />

Under the idealized model, the culture contains <InlineMath math="30{,}720" /> bacteria after <InlineMath math="5\text{ hours}" />.

## Changing or Recovering the Initial Value

For a fixed growth factor <InlineMath math="a" />, changing <InlineMath math="P_0" /> scales every output:

<BlockMath math="f(x)=P_0a^x" />

For example, with a doubling factor:

- <InlineMath math="P_0=50" /> gives <InlineMath math="f(x)=50\cdot2^x" />
- <InlineMath math="P_0=100" /> gives <InlineMath math="f(x)=100\cdot2^x" />
- <InlineMath math="P_0=200" /> gives <InlineMath math="f(x)=200\cdot2^x" />

### Recovering an Unknown Initial Value

Suppose a culture contains <InlineMath math="8{,}000" /> bacteria after <InlineMath math="2" /> doubling intervals. Let <InlineMath math="P_0" /> be the unknown initial count:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="8{,}000=P_0\cdot2^2" />
  <BlockMath math="8{,}000=4P_0" />
  <BlockMath math="P_0=\frac{8{,}000}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="P_0=2{,}000" />
</MathContainer>

The culture started with <InlineMath math="2{,}000" /> bacteria.

### Extending the Same Interval Model

After <InlineMath math="10" /> doubling intervals, the same culture has:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="f(10)=2{,}000\cdot2^{10}" />
  <BlockMath math="f(10)=2{,}000\cdot1{,}024" />
  <BlockMath math="f(10)=2{,}048{,}000" />
</MathContainer>

The time represented by those ten intervals depends on the interval length. If one interval is thirty minutes, ten intervals equal five hours.

## Model Boundaries

Exponential growth appears in compound interest, idealized population models, early transmission models, and unconstrained laboratory growth. Always identify the initial value, factor, and interval. Real systems usually introduce changing rates, limited resources, or other constraints, so an exponential rule should only be extended as far as its assumptions remain reasonable.
