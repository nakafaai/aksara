## From Paper Folding to Growth Models

Suppose a sheet of paper starts at about <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mm}" /> thick. In an ideal model, every fold doubles its thickness. After <InlineMath math="42" /> folds, the model gives:

<BlockMath math="0.1\text{ mm}\times 2^{42}\approx 439{,}805\text{ km}" />

That is farther than the average distance from Earth to the Moon. A real sheet cannot be folded that many times, but the model exposes the central pattern: the same factor is applied again and again. This is **exponential growth**.

The same pattern can approximate transmission during a short, early phase of an outbreak. If each case produced exactly two new cases under unchanged conditions, the sequence would be <InlineMath math="1,2,4,8,\ldots" />. Real outbreaks do not keep a constant factor, so this is a teaching model, not a literal prediction.

## Definition of Exponents

An exponent is a compact way to write repeated multiplication. In the doubling model, the first few stages are:

<BlockMath math="1 = 2^0 \quad 2 = 2^1 \quad 4 = 2 \times 2 = 2^2 \quad 8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^3 \quad 16 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^4" />

At stage <InlineMath math="n" />, the model can be written as <InlineMath math="m(n)=2^n" />. For example:

<InlineMath math="m(5)=2^5=32" />.

## Meaning of Exponent Notation

An expression such as <InlineMath math="a^n" /> has two parts:

<BlockMath math="a^n" />

- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the **base**, the number being multiplied
- <InlineMath math="n" /> is the **exponent**, the number of equal factors

If <InlineMath math="a" /> is real and <InlineMath math="n" /> is a positive integer, then:

<BlockMath math="a^n = \underbrace{a \times a \times a \times \ldots \times a}_{n \text{ factors}}" />

## Important Exponent Definitions

### Zero Exponent

For every nonzero real number <InlineMath math="a" />:

<BlockMath math="a^0=1" />

This is not an arbitrary rule. Dividing a nonzero power by itself gives both <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="a^{n-n}=a^0" />, so the two expressions must agree.

<BlockMath math="\frac{a^n}{a^n}=a^{n-n}=a^0=1" />

### Negative Exponents

For every nonzero real number <InlineMath math="a" /> and positive integer <InlineMath math="n" />:

<BlockMath math="a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}=\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^n" />

The reason is that <InlineMath math="a^n a^{-n}=a^0=1" />. A negative exponent therefore represents the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. For example:

<InlineMath math="3^{-2}=\frac{1}{3^2}=\frac{1}{9}=0.111\ldots" />.

### Fractional Exponents

For real-valued roots in this lesson, let <InlineMath math="a>0" /> and let <InlineMath math="n" /> be a positive integer. Then:

<BlockMath math="a^{\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a}=p" />

Here, <InlineMath math="p" /> is the unique positive real number satisfying <InlineMath math="p^n=a" />. For example, <InlineMath math="16^{\frac14}=2" /> because <InlineMath math="2^4=16" />.

Odd roots can also accept negative real inputs, such as <InlineMath math="(-8)^{\frac13}=-2" />. Even roots of negative numbers are not real. Stating the domain matters because the notation does not have the same real-valued meaning for every base.

### General Fractional Exponents

If <InlineMath math="a>0" /> and <InlineMath math="m,n" /> are positive integers, then:

<BlockMath math="a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(a^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^m=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m" />

First take the <InlineMath math="n" />th root, then raise the result to the <InlineMath math="m" />th power. For example:

<InlineMath math="8^{\frac23}=\left(8^{\frac13}\right)^2=2^2=4" />.

## Exponential Functions

An exponential function has the form <InlineMath math="f(x)=a^x" /> with <InlineMath math="a>0" /> and <InlineMath math="a\neq1" />:

1. If <InlineMath math="a>1" />, the function increases as <InlineMath math="x" /> increases
2. If <InlineMath math="0<a<1" />, the function decreases as <InlineMath math="x" /> increases

The first case models growth; the second models decay. The model is useful only while its factor and time interval reasonably match the situation being studied.

## Applications

### Bacterial Growth

In an ideal culture model, suppose every bacterium divides into two during each interval, with no deaths or resource limit. If <InlineMath math="B_0" /> is the initial count, then after <InlineMath math="t" /> intervals:

<BlockMath math="B(t)=B_0\times2^t" />

The base <InlineMath math="2" /> is the growth factor. If the culture starts with <InlineMath math="100" /> bacteria and divides every <InlineMath math="30\text{ minutes}" />, then <InlineMath math="2\text{ hours}" /> contains <InlineMath math="4" /> intervals:

### Virus Spread

A simplified early-stage transmission model uses an average factor <InlineMath math="R" />:

<BlockMath math="C(n)=C_0\times R^n" />

Here, <InlineMath math="C_0" /> is the initial number of cases and <InlineMath math="n" /> is the number of transmission cycles. If <InlineMath math="C_0=10" />, <InlineMath math="R=2.5" />, and <InlineMath math="n=3" />, then:

<BlockMath math="C(3)=10\times2.5^3=156.25\approx156" />

The decimal result is an expected count from the model, not a fraction of a person. In practice, <InlineMath math="R" /> changes with behavior, immunity, interventions, and measurement, so the calculation should not be read as an exact forecast.

### Population Growth

If a population grows by the same relative rate <InlineMath math="r" /> during every interval, then:

<BlockMath math="P(t)=P_0(1+r)^t" />

The factor <InlineMath math="1+r" /> includes the original population and the new growth. For an initial population of <InlineMath math="1{,}000{,}000" /> and a constant annual growth rate of <InlineMath math="2\%" />:

<BlockMath math="P(10)=1{,}000{,}000(1.02)^{10}\approx1{,}218{,}994" />

This is a compound-growth estimate. A real population rate can change from year to year.
