## A Logarithm Answers an Exponent Question

A logarithm reverses exponentiation. If <InlineMath math="a^c=b" />, then the logarithm asks: “To what exponent must the base <InlineMath math="a" /> be raised to produce <InlineMath math="b" />?”

<BlockMath math="\log_a b=c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a^c=b" />

For real logarithms, the conditions are:

<BlockMath math="a>0\qquad a\neq1\qquad b>0" />

- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the **base**
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the **argument**
- <InlineMath math="c" /> is the exponent returned by the logarithm

The conditions match the behavior of the exponential function. A valid exponential base is positive and not equal to one, and <InlineMath math="a^c" /> is always positive, so a real logarithm cannot have a nonpositive argument.

## Converting Between the Two Forms

Each row states the same relationship twice:

| Exponential form | Logarithmic form |
| ---------------- | ---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="2^5=32" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_2 32=5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3^2=9" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_3 9=2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5^{-2}=\frac1{25}" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_5\frac1{25}=-2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="7^0=1" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_7 1=0" /> |

When converting, keep the base unchanged. The exponential output becomes the logarithm's argument, and the exponent becomes the logarithm's value.

## Common and Natural Logarithms

A logarithm with base <InlineMath math="10" /> is called the **common logarithm**. Its base is often omitted:

<BlockMath math="\log_{10}x=\log x" />

A logarithm with base <InlineMath math="e" /> is called the **natural logarithm** and is written <InlineMath math="\ln x" />. Both notations are special cases of <InlineMath math="\log_a x" />.

## Solving for an Unknown Exponent

Suppose an idealized culture starts with <InlineMath math="2{,}000" /> bacteria and doubles every hour:

<BlockMath math="P(x)=2{,}000\cdot2^x" />

To find when the continuous model reaches <InlineMath math="100{,}000" />, set the output equal to the target:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="100{,}000=2{,}000\cdot2^x" />
  <BlockMath math="50=2^x" />
  <BlockMath math="x=\log_2 50" />
</MathContainer>

Using a calculator or change of base:

<BlockMath math="x=\frac{\ln50}{\ln2}\approx5.643856" />

The continuous curve crosses <InlineMath math="100{,}000" /> at about <InlineMath math="5.64\text{ hours}" />. If the culture is counted only after completed hourly divisions, the interpretation changes:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(5)=2{,}000\cdot2^5=64{,}000" />
  <BlockMath math="P(6)=2{,}000\cdot2^6=128{,}000" />
</MathContainer>

So the first completed hourly interval at or above the target is <InlineMath math="6\text{ hours}" />. The logarithm solves the equation; the context determines how that real-valued answer should be interpreted.
