## Sequences

A number sequence is an arrangement of numbers that follows a specific pattern. Two common types are [arithmetic sequences](./arithmetic-sequence) and [geometric sequences](./geometric-sequence).

## Arithmetic Sequences

### Definition of Arithmetic Sequences

An arithmetic sequence is a sequence of numbers where the difference between consecutive terms is constant.

If we have a sequence <InlineMath math="a_1, a_2, a_3, ..., a_n" />, then it is an arithmetic sequence if the difference between consecutive terms is always the same:

<BlockMath math="a_2 - a_1 = a_3 - a_2 = a_4 - a_3 = ... = a_n - a_{n-1} = b" />

In this equation, <InlineMath math="b" /> is the constant difference between consecutive terms.

### Formula for the General Term of an Arithmetic Sequence

For an arithmetic sequence with first term <InlineMath math="a" /> and common difference <InlineMath math="b" />, the formula for the general term is:

<BlockMath math="U_n = a + (n-1)b" />

## Geometric Sequences

### Definition of Geometric Sequences

A geometric sequence is a sequence of numbers where the ratio between consecutive terms is constant.

If we have a sequence <InlineMath math="a_1, a_2, a_3, ..., a_n" />, then it is a geometric sequence if the ratio between consecutive terms is always the same:

<BlockMath math="\frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{a_3}{a_2} = \frac{a_4}{a_3} = ... = \frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}} = r" />

In this equation, <InlineMath math="r" /> is the constant ratio. If a denominator is zero, the quotient form is unavailable, but the equivalent multiplication rule <InlineMath math="a_{n+1}=ra_n" /> remains meaningful.

### Formula for the General Term of a Geometric Sequence

For a geometric sequence with first term <InlineMath math="a" /> and common ratio <InlineMath math="r" />, the formula for the general term is:

<BlockMath math="U_n = a \times r^{n-1}" />

## Arithmetic Sequences Use Differences and Geometric Sequences Use Ratios

### How to Identify the Type of Sequence

To determine whether a sequence is arithmetic or geometric:

1. **Arithmetic Sequence**: Calculate the difference between consecutive terms. If the difference is always the same, then the sequence is arithmetic.

   <BlockMath math="b = a_2 - a_1 = a_3 - a_2 = a_4 - a_3 = ..." />

2. **Geometric Sequence**: Calculate the ratio between consecutive terms. If the ratio is always the same, then the sequence is geometric.

   <BlockMath math="r = \frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{a_3}{a_2} = \frac{a_4}{a_3} = ..." />

### Comparison Table

| Aspect                   | Arithmetic Sequence                    | Geometric Sequence                           |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Pattern                  | Constant difference                    | Constant ratio                               |
| Formula for the general term | <InlineMath math="U_n = a + (n-1)b" /> | <InlineMath math="U_n = a \times r^{n-1}" /> |
| How the next term is formed | Add a constant                         | Multiply by a constant                       |

### Worked Classification Examples

The sequence <InlineMath math="7,11,15,19,\ldots" /> has constant differences:

<BlockMath math="11-7=15-11=19-15=4" />

These equal differences show that the sequence is arithmetic. The sequence <InlineMath math="5,10,20,40,\ldots" /> satisfies:

<BlockMath math="10=2\cdot5,\qquad20=2\cdot10,\qquad40=2\cdot20" />

Each term is twice the previous term, so the sequence is geometric with <InlineMath math="r=2" />. The sequence <InlineMath math="1,2,4,7,\ldots" /> is neither arithmetic nor geometric because its differences <InlineMath math="1,2,3" /> are not constant and its ratios also vary.

### Linear and Exponential Models

A non-constant arithmetic sequence depends linearly on its index. A geometric sequence with a positive ratio other than one depends exponentially on its index. These descriptions identify the mathematical form, but they do not imply that a real process continues unchanged forever.

Adding the same amount each month creates an arithmetic model. Increasing by the same percentage each month creates a geometric model.

### Special Cases

The constant zero sequence is arithmetic with <InlineMath math="b=0" /> and also satisfies <InlineMath math="a_{n+1}=ra_n" /> for every ratio <InlineMath math="r" />. A constant non-zero sequence is arithmetic with <InlineMath math="b=0" /> and geometric with <InlineMath math="r=1" />.

The arithmetic and geometric categories therefore overlap in these special cases.

## Arithmetic and Geometric Models

### Examples of Arithmetic Sequences

1. **Regular Savings**: A student saves money in the school cooperative with an arithmetic pattern. In the first month, they save <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}5{,}000" />, in the second month <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}7{,}000" />, in the third month <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}9{,}000" />, and so on. With a difference of <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}2{,}000" />, the amount deposited in month <InlineMath math="10" /> can be calculated using the arithmetic sequence formula.

2. **Plant Growth**: In a simplified model, a plant grows <InlineMath math="3 \text{ cm}" /> taller every week from an initial height of <InlineMath math="15 \text{ cm}" />. Its modeled height follows an arithmetic sequence.

### Examples of Geometric Sequences

1. **Investment with Compound Interest**: Suppose <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}1{,}000{,}000" /> earns <InlineMath math="10\%" /> interest compounded annually, with no deposits or withdrawals. Its value at the end of each year forms a geometric sequence with a ratio of <InlineMath math="1.1" />.

2. **Idealized Population Growth**: A model in which a bacterial population doubles every hour forms a geometric sequence with a ratio of <InlineMath math="2" />. Real populations eventually face limits that this simple model omits.

## Exercise

Classify each sequence and determine <InlineMath math="b" /> or <InlineMath math="r" /> when possible:

1. <InlineMath math="18,12,6,0,\ldots" />
2. <InlineMath math="81,-27,9,-3,\ldots" />
3. <InlineMath math="2,4,8,14,\ldots" />

### Solution

1. The first sequence is arithmetic with <InlineMath math="b=-6" />.
2. The second sequence is geometric with <InlineMath math="r=-1/3" />.
3. The third sequence is neither arithmetic nor geometric because neither its differences nor its ratios are constant.
