## Concept of Geometric Series

Suppose a nursery prepares a new batch of seedlings each month according to the following idealized pattern:

| Month                 | January | February | March | April | May |
| :-------------------- | :------ | :------- | :---- | :---- | :-- |
| New seedlings prepared | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | <InlineMath math="36" /> | <InlineMath math="108" /> | <InlineMath math="324" /> |

The number prepared in each month forms a geometric sequence. Adding the new batches from the first month through a chosen month forms a **geometric series**. Because every batch is newly prepared, this sum does not count the same seedlings twice.

A geometric series is the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence. In that sequence, each term is obtained by multiplying the preceding term by a **constant factor** <InlineMath math="r" />. When the preceding term is nonzero, this factor is also the quotient of the two consecutive terms.

In the seedling data:

- The first term (<InlineMath math="a" /> or <InlineMath math="U_1" />) is <InlineMath math="4" />.
- The ratio <InlineMath math="(r) = \frac{12}{4} = \frac{36}{12} = \frac{108}{36} = \frac{324}{108} = 3" />.

So, the sequence of monthly batches is <InlineMath math="4, 12, 36, 108, 324" />.
The geometric series is the sum of the terms of this sequence:

- Sum of the first <InlineMath math="2 \text{ months}" /> <InlineMath math="(S_2) = 4 + 12 = 16" />
- Sum of the first <InlineMath math="3 \text{ months}" /> <InlineMath math="(S_3) = 4 + 12 + 36 = 52" />
- Sum of the first <InlineMath math="4 \text{ months}" /> <InlineMath math="(S_4) = 4 + 12 + 36 + 108 = 160" />
- and so on.

## Finding the Formula for the Sum of the First Terms

How can we calculate the sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms <InlineMath math="(S_n)" /> without adding them one by one? The following calculation derives the formula.

Consider this table which shows the process of rediscovering the formula for the sum of a geometric series:

| Notation                     | Direct Summation                              | Using <InlineMath math="U_{n+1}" /> and <InlineMath math="U_1" />       | General Form                                            |
| :--------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------ |
| <InlineMath math="S_2" />    | <InlineMath math="4 + 12 = 16" />             | <InlineMath math="S_2 = \frac{36 - 4}{3 - 1} = \frac{32}{2} = 16" />    | <InlineMath math="S_2 = \frac{U_3 - U_1}{r - 1}" />     |
| <InlineMath math="S_3" />    | <InlineMath math="4 + 12 + 36 = 52" />        | <InlineMath math="S_3 = \frac{108 - 4}{3 - 1} = \frac{104}{2} = 52" />  | <InlineMath math="S_3 = \frac{U_4 - U_1}{r - 1}" />     |
| <InlineMath math="S_4" />    | <InlineMath math="4 + 12 + 36 + 108 = 160" /> | <InlineMath math="S_4 = \frac{324 - 4}{3 - 1} = \frac{320}{2} = 160" /> | <InlineMath math="S_4 = \frac{U_5 - U_1}{r - 1}" />     |
| <InlineMath math="\vdots" /> | <InlineMath math="\dots" />                   | <InlineMath math="\dots" />                                             | <InlineMath math="\dots" />                             |
| <InlineMath math="S_n" />    | <InlineMath math="\dots" />                   | <InlineMath math="\dots" />                                             | <InlineMath math="S_n = \frac{U_{n+1} - U_1}{r - 1}" /> |

For <InlineMath math="r\ne1" />, the bottom-right cell gives the general form:

<BlockMath math="S_n = \frac{U_{n+1} - U_1}{r - 1}" />

We know that the formula for the <InlineMath math="n" />-th term in a geometric sequence is <InlineMath math="U_n = ar^{n-1}" />. Thus, <InlineMath math="U_{n+1} = ar^{(n+1)-1} = ar^n" />.
Substitute <InlineMath math="U_{n+1} = ar^n" /> and <InlineMath math="U_1 = a" /> into the <InlineMath math="S_n" /> formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="S_n = \frac{ar^n - a}{r - 1}" />
  <BlockMath math="S_n = \frac{a(r^n - 1)}{r - 1}" />
</MathContainer>

This is the formula for the sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms of a geometric series.

We can also derive the same result by multiplying the sum by <InlineMath math="r" /> and subtracting:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="S_n=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{n-1}" />
  <BlockMath math="rS_n=ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^n" />
  <BlockMath math="(1-r)S_n=a(1-r^n)" />
</MathContainer>

The middle terms cancel. This explains why the formula contains only the first term and the next power after the final term.

## Geometric Series Formula

In general, the formula to calculate the sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms of a geometric series is:

<BlockMath math="S_n=\frac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1},\quad r\ne1" />

An equivalent form is

<BlockMath math="S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r},\quad r\ne1" />

Both forms work for positive or negative ratios as long as <InlineMath math="r\ne1" />. If <InlineMath math="r=1" />, all terms equal <InlineMath math="a" />, so:

<BlockMath math="S_n=na" />

**Legend:**

- <InlineMath math="S_n" /> = sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms
- <InlineMath math="a" /> = first term <InlineMath math="(U_1)" />
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = ratio <InlineMath math="(r \neq 1)" />
- <InlineMath math="n" /> = number of terms

## Calculating Total Bicycle Production

In a simplified production plan for <InlineMath math="2020" />, a bicycle company's newly produced units each month form a geometric sequence. January production is <InlineMath math="120 \text{ units}" />, and April production is <InlineMath math="3{,}240 \text{ units}" />. What is the total production from January through May?

**Solution:**

- January Production <InlineMath math="(U_1) = a = 120" />
- April Production <InlineMath math="(U_4) = 3{,}240" />
- Question: Total production up to May <InlineMath math="(S_5)" />

**Step** <InlineMath math="1" />: Find the ratio <InlineMath math="(r)" />

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="U_4 = ar^{4-1} = ar^3" />
  <BlockMath math="3{,}240 = 120 \cdot r^3" />
  <BlockMath math="r^3 = \frac{3{,}240}{120}" />
  <BlockMath math="r^3 = 27" />
  <BlockMath math="r = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3" />
</MathContainer>

**Step** <InlineMath math="2" />: Calculate <InlineMath math="S_5" />. Since <InlineMath math="r = 3 > 1" />, use the formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="S_n = \frac{a(r^n - 1)}{r - 1}" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5 = \frac{120(3^5 - 1)}{3 - 1}" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5 = \frac{120(243 - 1)}{2}" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5 = \frac{120(242)}{2}" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5 = 60 \cdot 242" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5 = 14{,}520" />
</MathContainer>

The total bicycle production up to May is <InlineMath math="14{,}520 \text{ units}" />.

The addition is meaningful here because each monthly value represents newly produced bicycles. Adding repeated measurements of one unchanged stock would instead count the same objects more than once.

## Practice with a Negative Ratio

Calculate the finite sum:

<BlockMath math="81-27+9-3+1" />

The first term is <InlineMath math="a=81" />, the ratio is <InlineMath math="r=-\frac13" />, and there are <InlineMath math="n=5" /> terms. Therefore:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="S_5=81\left(\frac{1-(-1/3)^5}{1-(-1/3)}\right)" />
  <BlockMath math="S_5=81\left(\frac{244/243}{4/3}\right)=61" />
</MathContainer>

Check by direct addition: <InlineMath math="81-27+9-3+1=61" />.
