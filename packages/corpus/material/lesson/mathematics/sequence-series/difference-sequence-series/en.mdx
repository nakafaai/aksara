## Sequence

An ordered list of numbers that follows a rule is a <Highlight>sequence</Highlight>, and each number in that list is a <Highlight>term</Highlight>. The **position of each term** determines the order. Swapping two unequal terms produces a different sequence.

### Terms and Order in a Sequence

A sequence rule determines the **value at each position**. For the even numbers, multiply the position by two. For the square numbers, square the position. These two examples already have different values at the first position.

- Sequence of even numbers: <InlineMath math="2, 4, 6, 8, \dots" />
- Sequence of squares: <InlineMath math="1, 4, 9, 16, \dots" />

In both examples the sequence records each separate term and the rule that produces the next one.

## Series

A series is written as a **sum of sequence terms**. The partial sum <InlineMath math="S_n" /> adds the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms. Adding one more term changes the partial sum by the value of that new term. If the new term is zero, the partial sum stays unchanged.

### A Series as a Sum

A series **adds the terms** of a sequence, and the plus signs between the terms show that addition. The two sums below add the even numbers and the square numbers one term at a time.

- Series of even numbers: <InlineMath math="2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + \dots" />
- Series of squares: <InlineMath math="1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + \dots" />

When the terms stop after the <InlineMath math="n" />-th term, the value of the sum is the partial sum, usually written <InlineMath math="S_n" />.

## Difference between a Sequence and a Series

The table puts the two side by side. A sequence keeps the terms in order, and a series represents **addition of those terms**.

| Aspect      | Sequence                                                                                | Series                                                                           |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Form**    | Ordered list of numbers separated by commas, such as <InlineMath math="U_1, U_2, U_3, \dots" /> | Sum of numbers separated by plus signs, such as <InlineMath math="U_1 + U_2 + U_3 + \dots" /> |
| **Focus**   | Pattern and value of each individual term                                               | Result of summing the terms                                                      |

## Partial Sums of Sequence Terms

The sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms of a sequence is its <InlineMath math="n" />th **partial sum**:

<BlockMath math="S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k" />

A sequence <InlineMath math="(a_n)" /> therefore creates a new sequence <InlineMath math="(S_n)" /> of partial sums. For <InlineMath math="a_n=n" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4,\ldots=1,2,3,4,\ldots" />
  <BlockMath math="S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4,\ldots=1,3,6,10,\ldots" />
</MathContainer>

Adding the terms of <InlineMath math="a_n=n" /> produces a second sequence, <InlineMath math="1, 3, 6, 10, \dots" />, and those partial sums grow faster than the original terms.

Now add the terms as you go. Bar <InlineMath math="n" /> is the partial sum <InlineMath math="S_n = 1 + 2 + \cdots + n" />, so the bars read <InlineMath math="1, 3, 6, 10, \ldots, 36" />. Compare bar 8 with the previous chart: the term is <InlineMath math="8" />, but the sum of the first eight terms is <InlineMath math="36" />.

## Finite and Infinite Sums

<InlineMath math="S_{20}" /> is a finite sum of twenty terms. For an infinite series, we instead ask whether <InlineMath math="S_n" /> approaches a limit as <InlineMath math="n\to\infty" />.

An infinite series is defined through the **limit of its partial sums**. When those partial sums approach one fixed value, the series converges. The topic [convergent and divergent series](/en/subjects/mathematics/sequence-series/convergence-divergence) uses this limit to separate series that settle from those that do not.

The partial sums of <InlineMath math="a_n = n" /> lie on a parabola. Each point <InlineMath math="(n, S_n)" /> sits on <InlineMath math="y = \tfrac12 x^2 + \tfrac12 x" />, because <InlineMath math="S_n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}" />. A sequence that grows by equal steps has partial sums that grow like a square.

## Exercise

Given the sequence <InlineMath math="a_n=2n" />. Write its first four terms, then determine its first four partial sums. Keep the two lists apart, because a **term describes one position** while a partial sum totals every term up to that position.

1. Write its first four terms.
2. Determine its first four partial sums.

### Solution

Substituting <InlineMath math="n=1,2,3,4" /> into <InlineMath math="a_n=2n" /> gives the first four terms, and **adding those terms one by one** gives the partial sums.

| Position | Problem 1: term | Problem 2: partial sum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="a_1=2\cdot1=2" /> | <InlineMath math="S_1=2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="a_2=2\cdot2=4" /> | <InlineMath math="S_2=2+4=6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="a_3=2\cdot3=6" /> | <InlineMath math="S_3=2+4+6=12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="a_4=2\cdot4=8" /> | <InlineMath math="S_4=2+4+6+8=20" /> |
