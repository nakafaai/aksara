## Number Series

How can the terms of a number sequence be accumulated? An expression such as <InlineMath math="1+2+3+\cdots" /> is called a **series**.

A **number series** is formed by adding the terms of a number sequence in their given order. Two common examples are an **arithmetic series**, whose terms come from an arithmetic sequence, and a **geometric series**, whose terms come from a geometric sequence.

In the handshake example below, each new person adds another term to the sum.

## Partial Sums Connect a Sequence to Its Series

For a sequence <InlineMath math="(U_n)" />, the sum of its first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms is the <InlineMath math="n" />th partial sum:

<BlockMath math="S_n=U_1+U_2+\cdots+U_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_k" />

Finite problems ask for a particular partial sum. For an infinite series, we study whether the sequence <InlineMath math="(S_n)" /> approaches a finite limit as <InlineMath math="n" /> grows.

## Number of Handshakes

Imagine there are several people in a group. If each person shakes hands exactly once with every other person in the group, how many total handshakes occur?

Count the handshakes step by step:

- **If there are <InlineMath math="2 \text{ people}" />:** There is only <InlineMath math="1 \text{ handshake}" />.
- **If there are <InlineMath math="3 \text{ people}" />:** The first person shakes hands with <InlineMath math="2 \text{ people}" />. The second person has already shaken hands with the first, so they only need to shake hands with the third person. The total is <InlineMath math="2 + 1 = 3 \text{ handshakes}" />.
- **If there are <InlineMath math="4 \text{ people}" />:** Following the same pattern, we get <InlineMath math="3 + 2 + 1 = 6 \text{ handshakes}" />.
- **If there are <InlineMath math="5 \text{ people}" />:** The total is <InlineMath math="4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 \text{ handshakes}" />.

The number of handshakes forms a sequential sum of natural numbers.

| Number of people present | Number of handshakes | Breakdown of handshakes       |
| :----------------------- | :------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| Two people               | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Three people             | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="1+2" />     |
| Four people              | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="1+2+3" />   |
| Five people              | <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="1+2+3+4" /> |

Summations like <InlineMath math="1+2+3+4" /> are examples of a **number series**. In this case, the series is formed from the sum of the terms of the sequence of natural numbers <InlineMath math="(1, 2, 3, 4, ...)" />.

Each pair must be counted exactly once. Counting every person's handshakes independently would count each pair twice, once from each person's point of view.

### A Second Counting Method

A handshake is an unordered pair of people. The number of pairs chosen from <InlineMath math="n" /> people is:

<BlockMath math="\binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}" />

For <InlineMath math="n=5" />, this gives <InlineMath math="\binom52=10" />, matching the sequential sum.

### Arithmetic and Geometric Patterns

The handshake totals <InlineMath math="(1, 3, 6, 10, ...)" /> are neither arithmetic nor geometric because consecutive terms have no constant difference or ratio (<InlineMath math="3-1=2" />, <InlineMath math="6-3=3" />, <InlineMath math="10-6=4" />).

Each expression <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="1+2" />, <InlineMath math="1+2+3" />, and <InlineMath math="1+2+3+4" /> is a partial sum of the natural-number sequence. These partial sums show how a **series** is built from the terms of a sequence.

## Practice

How many handshakes occur among <InlineMath math="12 \text{ people}" /> if every pair shakes hands exactly once?

The sequential-sum method gives:

<BlockMath math="S_{11}=1+2+\cdots+11" />

The pair-counting formula gives the same result directly:

<BlockMath math="S_{11}=\frac{12\cdot11}{2}=66" />

There are <InlineMath math="66 \text{ handshakes}" />.
