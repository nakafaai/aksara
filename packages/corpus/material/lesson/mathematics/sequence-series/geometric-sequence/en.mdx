## Multiplying Each Term by the Same Factor

In a geometric sequence, every term after the first is obtained by **multiplying the previous term by a constant factor**. This ratio is denoted by the letter <InlineMath math="r" />.

For nonzero terms, we can find that factor by division:

<BlockMath math="\frac{U_2}{U_1} = \frac{U_3}{U_2} = \frac{U_4}{U_3} = ... = r" />

Each term is obtained by multiplying the previous term by the ratio <InlineMath math="r" />. The quotient form above requires nonzero denominators, but the multiplication rule also works when a term is zero:

<BlockMath math="U_{n+1}=rU_n" />

## Paper Folding Exploration

In a simple folding model, **each fold doubles** the number of layers. Fold a rectangular sheet of paper in half several times.

If the paper is folded <InlineMath math="1 \text{ time}" />, it will be divided into <InlineMath math="2 \text{ parts}" /> of equal size. If folded again (<InlineMath math="2 \text{ times}" />), it will form <InlineMath math="4 \text{ parts}" /> of equal size. The following pattern emerges:

| Number of folds | Number of equal parts |
| --------------- | --------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="1 \text{ fold}" />          | <InlineMath math="2 \text{ parts}" />               |
| <InlineMath math="2 \text{ folds}" />         | <InlineMath math="4 \text{ parts}" />               |
| <InlineMath math="3 \text{ folds}" />         | <InlineMath math="8 \text{ parts}" />               |
| <InlineMath math="4 \text{ folds}" />         | <InlineMath math="16 \text{ parts}" />              |

Notice that the number of parts formed creates a sequence: <InlineMath math="2, 4, 8, 16, \ldots" />

In this sequence, each term is obtained by multiplying the previous term by <InlineMath math="2" />, so the ratio is <InlineMath math="2" />.

## General Formula for Geometric Sequences

For a **nonzero ratio** and a positive integer term number, the general term is:

<BlockMath math="U_n = a \cdot r^{n-1}" />

If <InlineMath math="r=0" />, use <InlineMath math="U_1=a" /> and <InlineMath math="U_n=0" /> for <InlineMath math="n\ge2" />. This avoids evaluating <InlineMath math="0^0" /> for the first term.

The quantities in this formula are:

- <InlineMath math="U_n" /> = general term
- <InlineMath math="a" /> = first term
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = ratio
- <InlineMath math="n" /> = term number

The exponent is <InlineMath math="n-1" /> because moving from the first term to the <InlineMath math="n" />th term takes exactly <InlineMath math="n-1" /> multiplications by <InlineMath math="r" />.

## Sequence Behavior for Different Ratios

For a **positive first term**, the ratio determines the overall behavior:

| Ratio | First terms starting at 8 | Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="8,16,32,64" /> | Increasing |
| <InlineMath math="1/2" /> | <InlineMath math="8,4,2,1" /> | Approaching zero |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="8,8,8,8" /> | Constant |
| <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="8,0,0,0" /> | Zero after the first term |
| <InlineMath math="-2" /> | <InlineMath math="8,-16,32,-64" /> | Alternating signs, increasing magnitudes |
| <InlineMath math="-1" /> | <InlineMath math="8,-8,8,-8" /> | Alternating signs, constant magnitudes |
| <InlineMath math="-1/2" /> | <InlineMath math="8,-4,2,-1" /> | Alternating signs, magnitudes approaching zero |

This is why a geometric sequence does not always represent growth.

## An Idealized Bacterial Growth Model

Suppose the number of bacteria in an ideal culture **triples** every <InlineMath math="2 \text{ hours}" />. The factor <InlineMath math="3" /> describes how the total number of bacteria changes during each interval.

If the initial number of bacteria is <InlineMath math="2 \text{ cells}" />, then:

- Initial count (<InlineMath math="N_0" />) <InlineMath math="=2" />
- Growth factor (<InlineMath math="r" />) <InlineMath math="=3" />

In <InlineMath math="20 \text{ hours}" />, the culture passes through <InlineMath math="10 \text{ growth intervals}" /> because <InlineMath math="20 \div 2=10" />.

Starting at index <InlineMath math="0" /> keeps the initial count separate from the first completed growth interval. After <InlineMath math="10" /> intervals:

<BlockMath math="N_{10}=N_0\cdot r^{10}=2\cdot3^{10}=118{,}098" />

So, under this idealized model, the culture contains <InlineMath math="118{,}098 \text{ bacterial cells}" /> after <InlineMath math="20 \text{ hours}" />.

## Growth and Decay Models Using a Constant Ratio

Each process below repeats one multiplication at every step, so the same ratio drives the whole list:

1. Population growth (as in the bacteria example)
2. Compound interest in economics
3. Radioactive decay in physics
4. Cell growth in biology

A geometric sequence models repeated growth or decay only when <Highlight>the same ratio applies at every step</Highlight>.

Always check whether a constant-factor model is reasonable for the situation and for the time span being studied. Real populations eventually face limits that this simple model does not include.

## Finding a Ratio and an Unknown Term

Both examples give part of a geometric sequence and ask for the missing value. Substitute the **known terms** into the general formula and solve for the unknown.

### Finding the Ratio

The first term of a geometric sequence is <InlineMath math="4" /> and term <InlineMath math="4" /> is <InlineMath math="108" />. Determine the ratio of this sequence.

**Solution**:

Given:

- <InlineMath math="a = 4" /> (first term)
- <InlineMath math="U_4 = 108" /> (term <InlineMath math="4" />)

Using the general formula for geometric sequences:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="U_4 = a \cdot r^{4-1}" />
  <BlockMath math="108 = 4 \cdot r^3" />
  <BlockMath math="r^3 = \frac{108}{4} = 27" />
  <BlockMath math="r = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3" />
</MathContainer>

The ratio of the geometric sequence is <InlineMath math="3" />.

### Length of Rope Sections

A rope is divided into <InlineMath math="5 \text{ parts}" /> whose positive lengths, ordered from shortest to longest, form a geometric sequence. The shortest piece is <InlineMath math="16 \text{ cm}" /> and the longest is <InlineMath math="81 \text{ cm}" />. Determine the length of the third piece.

**Solution**:

Given:

- <InlineMath math="a = 16" /> (shortest piece)
- <InlineMath math="U_5 = 81" /> (longest piece)

Each piece has a **positive length**, so the ratio is positive too. We therefore take the positive fourth root:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="U_5 = a \cdot r^{5-1}" />
  <BlockMath math="81 = 16 \cdot r^4" />
  <BlockMath math="r^4 = \frac{81}{16}" />
  <BlockMath math="r = \sqrt[4]{\frac{81}{16}} = \frac{3}{2}" />
</MathContainer>

Then, find the length of the third piece (<InlineMath math="U_3" />):

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="U_3 = a \cdot r^{3-1}" />
  <BlockMath math="U_3 = 16 \cdot \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^2" />
  <BlockMath math="U_3 = 16 \cdot \frac{9}{4}" />
  <BlockMath math="U_3 = 36" />
</MathContainer>

The length of the third piece is <InlineMath math="36 \text{ cm}" />.
