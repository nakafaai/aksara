## Number Sequences

A number sequence assigns numbers to positions in a fixed order. Each number in the sequence is called a **term**. We use the following notation:

- The term at index <InlineMath math="1" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="U_1" />
- The term at index <InlineMath math="2" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="U_2" />
- The term at index <InlineMath math="3" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="U_3" />
- The general term is denoted by <InlineMath math="U_n" />

A sequence rule determines its later terms and may also give a formula for the general term.

More precisely, a sequence assigns one value to each allowed index:

<BlockMath math="U:\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad n\mapsto U_n" />

Order is part of the definition. The sequences <InlineMath math="1,2,3,\ldots" /> and <InlineMath math="3,2,1,\ldots" /> may contain some of the same numbers, but they are not the same sequence.

## Explicit and Recursive Rules

An **explicit rule** calculates a term directly from its index. For example:

<BlockMath math="U_n=2n+1" />

This gives <InlineMath math="3,5,7,9,\ldots" />. A **recursive rule** states a starting term and explains how to obtain the next term:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="U_1=3" />
  <BlockMath math="U_{n+1}=U_n+2" />
</MathContainer>

Both rules describe the same sequence. The explicit form calculates any term directly from its index. The recursive form calculates each new term from the previous one.

<BlockMath math="U_n = 2n + 2" />

- <InlineMath math="U_n" /> is the number of chairs

## Applications of Sequence Concepts

### Determining the Number of Chairs and Tables

The sequence rule answers questions such as:

**If there are <InlineMath math="20 \text{ people}" /> who will sit on chairs, how many tables need to be joined?**

We can use the formula <InlineMath math="U_n = 2n + 2" /> where <InlineMath math="U_n = 20" />, so:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="20 = 2n + 2" />
  <BlockMath math="18 = 2n" />
  <BlockMath math="n = 9" />
</MathContainer>

<InlineMath math="9 \text{ tables}" /> need to be joined to accommodate <InlineMath math="20 \text{ people}" />.

## Types of Sequences

Based on their patterns, number sequences can be classified into several types:

### Arithmetic Sequence

A number sequence where the difference between two consecutive terms is always constant. This difference is called the common difference (<InlineMath math="b" />).

Example: <InlineMath math="2, 4, 6, 8, 10, \ldots" /> Common difference <InlineMath math="(b) = 2" />

### Geometric Sequence

A number sequence where the ratio between two consecutive terms is always constant. This ratio is called the common ratio (<InlineMath math="r" />).

Example: <InlineMath math="2, 6, 18, 54, \ldots" /> Common ratio <InlineMath math="(r) = 3" />

### Other Sequences

Besides arithmetic and geometric sequences, there are many other types of sequences such as Fibonacci sequences, quadratic sequences, cubic sequences, and more.

Example of a Fibonacci sequence: <InlineMath math="0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \ldots" />

## A Finite Prefix Does Not Determine One Unique Rule

A short list of terms can suggest a plausible pattern, but it does not logically force one continuation. The prefix <InlineMath math="1,2,4" /> might continue as <InlineMath math="8,16,\ldots" /> if every term is doubled. A different rule could agree with the first three terms and change from the fourth term onward.

A fully defined sequence needs an unambiguous rule or enough conditions to identify it. Guessing a pattern produces a conjecture. A proof requires a rule that determines every term.

## Finding Patterns

To determine the pattern of a sequence:

1. Observe the differences between consecutive terms
2. Check if the difference is constant (arithmetic sequence)
3. If not, check if the ratio is constant (geometric sequence)
4. If neither, check for other possible patterns

A sequence assigns one term to each index. Its rule may be explicit, recursive, or defined in another way.
