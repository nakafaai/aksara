## Bouncing Ball

Suppose a tennis ball is dropped from a height of <InlineMath math="1 \text{ meter}" />. In an idealized model, each bounce reaches <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" /> of the previous height.

The release height followed by the rebound heights forms a **geometric sequence**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="1, \quad 1 \times \frac{1}{4}, \quad (1 \times \frac{1}{4}) \times \frac{1}{4}, \quad \dots" />
  <BlockMath math="1, \quad \frac{1}{4}, \quad \frac{1}{16}, \quad \frac{1}{64}, \quad \dots" />
</MathContainer>

The first term (<InlineMath math="a" />) is <InlineMath math="1" />, and the ratio (<InlineMath math="r" />) is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" />.

What _total distance_ does the ball travel from the moment it is released until it comes to rest?

In the idealized model, the ball moves down, then up, down again, and so on without a final bounce. The total distance is built from all downward and upward paths. Because their lengths form an infinite geometric pattern, the calculation uses an **infinite geometric series**.

### When Does the Series Converge

The mathematical series does not have a final bounce. Instead, its partial sums may approach a finite limit. For a geometric series, this happens when the absolute value of the ratio <InlineMath math="r" /> is less than <InlineMath math="1" />.

<BlockMath math="|r| < 1 \quad \text{or} \quad -1 < r < 1" />

A series like this is called **convergent**, meaning its partial sums approach a specific finite value. In the ball example, <InlineMath math="r = \frac{1}{4}" />, and since <InlineMath math="-1 < \frac{1}{4} < 1" />, the series is convergent.

If <InlineMath math="|r| \ge 1" /> and <InlineMath math="a\ne0" />, the terms do not approach zero, so the series is **divergent**. Its partial sums may grow without bound, decrease without bound, or oscillate. Divergence means that no finite limit exists. It does not always mean <InlineMath math="+\infty" /> or <InlineMath math="-\infty" />.

## Calculating the Sum of an Infinite Series

How do we calculate the sum of a convergent infinite series? We start with the formula for the sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> terms of a geometric series:

<BlockMath math="S_n = \frac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}" />

For an infinite series, we examine <InlineMath math="S_n" /> as <InlineMath math="n" /> grows without bound. If the series is convergent (<InlineMath math="-1 < r < 1" />), then <InlineMath math="r^n" /> approaches zero as <InlineMath math="n" /> approaches infinity. For example, <InlineMath math="(\frac{1}{4})^2 = \frac{1}{16}" />, <InlineMath math="(\frac{1}{4})^3 = \frac{1}{64}" />, and <InlineMath math="(\frac{1}{4})^{10} \approx 0.00000095" />. The larger <InlineMath math="n" /> becomes, the closer <InlineMath math="(\frac{1}{4})^n" /> gets to <InlineMath math="0" />.

So, for <InlineMath math="n \to \infty" /> and <InlineMath math="-1 < r < 1" />, we have <InlineMath math="r^n \to 0" />. The formula becomes:

<BlockMath math="S_\infty = \lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r} = \frac{a(1 - 0)}{1 - r}" />

The formula for the sum of a convergent infinite geometric series is:

<BlockMath math="S_\infty = \frac{a}{1 - r}" />

In this notation:

- <InlineMath math="S_\infty" /> = sum of the infinite series
- <InlineMath math="a" /> = first term
- <InlineMath math="r" /> = ratio, with <InlineMath math="-1<r<1" />

## A Check with Halved Distances

Consider the series:

<BlockMath math="1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots" />

Here <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="r=\frac12" />, so:

<BlockMath math="S_\infty=\frac{1}{1-1/2}=2" />

No finite partial sum equals <InlineMath math="2" />, but the partial sums can get as close to <InlineMath math="2" /> as desired. The infinite sum is the limit of those finite partial sums.

## Calculating the Total Distance

We can calculate the total distance traveled by the ball using the <InlineMath math="S_\infty" /> formula. There are two parts to the path:

1.  **Downward Path:** The ball falls from height <InlineMath math="a" />, then falls again after the first bounce (<InlineMath math="ar" />), falls again after the second bounce (<InlineMath math="ar^2" />), and so on.

    - Downward series: <InlineMath math="a + ar + ar^2 + ar^3 + \dots" />
    - First term (<InlineMath math="a_{\downarrow}" />) is <InlineMath math="a" />
    - Ratio (<InlineMath math="r_{\downarrow}" />) is <InlineMath math="r" />
    - Sum of downward path: <InlineMath math="S_{\downarrow}=\frac{a_{\downarrow}}{1-r_{\downarrow}}=\frac{a}{1-r}" />

2.  **Upward Path:** The ball moves up after the first bounce (<InlineMath math="ar" />), up again after the second bounce (<InlineMath math="ar^2" />), and so on.
    - Upward series: <InlineMath math="ar + ar^2 + ar^3 + \dots" />
    - First term (<InlineMath math="a_{\uparrow}" />) is <InlineMath math="ar" />
    - Ratio (<InlineMath math="r_{\uparrow}" />) is <InlineMath math="r" />
    - Sum of upward path: <InlineMath math="S_{\uparrow}=\frac{a_{\uparrow}}{1-r_{\uparrow}}=\frac{ar}{1-r}" />

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{Total Distance} = \text{Sum of Downward Path} + \text{Sum of Upward Path}" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Total} = S_{\downarrow} + S_{\uparrow} = \frac{a}{1 - r} + \frac{ar}{1 - r}" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Total} = \frac{a(1 + r)}{1 - r}" />
</MathContainer>

For the tennis ball example with <InlineMath math="a = 1 \text{ meter}" /> and <InlineMath math="r = \frac{1}{4}" />:

<BlockMath math="\text{Total} = \frac{1(1 + \frac{1}{4})}{1 - \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1(\frac{5}{4})}{\frac{3}{4}} = \frac{5/4}{3/4} = \frac{5}{4} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{5}{3}" />

So, the model gives a total travel distance of <InlineMath math="\frac{5}{3} \text{ meters}" />.

This is an idealized mathematical model. A real ball does not complete infinitely many measurable bounces, and its bounce ratio is not perfectly constant.

**A Second Calculation:**
The total distance can also be calculated as the first downward path plus <InlineMath math="2 \text{ times}" /> the sum of all upward paths.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{Total} = a + 2 \times S_{\uparrow} = a + 2 \times \left( \frac{ar}{1 - r} \right)" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{Total} = 1 + 2 \times \left( \frac{1 \times \frac{1}{4}}{1 - \frac{1}{4}} \right) = 1 + 2 \times \left( \frac{1/4}{3/4} \right) = 1 + 2 \times \left( \frac{1}{3} \right) = 1 + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{5}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

Both methods give the same result.

## Practice with an Alternating Series

Find the sum of:

<BlockMath math="12-6+3-\frac32+\cdots" />

Here <InlineMath math="a=12" /> and <InlineMath math="r=-\frac12" />. Since <InlineMath math="|r|<1" />, the series converges:

<BlockMath math="S_\infty=\frac{12}{1-(-1/2)}=8" />

The negative ratio makes the terms alternate in sign, but their magnitudes shrink quickly enough for the partial sums to approach <InlineMath math="8" />.
