## How Reflection Changes the Vertical Coordinate

In a reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, the image of a point outside the axis lies on the opposite side of the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at the same distance. The <InlineMath math="x" />-axis <Highlight>acts like a horizontal mirror</Highlight>.

If a point <InlineMath math="P" /> has coordinates <InlineMath math="(x,y)" />, then its reflection, which we'll call <InlineMath math="P'" />, will have the same <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate, but its <InlineMath math="y" />-coordinate will be the negative of the original <InlineMath math="y" /> value.

Mathematically, if the initial point is <InlineMath math="P(x,y)" />, then after reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, its image is <InlineMath math="P'(x,-y)" />.

## Property of Reflection across the Horizontal Axis

Reflection keeps the horizontal coordinate and **reverses the vertical coordinate sign**. The midpoint between an original point and its image lies on the mirror axis:

<BlockMath math="P(x,y)\longmapsto P'(x,-y),\qquad M=\left(\frac{x+x}{2},\frac{y+(-y)}{2}\right)=(x,0)" />

Both points are at distance <InlineMath math="|y|" /> from the axis. If the original point lies on the axis, its vertical coordinate is zero and it remains fixed:

<BlockMath math="P(x,0)\longmapsto P'(x,-0)=P(x,0)" />

## Reflecting Triangles and Lines

Reflection acts on **every point of a figure**. Three vertices are enough to determine the reflected triangle. For a line, substitute the coordinate rule into its equation so it represents every point on the line at once.

### Reflecting a Triangle

Determine the image of triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" /> with vertices <InlineMath math="A(-1,4)" />, <InlineMath math="B(2,1)" />, and <InlineMath math="C(-2,-1)" /> reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis.

To determine the image of triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" />, we apply the reflection property to **each of its vertices**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="A(-1,4) \xrightarrow{\text{X-axis}} A'(-1,-4)" />
  <BlockMath math="B(2,1) \xrightarrow{\text{X-axis}} B'(2,-1)" />
  <BlockMath math="C(-2,-1) \xrightarrow{\text{X-axis}} C'(-2,1)" />
</MathContainer>

Consequently, the image of triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" /> is triangle <InlineMath math="A'B'C'" /> with vertices <InlineMath math="A'(-1,-4)" />, <InlineMath math="B'(2,-1)" />, and <InlineMath math="C'(-2,1)" />.

### Reflecting a Line

If a line has the equation <InlineMath math="2x - 3y = 0" /> and is reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, determine the equation of its reflected line.

**Solution**:

Let an arbitrary point <InlineMath math="P(a,b)" /> lie on the line <InlineMath math="2x - 3y = 0" />. Then, the following holds:

<BlockMath math="2a - 3b = 0" />

The point <InlineMath math="P(a,b)" /> reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis produces the image <InlineMath math="P'(a,-b)" />.

To obtain the equation of the reflected line, we substitute the coordinates of the image into new variables. Let <InlineMath math="x' = a" /> and <InlineMath math="y' = -b" />.

From this, we get <InlineMath math="a = x'" /> and <InlineMath math="b = -y'" />.

Substitute <InlineMath math="a = x'" /> and <InlineMath math="b = -y'" /> into the **original equation** <InlineMath math="2a - 3b = 0" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="2(x') - 3(-y') = 0" />
  <BlockMath math="2x' + 3y' = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="x'" /> and <InlineMath math="y'" /> are arbitrary variables representing the coordinates on the reflected line, we can rewrite them as <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />.
