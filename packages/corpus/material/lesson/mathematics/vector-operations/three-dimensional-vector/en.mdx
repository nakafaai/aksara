## Vectors in a Space with Three Coordinate Axes

A three-dimensional vector is described by <Highlight>three coordinate components</Highlight>. These components measure displacement along the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes. A nonzero vector has magnitude and direction, while the zero vector has no geometric direction.

## Representation of Three Dimensional Vectors

The <InlineMath math="z" />-axis is perpendicular to the <InlineMath math="xy" />-plane. In this Cartesian system, the **component order** always follows the <InlineMath math="x" />-, then <InlineMath math="y" />-, then <InlineMath math="z" />-axis.

For example, the third component of <InlineMath math="(3,4,2)" /> is <InlineMath math="2" />. It does not have to appear as vertical height on the screen.

### Notation of Three Dimensional Vectors

Three-dimensional vectors can be notated in various ways:

1. Letter notation with an arrow above it: <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> or <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" />
2. Component notation: <InlineMath math="(a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> or <InlineMath math="(a_1, a_2, a_3)" />
3. Basis notation: <InlineMath math="a_x\vec{i} + a_y\vec{j} + a_z\vec{k}" />

For the vector in the first diagram, these three component notations describe the **same displacement**:

<BlockMath math="\vec v=(3,4,2)=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\2\end{pmatrix}=3\vec i+4\vec j+2\vec k" />

### Components of Three Dimensional Vectors

A vector in three-dimensional space has **three scalar coordinate components**. Multiplying each scalar by its axis unit vector gives the corresponding vector projection:

<BlockMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z) = a_x\vec{i} + a_y\vec{j} + a_z\vec{k}" />

The components in this form are:

- <InlineMath math="a_x" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="a_x\vec{i}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="a_y" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="a_y\vec{j}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="a_z" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="z" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="a_z\vec{k}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="\vec{i}, \vec{j}, \vec{k}" /> are the unit vectors on the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes

## Magnitude of Three Dimensional Vectors

The magnitude or length of a three-dimensional vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> is determined by the formula:

<BlockMath math="|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2}" />

**Example**:

If <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (3, 4, 5)" />, then the magnitude of vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} |\vec{a}| &amp;= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{9 + 16 + 25} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{50} \\ &amp;= 5\sqrt{2} \end{aligned}" />

## Operations on Three Dimensional Vectors

For addition and subtraction, pair **components on the same axis**. Scalar multiplication multiplies every component by the same number. Also distinguish the result types: a dot product produces a scalar, while a cross product produces a vector.

### Addition and Subtraction of Vectors

Addition and subtraction of three-dimensional vectors are performed by adding or subtracting the **corresponding components**.

If <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b} = (b_x, b_y, b_z)" />, then:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} + \vec{b} = (a_x + b_x, a_y + b_y, a_z + b_z)" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} - \vec{b} = (a_x - b_x, a_y - b_y, a_z - b_z)" />
</MathContainer>

### Scalar Multiplication of Vectors

Multiplying a scalar <InlineMath math="k" /> by a vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> scales its magnitude by <InlineMath math="|k|" />. If <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> is nonzero, the direction stays the same when <InlineMath math="k > 0" /> and **reverses** when <InlineMath math="k < 0" />.

<BlockMath math="k\vec{a} = (k \cdot a_x, k \cdot a_y, k \cdot a_z)" />

### Dot Product

The dot product between two vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> **produces a scalar** defined as:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} &amp;= a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z \\ &amp;= |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta \end{aligned}" />

For this angle formula, both vectors must be nonzero. The symbol <InlineMath math="\theta" /> denotes the angle between their directions.

The dot product has the following properties:

1. <InlineMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a}" /> (commutative)
2. <InlineMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0" /> if and only if <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> are orthogonal. If both are nonzero, their geometric directions are perpendicular
3. <InlineMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} = |\vec{a}|^2" />

### Cross Product

The cross product between two vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> produces a vector whose dot product with each factor is zero:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{a}=(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{b}=0" />

  <BlockMath math="\vec a\times\vec b=\begin{pmatrix}a_yb_z-a_zb_y\\a_zb_x-a_xb_z\\a_xb_y-a_yb_x\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The magnitude of the cross product is:

<BlockMath math="|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta" />

For this angle formula, both vectors must be nonzero. The symbol <InlineMath math="\theta" /> denotes the angle between their directions.

When <InlineMath math="\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\neq\vec{0}" />, its direction is perpendicular to both factors and follows the right-hand rule from <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" />. If either factor is zero or the factors are parallel, the cross product is <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" /> and therefore has no geometric direction.

In the diagram, take <InlineMath math="\vec a=(2,0,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec b=(0,2,0)" />. The vectors are **perpendicular**, so their dot product is zero:

<BlockMath math="\vec a\cdot\vec b=2\cdot0+0\cdot2+0\cdot0=0" />

The cross product gives a different kind of result. Substitute the components of both vectors into the formula:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\vec a\times\vec b&amp;=(0\cdot0-0\cdot2,\;0\cdot0-2\cdot0,\;2\cdot2-0\cdot0)\\&amp;=(0,0,4)\end{aligned}" />

The result points along the positive <InlineMath math="z" />-axis, matching the green arrow. Its magnitude of <InlineMath math="4" /> equals the area of a rectangle with side lengths <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />.


## Applications of Three Dimensional Vectors

In robotics, a displacement vector tells a robot arm **how far to move along each axis**. Suppose the arm tip starts at <InlineMath math="P(1,2,0)" /> and must reach <InlineMath math="Q(3,4,2)" />:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P=(3-1,4-2,2-0)=(2,2,2)" />

The displacement along each axis is <InlineMath math="2" /> units. The straight-line displacement magnitude differs from the sum of the components:

<BlockMath math="|\overrightarrow{PQ}|=\sqrt{2^2+2^2+2^2}=\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3" />

Computer graphics and navigation use the same position and displacement calculations. In physics, components can represent force, velocity, acceleration, or momentum. In mechanical engineering, vectors help analyze structural forces and fluid flow. The meaning and units of the components follow the quantity being modeled.
