## Three Axis Coordinate Space

A three-dimensional vector is described by <Highlight>three coordinate components</Highlight>. These components measure displacement along the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes. A nonzero vector has magnitude and direction, while the zero vector has no geometric direction.

> In a right-handed Cartesian frame, the component order always reads the <InlineMath math="x" />, then <InlineMath math="y" />, then <InlineMath math="z" /> displacement, regardless of how the scene is rotated on screen.

## Notation and Components

The <InlineMath math="z" />-axis stands perpendicular to the <InlineMath math="xy" />-plane. For example, the third component of <InlineMath math="(3,4,2)" /> is <InlineMath math="2" />, which marks the step along the <InlineMath math="z" />-axis even when the camera tilts.

Three-dimensional vectors appear in three standard notations:

1. Arrow notation: <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> or <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" />
2. Component notation: <InlineMath math="(a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> or <InlineMath math="(a_1, a_2, a_3)" />
3. Basis notation: <InlineMath math="a_x\vec{i} + a_y\vec{j} + a_z\vec{k}" />

For the vector in the first diagram, these notations describe the **same displacement**:

<BlockMath math="\vec v=(3,4,2)=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\2\end{pmatrix}=3\vec i+4\vec j+2\vec k" />

Multiplying each **scalar component** by its axis unit vector produces the corresponding vector projection along that axis:

<BlockMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z) = a_x\vec{i} + a_y\vec{j} + a_z\vec{k}" />

Each piece plays a distinct geometric role:

- Along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, <InlineMath math="a_x" /> gives the signed displacement and <InlineMath math="a_x\vec{i}" /> is the directed axis arrow
- Along the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, <InlineMath math="a_y" /> gives the signed displacement and <InlineMath math="a_y\vec{j}" /> is the directed axis arrow
- Along the <InlineMath math="z" />-axis, <InlineMath math="a_z" /> gives the signed displacement and <InlineMath math="a_z\vec{k}" /> is the directed axis arrow
- <InlineMath math="\vec{i}, \vec{j}, \vec{k}" /> are the unit vectors along the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes

## Vector Magnitude

The **magnitude** or length of a three-dimensional vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> extends the Pythagorean theorem to three perpendicular axes:

<BlockMath math="|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2}" />

For example, take <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (3, 4, 5)" /> and square each component before summing:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} |\vec{a}| &amp;= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{9 + 16 + 25} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{50} \\ &amp;= 5\sqrt{2} \end{aligned}" />

Because every square is nonnegative, only the zero vector has magnitude <InlineMath math="0" />.

## Addition and Scaling

For addition and subtraction, pair **components on the same axis**. Scalar multiplication multiplies every component by the same factor, stretching, shrinking, or reversing the arrow without bending it off its line.

### Adding Space Vectors

Addition and subtraction of three-dimensional vectors operate on **corresponding components**. Given <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = (a_x, a_y, a_z)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b} = (b_x, b_y, b_z)" />, combine each coordinate separately:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} + \vec{b} = (a_x + b_x, a_y + b_y, a_z + b_z)" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} - \vec{b} = (a_x - b_x, a_y - b_y, a_z - b_z)" />
</MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="k\vec{a} = (k \cdot a_x, k \cdot a_y, k \cdot a_z)" />

## Dot and Cross Products

Two vector multiplications answer different geometric questions. The **dot product** returns a scalar measuring alignment and projection, while the **cross product** returns a perpendicular vector whose length equals a parallelogram area.

### Dot Product

The dot product between two vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> **produces a scalar** defined by their components or the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> between nonzero directions:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} &amp;= a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z \\ &amp;= |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta \end{aligned}" />

Three algebraic identities follow directly from this definition:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a}" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 \iff \vec{a} \perp \vec{b}" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} = |\vec{a}|^2" />
</MathContainer>

When both vectors are nonzero, a zero dot product means they meet at a right angle. Sweep the angle between <InlineMath math="\vec{u}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> below to see how the signed projection along <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> changes sign across ninety degrees.

### Cross Product

The cross product of <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" /> produces a vector whose dot product with each factor is zero:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{a}=(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{b}=0" />
  <BlockMath math="\vec a\times\vec b=\begin{pmatrix}a_yb_z-a_zb_y\\a_zb_x-a_xb_z\\a_xb_y-a_yb_x\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

For nonzero vectors forming angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />, the cross product magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram they span:

<BlockMath math="|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta" />

Whenever the cross product is nonzero, it points perpendicular to both factors along the **right-hand rule** from <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" /> toward <InlineMath math="\vec{b}" />. If either factor is zero or the factors are parallel, the result is the zero vector <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" />.

## Triple Product and Uses

Combining a dot product with a cross product measures the **volume of a parallelepiped** spanned by three vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{u}" />, <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" />, and <InlineMath math="\vec{w}" />:

<BlockMath math="V = |\vec{w} \cdot (\vec{u} \times \vec{v})|" />

In robotics, a displacement vector tells a robot arm **how far to move along each axis**. Suppose the arm tip starts at <InlineMath math="P(1,2,0)" /> and must reach <InlineMath math="Q(3,4,2)" />:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P=(3-1,4-2,2-0)=(2,2,2)" />

The displacement along each axis is <InlineMath math="2" /> units, while the straight-line travel distance uses the magnitude formula:

<BlockMath math="|\overrightarrow{PQ}|=\sqrt{2^2+2^2+2^2}=\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3" />

Computer graphics and navigation rely on the same displacement and cross product calculations to orient surfaces and cameras. In physics and mechanical engineering, components model force, velocity, torque, and structural loads, with units set by the physical quantity.
