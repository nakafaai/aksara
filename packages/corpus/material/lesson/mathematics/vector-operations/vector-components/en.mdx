## Understanding Vector Components

Every vector in the coordinate plane can be decomposed along the two coordinate axes. In <InlineMath math="\vec{v}=a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}" />, the signed numbers <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are the scalar components. The vectors <InlineMath math="a\mathbf{i}" /> and <InlineMath math="b\mathbf{j}" /> are the corresponding vector components, or orthogonal projections onto the axes.

The scalar components tell us how far and in which signed direction the vector extends along the <InlineMath math="x" />- and <InlineMath math="y" />-axes. Every vector in the plane is a linear combination of the unit vectors <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathbf{j}" />.

If we have a vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" />, then:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{AB} = a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j}" />

where:

- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the horizontal scalar component, and <InlineMath math="a\mathbf{i}" /> is the corresponding vector projection
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the vertical scalar component, and <InlineMath math="b\mathbf{j}" /> is the corresponding vector projection
- <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i}" /> is the unit vector in the direction of the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis
- <InlineMath math="\mathbf{j}" /> is the unit vector in the direction of the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis

### Example of Vector Components

Consider the vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> in the figure. This vector can be written as:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{AB} = 6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}" />

This means that <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> has scalar components <InlineMath math="6" /> and <InlineMath math="8" />, with vector projections <InlineMath math="6\mathbf{i}" /> to the right and <InlineMath math="8\mathbf{j}" /> upward.

## Vector Magnitude from Its Components

When we know the components of a vector, we can calculate the length or magnitude of the vector using the Pythagorean theorem.

The magnitude of vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{AB}|" /> and calculated using the formula:

<BlockMath math="|\overrightarrow{AB}| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}" />

where <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> are the components of the vector.

### Example of Vector Magnitude Calculation

For the vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB} = 6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}" />, its magnitude is:

<BlockMath math="|\overrightarrow{AB}| = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10" />

Therefore, the magnitude of vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> is <InlineMath math="10 \text{ units}" />.

In three-dimensional space, a vector has three components: the x-component, y-component, and z-component. A vector in three-dimensional space can be expressed as:

<BlockMath math="\vec{v} = a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j} + c\mathbf{k}" />

where:

- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="a\mathbf{i}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="b\mathbf{j}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="c" /> is the scalar component along the <InlineMath math="z" />-axis, while <InlineMath math="c\mathbf{k}" /> is its vector projection
- <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathbf{j}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathbf{k}" /> are
  unit vectors in the direction of the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes

The magnitude of a vector in three-dimensional space is calculated using the formula:

<BlockMath math="|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}" />

## Unit Direction Vector

To represent the direction of a nonzero vector, we can use a unit vector. A unit vector has magnitude <InlineMath math="1 \text{ unit}" />. We obtain it by dividing the nonzero vector by its magnitude.

Denote the unit direction vector of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> by <InlineMath math="\mathbf{u}_{AB}" />. It is calculated using:

<BlockMath math="\mathbf{u}_{AB} = \frac{\overrightarrow{AB}}{|\overrightarrow{AB}|}" />

### Example of Unit Direction Vector

For the vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB} = 6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}" /> with magnitude <InlineMath math="10" />, its unit direction vector is:

<BlockMath math="\mathbf{u}_{AB} = \frac{6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}}{10} = \frac{6}{10}\mathbf{i} + \frac{8}{10}\mathbf{j} = 0.6\mathbf{i} + 0.8\mathbf{j}" />

This unit vector indicates the direction of vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> without regard to its magnitude.

## Applications of Vector Components

Vector components have many applications in everyday life, such as:

- Calculating velocity and displacement in physics
- Analyzing forces in mechanics
- Determining the direction and magnitude of resultants in object movement
- Navigation and position determination in coordinate systems

By understanding vector components, we can analyze various problems involving direction and magnitude in mathematics and other applied sciences.
