## Wind Arrows Show Magnitude and Direction

Imagine a weather map with arrows showing the wind at different locations. The direction of each arrow shows **where the wind is blowing**, while its length or thickness can show **how fast the wind is blowing**.

The wind's velocity is a **vector** because it has both **magnitude** and **direction**. Its _magnitude_ is the wind speed, for example <InlineMath math="30 \text{ km/h}" />, and its _direction_ tells us where the wind is blowing.

### Naming the Parts of a Vector

An arrow's initial and terminal points determine its direction and length. The four terms below connect the directed segment with the vector it represents.

1.  **Initial Point**: The starting point of the vector.
2.  **Terminal Point**: The ending point of the vector, marked with an arrowhead.
3.  **Magnitude**: The length of the arrow represents the size of the vector, also called its _vector length_. The magnitude of vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{AB}|" />.
4.  **Direction**: The direction pointed by the arrowhead.

The magnitude is the length of the arrow. For the arrow from <InlineMath math="A(0, 0)" /> to <InlineMath math="B(6, 8)" />, the horizontal and vertical steps 6 and 8 form a right triangle with the arrow as its hypotenuse, so <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{AB}| = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10" />.

### Vector Notation

There are several ways to write vectors:

1.  **Two Capital Letters with an Arrow Above**: Indicating the initial and terminal points. Example: <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" />, meaning a vector from point <InlineMath math="A" /> to point <InlineMath math="B" />.
2.  **One Lowercase Letter with an Arrow Above**: Example: <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />.
3.  **A Bold Lowercase Letter**: Examples: <InlineMath math="\mathbf{a}" /> or <InlineMath math="\mathbf{v}" />.

As drawn, none of the shapes is a **standard representation** of one vector:

1. **Shape 1**: The polygonal path represents a path made of several segments. Its net displacement can still be represented by one arrow from the starting point to the ending point.
2. **Shape 2**: The broken line also represents a path with several segments. One vector arrow would connect its starting point directly to its ending point.
3. **Shape 3**: The line has arrowheads at both ends, so it does not choose one orientation. A single vector must have one initial point and one terminal point.

A standard arrow representation of one vector must have these characteristics:

- It is one straight line segment
- It has one direction indicated by an arrowhead
- It has a clearly identified initial point and terminal point
