## Vector Magnitude and Direction

Every nonzero vector <Highlight>has two defining features</Highlight>: **magnitude** and **direction**. The zero vector is the special case discussed later.

- The **magnitude of vector** <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{CD}" /> is <InlineMath math="4 \text{ cm}" />. Magnitude tells us the vector's _size_. The magnitude of a vector <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> is denoted by <InlineMath math="|\vec{v}|" />. Therefore, <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{CD}| = 4 \text{ cm}" />.
- The **direction of vector** <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{CD}" /> is <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> relative to the horizontal line. Direction distinguishes vectors from scalar quantities, which have magnitude alone. It can be expressed using angles, compass points such as Northeast, or other references.

## Negative or Opposite Vector

For a nonzero vector, its negative or opposite vector has the **same magnitude** but the **opposite direction**.

Imagine Andi walks <InlineMath math="100 \text{ m}" /> at an angle of <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />. Call this displacement <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />. If Andi then walks directly back along the same route, the return displacement is the opposite vector of <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />, written as <InlineMath math="-\vec{A}" />.

- Vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="-\vec{A}" /> have the same magnitude (<InlineMath math="|\vec{A}| = |-\vec{A}|" />).
- The direction of vector <InlineMath math="-\vec{A}" /> is exactly opposite to the direction of vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" />. If <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> points in one direction, <InlineMath math="-\vec{A}" /> points in the opposite direction (<InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> difference).

## Zero Vector

The zero vector is a special vector because it has **zero magnitude**. Due to its zero length, this vector **does not have a specific direction**.

The zero vector can be visualized as a single point, where the initial point and terminal point coincide. The zero vector is usually denoted by <InlineMath math="\vec{0}" />.

**Example**:

If Andi walks <InlineMath math="100 \text{ m}" /> east, then walks back <InlineMath math="100 \text{ m}" /> west, Andi's total displacement is zero. This total displacement can be represented as the zero vector (<InlineMath math="\vec{0}" />).

<BlockMath math="\Delta x=100\,\text{m}+(-100\,\text{m})=0\,\text{m}" />

## Equivalent or Equal Vectors

Two vectors are **equivalent** or **equal** when their corresponding components are equal, even if their starting points differ. For nonzero vectors, this is equivalent to having the same **magnitude and direction**. The component definition also covers the zero vector, whose direction is undefined.

Consider the graph below showing three equivalent vectors: <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{CD}" />, <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{EF}" />, and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{KL}" />.

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{CD} = \overrightarrow{EF} = \overrightarrow{KL}" />

Is vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> the opposite vector of <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />?

**Answer**:

Vector <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> is **not** the opposite vector of <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />. Check the two required conditions:

1.  **They must have the same magnitude**: The diagram gives <InlineMath math="\vec{A} = (-1, -4)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{B} = (4, 1)" />, so <InlineMath math="|\vec{A}| = |\vec{B}| = \sqrt{17}" />. This condition is satisfied.
2.  **Their directions must be exactly opposite (<InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> apart)**: Negating both components gives <InlineMath math="-\vec{A} = (1, 4)" />. Vector <InlineMath math="\vec{B} = (4, 1)" /> has different component positions, so this condition is not satisfied.

Because the second condition fails, <InlineMath math="\vec{A}" /> is not the opposite vector of <InlineMath math="\vec{B}" />.
