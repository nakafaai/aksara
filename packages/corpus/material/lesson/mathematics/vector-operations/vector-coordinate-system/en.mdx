## Cartesian Coordinate System

To specify the position of an object, such as an aircraft in flight, we first need a **frame of reference** or **coordinate system**. One widely used system is the **Cartesian coordinate system**, named after René Descartes.

The Cartesian Coordinate System uses perpendicular axes to determine the location of a point.

### A two dimensional plane

In **two dimensions**, we use the <InlineMath math="x" /> axis (horizontal) and <InlineMath math="y" /> axis (vertical). A point's position is expressed as an ordered pair <InlineMath math="(x, y)" />, for example, point <InlineMath math="P(3, 4)" />.

This means that point <InlineMath math="P" /> is located <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" /> to the right (positive <InlineMath math="x" /> axis) and <InlineMath math="4 \text{ units}" /> upward (positive <InlineMath math="y" /> axis) from the origin <InlineMath math="O(0, 0)" />.

### Three dimensional space

In **three dimensions**, we add a <InlineMath math="z" /> axis that is perpendicular to the <InlineMath math="xy" /> plane. The coordinates <InlineMath math="(x, y, z)" /> give a point's position along all three axes. For example, they can specify an aircraft's position in three-dimensional space.

In one common spatial model, <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> can represent length, width, and height. The chosen model gives each axis its meaning.

## Difference Between Vectors and Coordinates

Coordinates identify the _position_ of a point relative to a coordinate system. A **nonzero vector**, by contrast, has both **magnitude** and **direction**. Imagine a fly traveling from point <InlineMath math="P(3, 4)" /> to point <InlineMath math="Q(-2, -8)" />. Its displacement can be represented by a vector.

### Position Vector

A vector **starting from the origin** <InlineMath math="O(0, 0)" /> to a point <InlineMath math="P(x, y)" />. The position vector of <InlineMath math="P" /> is written as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OP}" />.

Example: <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OP} = (3, 4)" />.

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{OP}=P-O=(3-0,4-0)=(3,4)" />

### Displacement Vector

A vector connecting any two points, for example, from point <InlineMath math="P(x_P, y_P)" /> to point <InlineMath math="Q(x_Q, y_Q)" />. This vector is written as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" /> and does not have to start at the origin.

Example: displacement vectors from <InlineMath math="P(3, 4)" /> to <InlineMath math="Q(-2, -8)" /> or from <InlineMath math="Q(-2, -8)" /> to <InlineMath math="R(1, 3)" />.

## Vector Notation

The **horizontal or vertical arrangement** distinguishes row and column vectors. Bracket shape does not determine the difference. We can also write components as multiples of unit vectors.

**Row Vector**: Components are arranged in one row.

Example: <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OP} = (3, 4)" />.

**Column Vector**: Components are arranged in one column.

For example:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{OP} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}" />

**Unit Vector Notation for Three Dimensions**: Using a combination of unit vectors <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathbf{j}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathbf{k}" /> which are aligned with the <InlineMath math="x" />, <InlineMath math="y" />, and <InlineMath math="z" /> axes.

Example: a vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> can be written as <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j} - 4\mathbf{k}" />, meaning vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" /> has a component of <InlineMath math="1" /> in the <InlineMath math="x" /> direction, <InlineMath math="-3" /> in the <InlineMath math="y" /> direction, and <InlineMath math="-4" /> in the <InlineMath math="z" /> direction.

## Coordinate Systems in Navigation

Imagine a map application on your phone. It uses a coordinate system to:

1.  Determine your current location.
2.  Find your destination.
3.  Relate your position to roads and possible routes.
4.  Provide direction instructions (turn left, turn right, go straight).

Coordinates provide the locations, while map data and routing algorithms determine a suitable route. Vectors then help describe <Highlight>changes in position and direction</Highlight>. The same distinction matters when an aircraft changes altitude or direction: coordinates describe where it is, and a displacement vector describes how its position changes.
