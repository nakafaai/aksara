## How a Real Multiplier Changes a Vector

Scalar multiplication gives a new vector. Its magnitude is the original magnitude multiplied by **the scalar's absolute value**. For a nonzero original vector, the sign determines whether the direction stays the same or reverses.

If <InlineMath math="k" /> is a real number (scalar) and <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> is a vector, then the scalar multiplication of a vector is denoted as <InlineMath math="k \cdot \vec{v}" />.

## Properties of Scalar Multiplication of Vectors

Scalar multiplication changes a vector according to the following rules:

1. If <InlineMath math="k > 0" /> and the original vector is nonzero, the resulting vector has the same direction.
2. If <InlineMath math="k < 0" /> and the original vector is nonzero, the resulting vector points in the opposite direction.
3. If <InlineMath math="k = 0" />, then the resulting vector is a zero vector.
4. The resulting magnitude is the original magnitude multiplied by the factor <InlineMath math="|k|" />.

## Representation of Scalar Multiplication of Vectors

The **absolute value of the scalar** determines the resulting length, while its sign determines whether the direction stays the same or reverses. Multiplication by zero produces the zero vector, which has no direction.

### Magnitude and Direction of a Scaled Vector

Geometrically, scalar multiplication changes a vector's magnitude by the factor <InlineMath math="|k|" />. For a nonzero vector, its direction depends on the **sign** of <InlineMath math="k" />:

- If <InlineMath math="k > 0" />, the direction of the vector remains unchanged
- If <InlineMath math="k < 0" />, the direction of the vector is opposite to the original vector

In both cases, and also when <InlineMath math="k=0" />, the magnitude satisfies:

<BlockMath math="|k \cdot \vec{v}| = |k| \cdot |\vec{v}|" />

The absolute value of the factor sets the length. <InlineMath math="\vec v = (4, 2)" /> has length <InlineMath math="2\sqrt5" />. Both <InlineMath math="\tfrac12\vec v = (2, 1)" /> and <InlineMath math="-\tfrac12\vec v = (-2, -1)" /> have length <InlineMath math="\tfrac12 \cdot 2\sqrt5 = \sqrt5" />, and the negative sign only reverses the direction.

### Scaling a Vector by Components

Apply the **same factor to every component**. Keep the component order aligned with the coordinate axes.

If <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (v_1, v_2, v_3)" /> is a vector in <InlineMath math="3" />-dimensional space, then:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} k \cdot \vec{v} &amp;= k(v_1, v_2, v_3) \\ &amp;= (k \cdot v_1, k \cdot v_2, k \cdot v_3) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

In unit vector notation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} k \cdot \vec{v} &amp;= k(v_1\vec{i} + v_2\vec{j} + v_3\vec{k}) \\ &amp;= k \cdot v_1\vec{i} + k \cdot v_2\vec{j} + k \cdot v_3\vec{k} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

## Multiplying Vectors by Positive and Negative Scalars

A factor greater than one stretches the vector, a factor between zero and one shrinks it, and a <Highlight>negative factor also reverses the direction</Highlight>. These direction statements apply to a **nonzero original vector**. The zero vector remains zero after multiplication by any scalar and has no defined direction.

### Multiplying by a Positive Scalar

Given the vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = 2\vec{i} + 3\vec{j} + 5\vec{k}" />. Determine the result of multiplication <InlineMath math="2\vec{a}" />.

**Solution**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 2\vec{a} &amp;= 2(2\vec{i} + 3\vec{j} + 5\vec{k}) \\ &amp;= 4\vec{i} + 6\vec{j} + 10\vec{k} \end{aligned}" />

### Multiplying by a Negative Scalar

Given the vector <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (4, -2, 3)" />. Determine the result of <InlineMath math="-3\vec{v}" />.

**Solution**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} -3\vec{v} &amp;= -3(4, -2, 3) \\ &amp;= (-12, 6, -9) \end{aligned}" />

The resulting vector points in the opposite direction because the scalar is negative.

In space the same rule holds. <InlineMath math="-3\vec{v} = (-12, 6, -9)" /> points exactly opposite to <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (4, -2, 3)" /> and is 3 times as long.

## Applications of Scalar Multiplication of Vectors

Scalar multiplication of vectors has many applications in physics and mathematics, such as:

1. **Force and Acceleration**: If an object with mass <InlineMath math="m" /> experiences acceleration <InlineMath math="\vec{a}" />, then the force acting on the object is <InlineMath math="\vec{F} = m\vec{a}" />.

2. **Velocity**: If an object moves with constant velocity <InlineMath math="\vec{v}" /> for a time <InlineMath math="t" />, then its displacement is <InlineMath math="\vec{s} = t\vec{v}" />.

3. **Scaling in Computer Graphics**: Multiplying every position vector by <InlineMath math="k" /> scales an object about the coordinate origin. To scale about another center <InlineMath math="\vec{c}" />, each point <InlineMath math="\vec{p}" /> must be translated, scaled, and translated back according to <InlineMath math="\vec{p}'=\vec{c}+k(\vec{p}-\vec{c})" />.

## Practice Problems

Each problem multiplies a vector by one or two scalars. Multiply **every component by the same factor**, then combine the results where two scalars appear.

1. Given the vector <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = 3\vec{i} - 4\vec{j} + 2\vec{k}" />. Determine the result of <InlineMath math="-2\vec{a}" />.

2. Vectors <InlineMath math="\vec{u} = (2, -5, 1)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (-4, 3, 6)" />. Determine the vector <InlineMath math="2\vec{u} - 3\vec{v}" />.

3. Given <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{BR}| = 3.4 \text{ cm}" />. If <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} = 0.65 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{UR} = 0.35 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" />, prove that all three vectors have the same direction.

4. Vector <InlineMath math="\vec{p}" /> has a length of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" /> and vector <InlineMath math="\vec{q} = 3\vec{p}" />. Determine the length of vector <InlineMath math="\vec{q}" />.

5. Given points <InlineMath math="A(2, 3, -1)" />, <InlineMath math="B(5, -2, 4)" />, and <InlineMath math="C" /> lies on the line passing through <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> such that <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AC} = 2\overrightarrow{AB}" />. Determine the coordinates of point <InlineMath math="C" />.

### Answer Key

Each solution multiplies **every component by the same factor**, then combines components on matching axes. A negative factor times a negative component produces a positive component. To recover a point, add its displacement vector to the starting position.

**Problem 1**. The given vector is <InlineMath math="\vec{a} = 3\vec{i} - 4\vec{j} + 2\vec{k}" />. To obtain <InlineMath math="-2\vec{a}" />, multiply every component by the same negative scalar.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} -2\vec{a} &amp;= -2(3\vec{i} - 4\vec{j} + 2\vec{k}) \\ &amp;= -6\vec{i} + 8\vec{j} - 4\vec{k} \end{aligned}" />

The result of <InlineMath math="-2\vec{a}" /> is <InlineMath math="-6\vec{i} + 8\vec{j} - 4\vec{k}" />.

**Problem 2**. The given vectors are <InlineMath math="\vec{u} = (2, -5, 1)" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (-4, 3, 6)" />. To obtain <InlineMath math="2\vec{u} - 3\vec{v}" />, calculate both scalar multiples, then subtract **matching components**.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 2\vec{u} &amp;= 2(2, -5, 1) = (4, -10, 2) \\ 3\vec{v} &amp;= 3(-4, 3, 6) = (-12, 9, 18) \\ 2\vec{u} - 3\vec{v} &amp;= (4, -10, 2) - (-12, 9, 18) \\ &amp;= (4 - (-12), -10 - 9, 2 - 18) \\ &amp;= (4 + 12, -10 - 9, 2 - 18) \\ &amp;= (16, -19, -16) \end{aligned}" />

The vector <InlineMath math="2\vec{u} - 3\vec{v}" /> is <InlineMath math="(16, -19, -16)" /> or <InlineMath math="16\vec{i} - 19\vec{j} - 16\vec{k}" />.

**Problem 3**. Given <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{BR}| = 3.4 \text{ cm}" />. If <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} = 0.65 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{UR} = 0.35 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" />, prove that all three vectors have the same direction.

To prove that all three vectors have the same direction, we need to show that they are **positive scalar multiples** of the same vector.

We know:

- <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} = 0.65 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" />
- <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{UR} = 0.35 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" />

Check if <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} + \overrightarrow{UR} = \overrightarrow{BR}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{BU} + \overrightarrow{UR} &amp;= 0.65 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR} + 0.35 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR} \\ &amp;= (0.65 + 0.35) \cdot \overrightarrow{BR} \\ &amp;= 1 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR} \\ &amp;= \overrightarrow{BR} \end{aligned}" />

The equality <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} + \overrightarrow{UR} = \overrightarrow{BR}" /> is the vector addition law for the collinear points <InlineMath math="B" />, <InlineMath math="U" />, and <InlineMath math="R" />.

Since <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BU} = 0.65 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" /> and <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{UR} = 0.35 \cdot \overrightarrow{BR}" />, where the scalar factors are positive (<InlineMath math="0.65" /> and <InlineMath math="0.35" />), all three vectors have the same direction. Positive scalar factors mean that these vectors point in the same direction as the reference vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{BR}" />.

**Problem 4**. Vector <InlineMath math="\vec{p}" /> has a length of <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" /> and vector <InlineMath math="\vec{q} = 3\vec{p}" />. Find the length of <InlineMath math="\vec{q}" /> by multiplying the original length by the **absolute value of the scalar**.

Given <InlineMath math="|\vec{p}| = 5 \text{ units}" /> and <InlineMath math="\vec{q} = 3\vec{p}" />.

To determine the length of vector <InlineMath math="\vec{q}" />, we use the property of scalar multiplication:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} |\vec{q}| &amp;= |3\vec{p}| \\ &amp;= |3| \cdot |\vec{p}| \\ &amp;= 3 \cdot 5 \\ &amp;= 15 \end{aligned}" />

The length of vector <InlineMath math="\vec{q}" /> is <InlineMath math="15 \text{ units}" />.

**Problem 5**. Given points <InlineMath math="A(2, 3, -1)" />, <InlineMath math="B(5, -2, 4)" />, and <InlineMath math="C" /> lies on the line passing through <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> such that <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AC} = 2\overrightarrow{AB}" />. Determine the coordinates of point <InlineMath math="C" />.

To find the **displacement from the starting point**, calculate the vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AB}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{AB} &amp;= B - A \\ &amp;= (5, -2, 4) - (2, 3, -1) \\ &amp;= (5-2, -2-3, 4-(-1)) \\ &amp;= (3, -5, 5) \end{aligned}" />

Then, we use the relationship <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AC} = 2\overrightarrow{AB}" /> to determine the vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{AC}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{AC} &amp;= 2\overrightarrow{AB} \\ &amp;= 2(3, -5, 5) \\ &amp;= (6, -10, 10) \end{aligned}" />

Next, we determine the coordinates of point <InlineMath math="C" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{AC} = C - A" />
  <BlockMath math="(6, -10, 10) = C - (2, 3, -1)" />
  <BlockMath math="C = (6, -10, 10) + (2, 3, -1)" />
  <BlockMath math="C = (6+2, -10+3, 10+(-1))" />
  <BlockMath math="C = (8, -7, 9)" />
</MathContainer>

The coordinates of point <InlineMath math="C" /> are <InlineMath math="C(8, -7, 9)" />.
