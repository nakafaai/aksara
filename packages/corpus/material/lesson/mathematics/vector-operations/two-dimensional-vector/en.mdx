## Describing a Vector with Horizontal and Vertical Steps

In the Cartesian coordinate system, each point on a plane can be represented by a pair of numbers <InlineMath math="(x, y)" />, where <InlineMath math="x" /> is the horizontal position and <InlineMath math="y" /> is the vertical position. The origin point is <InlineMath math="O(0, 0)" />.

If we draw a directed line segment from the origin <InlineMath math="O" /> to another point, for example <InlineMath math="Q(x, y)" />, we obtain a **vector**. This vector is written as <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ}" />. Its **magnitude** is the segment's length, and its **direction** runs from the initial point toward the arrowhead.

Two **unit vectors** describe the coordinate directions. Each has a length of <InlineMath math="1 \text{ unit}" />.

- <InlineMath math="\mathbf{i}" /> is the unit vector along the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis (horizontal).
- <InlineMath math="\mathbf{j}" /> is the unit vector along the positive <InlineMath math="y" />-axis (vertical).

Vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ}" /> can be expressed as a combination of horizontal movement of <InlineMath math="x" /> and vertical movement of <InlineMath math="y" />. In unit vector form, we write:

<BlockMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j}" />

## Vector Components and Magnitude

The values <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> in vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j}" /> are called the vector **components**.

- <InlineMath math="x" /> is the **horizontal scalar component**. The corresponding vector projection onto the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis is <InlineMath math="x\mathbf{i}" />.
- <InlineMath math="y" /> is the **vertical scalar component**. The corresponding vector projection onto the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis is <InlineMath math="y\mathbf{j}" />.

A vector with these two components is called a **two-dimensional vector**.

The **length** or **magnitude** of vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ}" />, written as <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{OQ}|" />, is the distance from the origin <InlineMath math="O" /> to the endpoint <InlineMath math="Q" />. If <InlineMath math="Q(x,y)" /> is the endpoint and <InlineMath math="R(x,0)" /> is the projection of <InlineMath math="Q" /> onto the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, the right triangle <InlineMath math="ORQ" /> gives its magnitude <Highlight>through the Pythagorean theorem</Highlight>:

<BlockMath math="|\overrightarrow{OQ}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}" />

Components can be negative. For <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OR} = -3\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j}" /> the horizontal component points left, along <InlineMath math="-\mathbf{i}" />, and the vertical one points up. The magnitude uses squares, so the signs do not change it: <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{OR}| = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{13}" />.

### A unit vector in the same direction

To obtain **unit length**, divide each component by the vector magnitude. The direction stays the same because the divisor is positive:

<BlockMath math="\hat{u}=\frac{\overrightarrow{OQ}}{|\overrightarrow{OQ}|}=\frac{4\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}}{5}=\frac45\mathbf{i}+\frac35\mathbf{j}" />

The new components have the same ratio as the original components. Check the unit vector magnitude with Pythagoras:

<BlockMath math="|\hat{u}|=\sqrt{\left(\frac45\right)^2+\left(\frac35\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{16}{25}+\frac9{25}}=\sqrt1=1" />

Dividing by <InlineMath math="5" /> shrinks <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{OQ}" /> to length <InlineMath math="1" /> without turning it. The unit vector <InlineMath math="\hat{u} = \tfrac45\mathbf{i} + \tfrac35\mathbf{j}" /> ends on the circle of radius <InlineMath math="1" />, on the same ray from <InlineMath math="O" /> as <InlineMath math="Q(4, 3)" />.
