## Preserving Direction with a Vector of Length One

A unit vector has length or magnitude <InlineMath math="1 \text{ unit}" />. It represents direction without carrying the original magnitude. From any nonzero vector, we obtain a unit vector in the same direction by <Highlight>dividing the vector by its length</Highlight>.

## Unit Vector Formula and Direction

Consider vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" /> in a Cartesian coordinate system. The unit vector of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" /> is defined as vector <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" /> divided by its length.

<BlockMath math="\mathbf{u}_{PQ} = \frac{\overrightarrow{PQ}}{|\overrightarrow{PQ}|}" />

The denominator <InlineMath math="|\overrightarrow{PQ}|" /> is the magnitude of <InlineMath math="\overrightarrow{PQ}" /> and must be **greater than zero**. The zero vector has no corresponding unit vector because division by zero is undefined.

A unit vector always points in the same direction as the original vector but has its length normalized to <InlineMath math="1 \text{ unit}" />.

## Calculating a Unit Vector

Dividing a nonzero vector by its length preserves **its direction** and gives length one. A vector initially longer than one becomes shorter. A vector initially shorter than one is enlarged.

1. Identify the original vector
2. Calculate the length of the vector
3. Divide the vector by its length

### Normalizing a Three Dimensional Vector

Take the vector <InlineMath math="\vec{v} = (3, 6, 4)" />.

First, calculate its magnitude:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} |\vec{v}| &amp;= \sqrt{3^2 + 6^2 + 4^2} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{9 + 36 + 16} \\ &amp;= \sqrt{61} \end{aligned}" />

Then **divide the vector by that magnitude**:

<BlockMath math="\hat{v} = \frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} = \frac{(3, 6, 4)}{\sqrt{61}}" />

The unit vector is therefore:

<BlockMath math="\hat{v} = \left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{61}}, \frac{6}{\sqrt{61}}, \frac{4}{\sqrt{61}}\right)" />

## Length and Components of a Unit Vector

The defining feature of a unit vector is its length of one, which forces a relation between its components. **Squaring the components and adding them** gives one, and that equation governs the step from any nonzero vector to its unit vector.

### Length of a Unit Vector

A unit vector has length <InlineMath math="1" />. For this example, add the squares of the three coordinates first. A length is nonnegative, so take the **positive square root** at the end.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;|\hat{v}|^2 \\ &amp;=\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{61}}\right)^2+\left(\frac{6}{\sqrt{61}}\right)^2 \\ &amp;\quad+\left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{61}}\right)^2 \\ &amp;=\frac{9+36+16}{61} \\ &amp;=\frac{61}{61} \\ &amp;=1 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="|\hat{v}|=\sqrt{1}=1" />
</MathContainer>

### Unit Vectors on Coordinate Axes

In a three-dimensional coordinate system, there are three standard unit vectors that point along the **positive coordinate axes**:

- <InlineMath math="\hat{\imath}" /> is the unit vector in the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis direction
- <InlineMath math="\hat{\jmath}" /> is the unit vector in the positive <InlineMath math="y" />-axis direction
- <InlineMath math="\hat{k}" /> is the unit vector in the positive <InlineMath math="z" />-axis direction

Every vector in <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^3" /> can be expressed as a linear combination of these three unit vectors.

## Recording Direction with a Unit Vector

A unit vector **separates direction from magnitude**, so it can describe a direction on its own. Two vectors that point the same way share one unit vector, which makes the unit vector a compact way to record a direction.

### Indicating Direction

A unit vector records direction with magnitude <InlineMath math="1" />. In physics, it can specify the direction of a force, velocity, or acceleration **separately from that quantity's magnitude**.

### Physics Calculations

Suppose a force has a <InlineMath math="3\text{ N}" /> component along the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and a <InlineMath math="-4\text{ N}" /> component along the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. The negative sign indicates the direction opposite the positive axis. The **force magnitude** follows from the two components:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\vec F=(3\hat\imath-4\hat\jmath)\text{ N}" />
  <BlockMath math="|\vec F|=\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2}\text{ N}=5\text{ N}" />
  <BlockMath math="\hat F=\frac{\vec F}{|\vec F|}=\frac35\hat\imath-\frac45\hat\jmath" />
</MathContainer>

The unit vector has no newton unit because the numerator and denominator units cancel. Multiplying the direction by the force magnitude restores the original components:

<BlockMath math="5\text{ N}\left(\frac35\hat\imath-\frac45\hat\jmath\right)=(3\hat\imath-4\hat\jmath)\text{ N}" />
