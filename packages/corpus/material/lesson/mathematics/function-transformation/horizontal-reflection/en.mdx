## Flipping a Graph Across the y-Axis

A horizontal reflection flips the graph of a function across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. To get it, replace every <InlineMath math="x" /> in <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="-x" />, which gives <InlineMath math="y=f(-x)" />. Each point <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)" /> on the graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-x_0,y_0)" />, so **the height stays the same and only the side changes**.

In the 3D view, you can turn the coordinate plane half a turn about the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis and watch the curve <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{x}" /> land exactly where the curve <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{-x}" /> lies.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="y=f(x)\;\longrightarrow\;y=f(-x)" />
  <BlockMath math="(x_0,\,y_0)\;\longmapsto\;(-x_0,\,y_0)" />
</MathContainer>

Points to the right of the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis move to the left side at the same distance from the axis, points on the left move to the right side, and **points on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis stay where they are**. It is called a horizontal reflection because every point moves sideways, even though the mirror itself is the vertical <InlineMath math="y" />-axis.

Try one point. The point <InlineMath math="(4,2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{x}" /> because <InlineMath math="\sqrt{4}=2" />. Its image, <InlineMath math="(-4,2)" />, lies on <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{-x}" /> because <InlineMath math="\sqrt{-(-4)}=\sqrt{4}=2" />. This point rule is the same one used for [reflecting a point over the vertical axis](/en/subjects/mathematics/geometric-transformation/reflection-over-y-axis). The difference is that here you reflect every point of a graph at once and write the result as a new equation.

## A Half Turn of the Plane About the y-Axis

In three dimensions, the coordinate plane is the plane <InlineMath math="z=0" />, and the <InlineMath math="z" />-axis points toward you. If you turn this plane through <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> with the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis as the axis of rotation, every point travels along a horizontal semicircle centered on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. The circle is horizontal, so **the height of a point never changes**.

A point <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0,0)" /> with <InlineMath math="x_0>0" /> sits behind the original plane after a quarter turn and reaches the other side of the axis, at the same distance, after a half turn:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} (x_0,\,y_0,\,0) &\xrightarrow{\;90^\circ\;} (0,\,y_0,\,-x_0) \\ &\xrightarrow{\;90^\circ\;} (-x_0,\,y_0,\,0) \end{aligned}" />

A point with <InlineMath math="x_0<0" /> turns the same way but passes in front of the plane, because <InlineMath math="-x_0>0" />. So for every point, the end result is exactly the rule for reflecting across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. The 3D view draws <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{x}" />, its image <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{-x}" />, and the semicircular paths of three points on <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{x}" />.

## Replacing Every Input With Its Opposite

The equation <InlineMath math="g(x)=f(-x)" /> tells you how to compute the height of the new graph. To find <InlineMath math="g(3)" />, you evaluate <InlineMath math="f(-3)" />. So **the height of <InlineMath math="g" /> at <InlineMath math="x=3" /> equals the height of <InlineMath math="f" /> at <InlineMath math="x=-3" />**. In general, the height that <InlineMath math="f" /> has at <InlineMath math="x_0" /> shows up on <InlineMath math="g" /> at <InlineMath math="-x_0" />:

<BlockMath math="g(-x_0)=f\big(-(-x_0)\big)=f(x_0)" />

For example, reflect <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2+3x+2" /> across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Replace every <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="(-x)" />, including the <InlineMath math="x" /> in the term <InlineMath math="3x" />. The parentheses make sure the minus sign gets squared too:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(x) &= f(-x) \\ &= (-x)^2+3(-x)+2 \\ &= x^2-3x+2 \end{aligned}" />

Check the result with features of the graph. The zeros, the vertex, and the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept must follow the rule <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)\mapsto(-x_0,y_0)" />:

| Feature | <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2+3x+2" /> | <InlineMath math="g(x)=x^2-3x+2" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zeros | <InlineMath math="x=-2" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-1" /> | <InlineMath math="x=1" /> and <InlineMath math="x=2" /> |
| Vertex | <InlineMath math="\left(-\tfrac{3}{2},-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)" /> | <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{3}{2},-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept | <InlineMath math="(0,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(0,2)" /> |

Every <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate changes sign, while every height stays the same. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept does not move because it lies right on the mirror. You can also check one point directly: <InlineMath math="g(1)=1-3+2=0" />, which equals <InlineMath math="f(-1)=1-3+2=0" />.

## Growth That Turns Into Decay

The function <InlineMath math="g(x)=3^x+1" /> increases from left to right, because each time <InlineMath math="x" /> grows by <InlineMath math="1" />, the term <InlineMath math="3^x" /> triples. Reflect <InlineMath math="g" /> across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, then rewrite <InlineMath math="3^{-x}" /> as a power of <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{3}" />, the reciprocal of the base <InlineMath math="3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} G(x) &= g(-x) = 3^{-x}+1 \\ &= \left(3^{-1}\right)^{x}+1 = \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{x}+1 \end{aligned}" />

The base <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{3}" /> lies between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />, so <InlineMath math="G" /> decreases from left to right. The function that used to grow now shows **decay**: each time <InlineMath math="x" /> grows by <InlineMath math="1" />, the term <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^x" /> shrinks to a third of its value, just like the models in [exponential decay](/en/subjects/mathematics/exponential-logarithm/exponential-decay).

A reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis **leaves the horizontal asymptote and the range unchanged**, because both depend only on heights: the asymptote stays <InlineMath math="y=1" /> and the range stays <InlineMath math="(1,\infty)" />. The domain also stays all real numbers, because that domain is symmetric about <InlineMath math="0" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept stays <InlineMath math="(0,2)" /> because <InlineMath math="g(0)=3^0+1=2" /> and that point lies on the mirror. Check two pairs of points: <InlineMath math="G(1)=\tfrac{1}{3}+1=\tfrac{4}{3}=g(-1)" /> and <InlineMath math="G(-1)=3+1=4=g(1)" />.

## Functions That Stay the Same When Reflected

Some graphs are already symmetric about the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, so their mirror image lands exactly on themselves. This happens when <InlineMath math="f(-x)=f(x)" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" /> in the domain of <InlineMath math="f" />, and such a function is called an **even function**. Examples are <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2" />, because <InlineMath math="(-x)^2=x^2" />, and <InlineMath math="f(x)=|x|" />, because <InlineMath math="|-x|=|x|" />. The condition also needs a symmetric domain: whenever <InlineMath math="x" /> is in the domain, <InlineMath math="-x" /> must be there too.

Other functions satisfy <InlineMath math="f(-x)=-f(x)" /> and are called **odd functions**. For an odd function, reflecting across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis gives the same graph as multiplying the whole function by <InlineMath math="-1" />. Take <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^3-x" /> as an example:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(-x) &= (-x)^3-(-x) = -x^3+x \\ &= -\left(x^3-x\right) = -f(x) \end{aligned}" />

The graph of an odd function also stays the same under a <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> [rotation](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/rotation) about the origin. That rotation sends <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-x_0,-y_0)" />, and the point <InlineMath math="(-x_0,-f(x_0))" /> really is on the graph because <InlineMath math="-f(x_0)=f(-x_0)" />.

| Function | <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> | Type | Graph after the reflection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2" /> | even | the same as the original graph |
| <InlineMath math="\lvert x\rvert" /> | <InlineMath math="\lvert x\rvert" /> | even | the same as the original graph |
| <InlineMath math="x^3-x" /> | <InlineMath math="-x^3+x" /> | odd | the same as the graph of <InlineMath math="y=-f(x)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="x^2+x" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2-x" /> | neither | a new graph |

The function <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2+x" /> is neither even nor odd. One pair of points is enough to show it: <InlineMath math="f(1)=2" /> while <InlineMath math="f(-1)=0" />, and <InlineMath math="0" /> equals neither <InlineMath math="2" /> nor <InlineMath math="-2" />.

## The Domain Moves to the Other Side

The domain holds the values of <InlineMath math="x" /> you are allowed to put in. The graph of <InlineMath math="y=f(-x)" /> is defined at <InlineMath math="x" /> only when <InlineMath math="-x" /> lies in the domain of <InlineMath math="f" />. Suppose the domain of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="[1,5]" />. The condition becomes <InlineMath math="1\le -x\le 5" />, and multiplying all three parts by <InlineMath math="-1" /> reverses both inequality signs:

<BlockMath math="1\le -x\le 5\iff -5\le x\le -1" />

So the new domain is <InlineMath math="[-5,-1]" />, and **both endpoints change sign and swap places**. The new left endpoint comes from the old right endpoint.

The same thing happens with a root function. The domain of <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{x}" /> is <InlineMath math="[0,\infty)" /> because the number under the root of a [square root function](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/square-root-function) cannot be negative. For <InlineMath math="y=\sqrt{-x}" />, the condition is <InlineMath math="-x\ge 0" />, that is <InlineMath math="x\le 0" />, so the domain is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,0]" />.

| Part of the graph | On <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" /> | On <InlineMath math="y=f(-x)" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point | <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(-x_0,y_0)" /> |
| Domain | <InlineMath math="[c,d]" /> | <InlineMath math="[-d,-c]" /> |
| Range | all heights of the graph | unchanged |
| <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept | <InlineMath math="(0,f(0))" /> | still <InlineMath math="(0,f(0))" /> |
| Zero | <InlineMath math="x=r" /> | <InlineMath math="x=-r" /> |
| Direction | increasing on <InlineMath math="[c,d]" /> | decreasing on <InlineMath math="[-d,-c]" /> |
| Horizontal asymptote | <InlineMath math="y=q" /> | still <InlineMath math="y=q" /> |
| Vertical asymptote | <InlineMath math="x=p" /> | <InlineMath math="x=-p" /> |

Replacing <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="-x" /> is the same as using the input multiplier <InlineMath math="b=-1" /> in the form <InlineMath math="y=f(bx)" /> from [horizontal dilation](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/horizontal-dilation). Every negative multiplier adds a reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. With <InlineMath math="b=-1" />, the width of the graph stays the same, so only the reflection is left. Another multiplier such as <InlineMath math="b=-2" /> in <InlineMath math="f(-2x)" /> reflects the graph and changes its width at the same time.

## Putting the Minus Sign in the Wrong Place

In <InlineMath math="f(-x)" />, the minus sign acts on the input only, but it must reach **every <InlineMath math="x" /> in the formula**. The first three mistakes in the table break that rule, and the last two come from reading the result:

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> | A minus sign outside flips the heights, so the graph is reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis | The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept must stay <InlineMath math="(0,f(0))" /> |
| Writing <InlineMath math="-x^2" /> for <InlineMath math="(-x)^2" /> | Without parentheses, only <InlineMath math="x" /> is squared and the result is then negated | Test <InlineMath math="x=1" />: <InlineMath math="(-1)^2=1" />, but <InlineMath math="-1^2=-1" /> |
| Replacing only some of the <InlineMath math="x" /> | In <InlineMath math="x^2+3x+2" />, replacing only the first <InlineMath math="x" /> leaves the formula unchanged | Test one point: <InlineMath math="g(1)" /> must equal <InlineMath math="f(-1)" /> |
| Not swapping the domain endpoints | Writing <InlineMath math="[-c,-d]" /> puts the larger number on the left | The left endpoint of an interval is always the smaller number |
| Expecting an even function to change | For <InlineMath math="x^2" /> and <InlineMath math="\lvert x\rvert" />, <InlineMath math="f(-x)=f(x)" /> | Compute <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> before you sketch |

The first mistake changes the kind of reflection. The formula <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> flips the height of every point and gives a [reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-reflection). The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept tells the two apart: for <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> it stays put, while for <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> its height changes sign, unless <InlineMath math="f(0)=0" />.

## Exercises

Problems 1 to 3 each ask for one feature of the reflected graph: its zeros, its domain, and its vertex. In problem 4 you decide whether a cubic graph changes at all. In each problem, **replace every <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="-x" />** before you simplify, then check your answer with one point.

1. Find the equation of the graph of <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2-6x+5" /> after it is reflected across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, then find its zeros.

2. The graph of <InlineMath math="g(x)=\sqrt{x-2}" /> is reflected across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Find the equation of the resulting graph and its domain.

3. Reflect <InlineMath math="h(x)=\lvert x+2\rvert" /> across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Write the result in its simplest form and find its vertex.

4. Does the graph of <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^3-x" /> change when it is reflected across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis? Compare the result with the graph of <InlineMath math="y=-f(x)" />.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. This quadratic is reflected across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, so replace every <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="(-x)" />, including in the term <InlineMath math="-6x" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} F(x) &= f(-x) \\ &= (-x)^2-6(-x)+5 \\ &= x^2+6x+5 \\ &= (x+1)(x+5) \end{aligned}" />

The zeros of <InlineMath math="F" /> are <InlineMath math="x=-5" /> and <InlineMath math="x=-1" />. Check against the original function: <InlineMath math="f(x)=(x-1)(x-5)" /> has zeros <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="5" />, and **each zero changes sign**. One more point fits too: <InlineMath math="F(-1)=1-6+5=0=f(1)" />. So the image is <InlineMath math="F(x)=x^2+6x+5" />, with zeros <InlineMath math="-5" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" />.

**Problem 2**. Replace <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="-x" /> under the square root, then find the values of <InlineMath math="x" /> that keep the number under the root non-negative:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="G(x)=g(-x)=\sqrt{-x-2}" />
  <BlockMath math="-x-2\ge 0\iff -x\ge 2\iff x\le -2" />
</MathContainer>

The domain of <InlineMath math="G" /> is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,-2]" />. For <InlineMath math="g" /> it was <InlineMath math="[2,\infty)" />, so **the endpoint <InlineMath math="2" /> changes sign** and the whole domain moves to the left of the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Check with the point <InlineMath math="(6,2)" /> on <InlineMath math="g" />, since <InlineMath math="\sqrt{6-2}=2" />. Its image <InlineMath math="(-6,2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="G" />, since <InlineMath math="G(-6)=\sqrt{6-2}=2" />. So <InlineMath math="G(x)=\sqrt{-x-2}" />, with domain <InlineMath math="(-\infty,-2]" />.

**Problem 3**. Replace <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="-x" />, then take the minus sign out of the absolute value. The last step is valid because **an absolute value ignores the sign**, that is <InlineMath math="\lvert -a\rvert=\lvert a\rvert" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} H(x) &= h(-x) = \lvert -x+2\rvert \\ &= \lvert -(x-2)\rvert = \lvert x-2\rvert \end{aligned}" />

An absolute value reaches its smallest value, <InlineMath math="0" />, when its inside is zero, that is at <InlineMath math="x=2" />, so the vertex of <InlineMath math="H" /> is <InlineMath math="(2,0)" />.
