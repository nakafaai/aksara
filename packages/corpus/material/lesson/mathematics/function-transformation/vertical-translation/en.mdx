## Lifting or Lowering the Whole Graph

A vertical shift moves the whole graph of a function up or down without changing its shape. A shift by <InlineMath math="k" /> changes the equation and every point of the graph like this:

<BlockMath math="y = f(x) \longrightarrow y = f(x) + k, \qquad (x_0, y_0) \longrightarrow (x_0, y_0 + k)" />

So **a positive <InlineMath math="k" /> lifts the graph and a negative <InlineMath math="k" /> lowers it**, and every <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays the same. The domain does not change, but the range, the horizontal asymptote (the horizontal line the graph approaches), and the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept move by <InlineMath math="k" />. The interactive 3D view shows the range as the span of heights you see from the side.

For example, <InlineMath math="y = x^2 + 3" /> is the parabola <InlineMath math="y = x^2" /> lifted <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" />, so its vertex moves from <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0, 3)" />. The sign of <InlineMath math="k" /> gives the direction directly, because <InlineMath math="k" /> is added to the output after it has been computed. The zeros do not move by <InlineMath math="k" /> and have to be worked out again.

## Seeing the Range From the Side

The range of a function is the set of all its outputs, so **the range is the span of heights the graph reaches**. Take <InlineMath math="f(x) = |x| - 1" /> with the domain <InlineMath math="-2 \leq x \leq 2" /> and shift its graph <InlineMath math="4 \text{ units}" /> up. The result is <InlineMath math="g(x) = f(x) + 4 = |x| + 3" /> with the same domain.

On the domain <InlineMath math="-2 \leq x \leq 2" />, the [absolute value](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/absolute-value-function) <InlineMath math="|x|" /> is smallest, <InlineMath math="0" />, at <InlineMath math="x = 0" /> and largest, <InlineMath math="2" />, at both ends, so the range of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="[-1, 1]" /> and the range of <InlineMath math="g" /> is <InlineMath math="[3, 5]" />.

In the 3D view, the graph of <InlineMath math="g" /> is drawn in a plane parallel to the plane of <InlineMath math="f" />, so you can look at both from the side without one hiding the other.

Turn the view until you are looking along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Each graph then appears as a vertical segment that covers its own range bar. **Each end of the range bar rises by <InlineMath math="4 \text{ units}" />**, from <InlineMath math="-1" /> to <InlineMath math="3" /> and from <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />, because every height has increased by <InlineMath math="k = 4" />. ## The Vertex and Zeros After the Shift

The function <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 2)^2 - 1" /> has its vertex at <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" /> and its zeros at <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />, the values of <InlineMath math="x" /> that make <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" />. Lift its graph <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" /> by adding <InlineMath math="5" /> to every output:

<BlockMath math="g(x) = f(x) + 5 = x^2 - 4x + 3 + 5 = x^2 - 4x + 8" />

The vertex rises from <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" /> to <InlineMath math="(2, 4)" />. Check: <InlineMath math="g(2) = 4 - 8 + 8 = 4" />. To find the zeros of <InlineMath math="g" />, compute the discriminant of the equation <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 8 = 0" />:

<BlockMath math="D = (-4)^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 8 = 16 - 32 = -16" />

A negative discriminant means the equation [has no real roots](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-equation-types-of-root), so **the graph of <InlineMath math="g" /> does not cross the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis**. Its lowest point already sits at height <InlineMath math="4" />.

The zeros do not move by <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" />, because the shift changes the heights of points while a zero is a value of <InlineMath math="x" />. The values of <InlineMath math="x" /> that make <InlineMath math="f(x) + k = 0" /> are exactly the values that make <InlineMath math="f(x) = -k" />. So the new zeros are **the <InlineMath math="x" />-values where the old graph has height <InlineMath math="-k" />**.

| Value of <InlineMath math="k" /> | Equation <InlineMath math="f(x) = -k" /> | Zeros of <InlineMath math="f(x) + k" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 3 = -5" /> | None |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 3 = -1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-5" /> | <InlineMath math="x^2 - 4x + 3 = 5" /> | <InlineMath math="2 - \sqrt{6}" /> and <InlineMath math="2 + \sqrt{6}" /> |

As <InlineMath math="k" /> increases from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="1" />, the two zeros move toward each other until they meet at <InlineMath math="x = 2" />, where the vertex touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. For <InlineMath math="k > 1" /> both disappear. When the graph is lowered <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" />, the two zeros move apart to about <InlineMath math="-0.45" /> and <InlineMath math="4.45" />.

## The Horizontal Asymptote Moves Too

The graph of the [exponential function](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/exponential-function) <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2^x" /> gets closer and closer to the line <InlineMath math="y = 0" /> as <InlineMath math="x" /> decreases, but it never touches that line. A line that a graph approaches like this is called a horizontal [asymptote](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/asymptote). Shift the graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> down by <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" />, so <InlineMath math="g(x) = 2^x - 3" />.

Since <InlineMath math="2^x" /> is always positive, <InlineMath math="2^x - 3" /> is always greater than <InlineMath math="-3" /> and gets closer to <InlineMath math="-3" /> as <InlineMath math="x" /> decreases. So **the horizontal asymptote of <InlineMath math="g" /> drops to <InlineMath math="y = -3" />**, and the range becomes <InlineMath math="(-3, \infty)" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept and the zero come straight from the rule for <InlineMath math="g" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g(0) = 2^0 - 3 = -2" />
<BlockMath math="2^x - 3 = 0 \iff 2^x = 3 \iff x = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept drops from <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0, -2)" />, exactly <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" />. Check the zero: <InlineMath math="2^{\log_2 3} - 3 = 3 - 3 = 0" />. The graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> has no zero, while the graph of <InlineMath math="g" /> has one, so the zeros do not simply move with the graph.

## Mistakes When Adding a Constant

A constant <InlineMath math="k" /> written **outside the parentheses** always moves the graph vertically by <InlineMath math="k" />, because there <InlineMath math="k" /> is added to the output. A constant inside the parentheses, as in <InlineMath math="f(x + 5)" />, gives a [horizontal shift](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/horizontal-translation) that moves the graph <InlineMath math="5 \text{ units}" /> to the left. Multiplying the output, as in <InlineMath math="3f(x)" />, is a [vertical dilation](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-dilation) that multiplies every height, while adding <InlineMath math="k" /> moves every point by the same distance.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="f(x + 3)" /> for a shift up | A constant inside the parentheses changes the input, so the graph moves sideways | The height at <InlineMath math="x_0" /> must rise by exactly <InlineMath math="3" />, but <InlineMath math="f(x_0 + 3)" /> usually differs from <InlineMath math="f(x_0) + 3" /> |
| Changing the domain | A vertical shift does not touch the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinates | The domain conditions for <InlineMath math="f" /> and <InlineMath math="f + k" /> are identical |
| Moving the zeros by <InlineMath math="k" /> | A zero is a value of <InlineMath math="x" />, while the shift changes heights | Solve <InlineMath math="f(x) = -k" /> |
| Leaving the asymptote at <InlineMath math="y = 0" /> | Every output grows by <InlineMath math="k" />, including the value the graph approaches | Work out the value that <InlineMath math="f(x) + k" /> approaches |
| Reading <InlineMath math="f(x) - 3" /> as a shift up by <InlineMath math="3" /> | That form means <InlineMath math="k = -3" /> | The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept must drop from <InlineMath math="f(0)" /> to <InlineMath math="f(0) - 3" /> |

In [combined function transformations](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/combined-transformation-function), the order of adding to and multiplying the output changes the result: <InlineMath math="2f(x) + 1" /> differs from <InlineMath math="2\big(f(x) + 1\big) = 2f(x) + 2" />.

## Exercises

In each problem, <InlineMath math="k" /> is the change in the height of every point. Add <InlineMath math="k" /> to the whole output <InlineMath math="f(x)" />, then work out the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept, the zeros, or the range directly from the new rule. **Check one point** before you write down your final answer.

1. The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 4x + 3" /> is shifted <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" /> down. Find the equation of the new graph, its vertex, and its zeros.

2. Shift the graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 3x + 2" /> down by <InlineMath math="7 \text{ units}" />. What are the equation of the new graph, its <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept, and its zero?

3. The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = \sqrt{x}" /> is shifted vertically so that the point <InlineMath math="(4, 2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(4, 5)" />. Find <InlineMath math="k" />, the equation of the new graph, and its range.

4. Let <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4" />. For which values of <InlineMath math="k" /> does the function <InlineMath math="y = f(x) + k" /> have exactly one zero, and for which values of <InlineMath math="k" /> does it have no zero?

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. A shift of <InlineMath math="3 \text{ units}" /> down means <InlineMath math="k = -3" />, so subtract <InlineMath math="3" /> from every output:

<BlockMath math="g(x) = f(x) - 3 = x^2 + 4x + 3 - 3 = x^2 + 4x" />

The form <InlineMath math="f(x) = (x + 2)^2 - 1" /> gives the old vertex <InlineMath math="(-2, -1)" />, so the new vertex is <InlineMath math="(-2, -4)" />. Factoring as <InlineMath math="x^2 + 4x = x(x + 4)" /> gives the zeros <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="-4" />. Check with the rule for zeros: both values must satisfy <InlineMath math="f(x) = 3" />, and indeed <InlineMath math="f(0) = 3" /> and <InlineMath math="f(-4) = 16 - 16 + 3 = 3" />. So the new graph is <InlineMath math="y = x^2 + 4x" /> with the vertex <InlineMath math="(-2, -4)" /> and **the zeros <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="-4" />**.

**Problem 2**. A shift of <InlineMath math="7 \text{ units}" /> down means <InlineMath math="k = -7" />. Write the new equation, then find the intercept from the input zero and the zero from the output zero:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g(x) = 3x + 2 - 7 = 3x - 5" />
<BlockMath math="g(0) = 3 \cdot 0 - 5 = -5" />
<BlockMath math="3x - 5 = 0 \iff x = \tfrac{5}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept drops from <InlineMath math="(0, 2)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0, -5)" />, exactly <InlineMath math="7 \text{ units}" />. The zero moves from <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{2}{3}" /> to <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{3}" />, which is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{7}{3} \text{ units}" /> to the right, **a different distance from the shift of the graph**. Check: <InlineMath math="g\left(\tfrac{5}{3}\right) = 5 - 5 = 0" />. So the new graph is <InlineMath math="y = 3x - 5" /> with the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept <InlineMath math="(0, -5)" /> and the zero <InlineMath math="\tfrac{5}{3}" />.

**Problem 3**. The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays <InlineMath math="4" /> and the height changes from <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="5" />, so this is a vertical shift with <InlineMath math="k = 5 - 2 = 3" />. Add <InlineMath math="3" /> to the output, then find the range from the smallest value of <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g(x) = \sqrt{x} + 3" />
<BlockMath math="\sqrt{x} \geq 0 \iff \sqrt{x} + 3 \geq 3" />
</MathContainer>

Check the point given in the problem: <InlineMath math="g(4) = 2 + 3 = 5" />. The domain stays <InlineMath math="[0, \infty)" />, while **the range moves from <InlineMath math="[0, \infty)" /> to <InlineMath math="[3, \infty)" />**. So <InlineMath math="k = 3" /> and the new graph is <InlineMath math="y = \sqrt{x} + 3" />.

**Problem 4**. The zeros of <InlineMath math="y = f(x) + k" /> satisfy this equation:

<BlockMath math="x^2 - 4 + k = 0 \iff x^2 = 4 - k" />

The square of a real number is never negative. If <InlineMath math="4 - k = 0" />, the only solution is <InlineMath math="x = 0" />, so the function has exactly one zero for **<InlineMath math="k = 4" />**. If <InlineMath math="4 - k < 0" />, no real number works, so the function has no zero for **<InlineMath math="k > 4" />**. For <InlineMath math="k < 4" /> there are two zeros, <InlineMath math="x = \pm\sqrt{4 - k}" />. Check <InlineMath math="k = 4" />: <InlineMath math="f(x) + 4 = x^2" />, and the vertex <InlineMath math="(0, -4)" /> rises exactly to <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />, where the graph touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at one point.
