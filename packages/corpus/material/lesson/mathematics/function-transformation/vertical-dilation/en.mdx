## Multiplying Every Output by the Same Factor

A **vertical dilation** by a factor <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />, also called a vertical stretch or compression, turns <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" /> into <InlineMath math="y = a f(x)" />. The factor multiplies the output after the rule <InlineMath math="f" /> has acted, so every point of the graph moves straight up or down:

<BlockMath math="(x_0, y_0) \longmapsto (x_0,\ a y_0)" />

The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays, and every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />. If <InlineMath math="a > 1" />, the graph is **stretched away from the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis**, and if <InlineMath math="0 < a < 1" />, it is compressed toward the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. A negative factor also reflects the graph across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis do not move, because their height is zero and <InlineMath math="a \cdot 0 = 0" />.

In the 3D layer view, several graphs <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> are stacked by their value of <InlineMath math="a" />. You can turn that view and watch every point travel along a straight line as <InlineMath math="a" /> changes.

Apply the rule to the parabola <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2" /> with <InlineMath math="a = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="a = \frac{1}{2}" />.

All three parabolas share the vertex <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />, because a height of zero stays zero. Follow the line <InlineMath math="x = 2" />: the height <InlineMath math="4" /> times <InlineMath math="2" /> becomes <InlineMath math="8" />, and times <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> it becomes <InlineMath math="2" />.

## Heights Scale but Zeros Stay Put

At the same <InlineMath math="x" />-value, the new graph is always <InlineMath math="a" /> times as high as the old one. A zero <InlineMath math="x_0" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="f(x_0) = 0" />, so <InlineMath math="a f(x_0) = 0" /> as well. Conversely, with <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />, the product <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> is zero only when <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" />, so no new zero appears. Together, these two facts mean that **zeros do not move**. For <InlineMath math="a = 0" />, every <InlineMath math="x" /> would become a zero, which is why a dilation needs <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />.

A [vertical shift](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-translation) adds the same number to every height, so points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis move up or down too, and zeros can move, appear, or disappear. Multiplication keeps a height of zero at zero, while addition turns it into the number that was added.

Apply the factor <InlineMath math="a = 3" /> to <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 1)(x - 3)" />. Multiply the whole function by <InlineMath math="3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(x) &= 3f(x) \\ &= 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) \\ &= 3x^2 - 12x + 9 \end{aligned}" />

The vertex of <InlineMath math="g" /> lies at <InlineMath math="x = \frac{12}{2 \cdot 3} = 2" /> with <InlineMath math="g(2) = -3 = 3 \cdot (-1)" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept becomes <InlineMath math="(0, 9)" /> because <InlineMath math="g(0) = 9 = 3 \cdot 3" />. **Every height triples**, negative heights included.

## Straight Lines Through All the Layers

At one fixed <InlineMath math="x" />, how does the height change as <InlineMath math="a" /> runs from <InlineMath math="2" /> down to <InlineMath math="-1" />? The 3D layer view stacks the graphs of <InlineMath math="y = a f(x)" /> for <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 1" /> and <InlineMath math="a = 2" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" />, <InlineMath math="0" />, and <InlineMath math="-1" />. The layer for the factor <InlineMath math="a" /> lies at <InlineMath math="z = 2(1 - a)" />, so the original graph <InlineMath math="a = 1" /> sits in the <InlineMath math="xy" />-plane, and **the gap between two layers is proportional to the difference of their <InlineMath math="a" />-values**.

The flat layer is <InlineMath math="a = 0" />, because <InlineMath math="0 \cdot f(x) = 0" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" />. A dilation needs <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />, so the flat layer is only the hinge that every line passes through. Straight lines connect the points with the same <InlineMath math="x" />-value on all layers, at <InlineMath math="x = -2" />, <InlineMath math="-1" />, <InlineMath math="0" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="2" />.

Follow the line at <InlineMath math="x = 2" />. Its points are <InlineMath math="(2, 3a)" />, with heights <InlineMath math="6" />, <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{2}" />, <InlineMath math="0" />, and <InlineMath math="-3" /> for <InlineMath math="a = 2" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" />, <InlineMath math="0" />, and <InlineMath math="-1" />. The line is straight because both the height <InlineMath math="3a" /> and the layer position <InlineMath math="z = 2(1 - a)" /> change linearly with <InlineMath math="a" />.

Drag the view until the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis points at you. In that side view, **every line passes through the flat layer <InlineMath math="a = 0" />**. The lines at <InlineMath math="x = -1" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> lie flat at height <InlineMath math="0" />, because those two values are the zeros of <InlineMath math="f" />. Past the flat layer, the height on every other line changes sign, so the layer <InlineMath math="a = -1" /> is upside down.

Every line stays at a single <InlineMath math="x" />-value, because a vertical dilation only moves points up or down. The two lines at the zeros do not even change height, because points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis never move.

All layers cover the same interval, from <InlineMath math="x = -2" /> to <InlineMath math="x = 2" />, because a vertical dilation moves no point left or right, so the domain does not change. In a [horizontal dilation](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/horizontal-dilation), the trace of a point across similar layers is curved, because the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate is divided by the input multiplier.

## A Negative Factor Flips the Graph

A negative factor can be read as two steps. First multiply by <InlineMath math="|a|" /> to stretch or compress, then multiply by <InlineMath math="-1" /> to **reflect the graph across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis**. For <InlineMath math="a = -1" />, only the [vertical reflection](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-reflection) <InlineMath math="y = -f(x)" /> remains.

For <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2" /> and <InlineMath math="a = -2" />, every height <InlineMath math="x^2" /> becomes <InlineMath math="-2x^2" />. The point <InlineMath math="(1, 1)" /> drops to <InlineMath math="(1, -2)" />, and the point <InlineMath math="(2, 4)" /> drops to <InlineMath math="(2, -8)" />. The parabola that opened upward now opens downward and is narrower, while the vertex <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> does not move.

Take <InlineMath math="h(x) = |x - 1| + 2" />. Its vertex <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" /> is the lowest point, so the range of <InlineMath math="h" /> is <InlineMath math="[2, \infty)" />. Multiply the whole function by <InlineMath math="a = -2" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} H(x) &= -2\,h(x) \\ &= -2\left(|x - 1| + 2\right) \\ &= -2|x - 1| - 4 \end{aligned}" />

The vertex moves to <InlineMath math="(1, -4)" /> and is now the highest point. The range changes from <InlineMath math="[2, \infty)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-\infty, -4]" />, because **multiplying by a negative number reverses the order of the endpoints**. Check one more point: <InlineMath math="h(3) = 4" />, and <InlineMath math="H(3) = -2 \cdot 2 - 4 = -8 = -2 \cdot 4" />.

For comparison, <InlineMath math="a = -1" /> gives <InlineMath math="-h(x) = -|x - 1| - 2" /> with vertex <InlineMath math="(1, -2)" />. That graph is only reflected, without a stretch.

## Range and Asymptote After Multiplying

Because only heights change, **every feature set by a height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />**, while every feature set by an <InlineMath math="x" />-value stays.

| Feature | In <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point <InlineMath math="(x_0, y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(x_0, a y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="x" /> kept, height multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Zeros | unchanged | a height of zero stays zero |
| Domain | unchanged | the domain holds <InlineMath math="x" />-values |
| Range <InlineMath math="[c, d]" /> for <InlineMath math="a > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="[ac, ad]" /> | every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Range <InlineMath math="[c, d]" /> for <InlineMath math="a < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="[ad, ac]" /> | the endpoints change order |
| <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept | <InlineMath math="(0, a f(0))" /> | its height <InlineMath math="f(0)" /> is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Horizontal asymptote <InlineMath math="y = y_1" /> | <InlineMath math="y = a y_1" /> | a horizontal line is fixed by a height |

The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2^x - 4" /> approaches the [horizontal asymptote](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/asymptote) <InlineMath math="y = -4" /> and has range <InlineMath math="(-4, \infty)" />. ## Mistakes When Multiplying the Output

Every mistake in this table breaks the same rule: **the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays**, and **every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />**.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiplying only the first term, as in <InlineMath math="3x^2 - 4x + 3" /> for <InlineMath math="3f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3" /> | The factor multiplies the whole output | At <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> the result is <InlineMath math="2" />, although the zero must stay <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Moving the zeros | <InlineMath math="a \cdot 0 = 0" /> | Substitute the old zeros into the new function |
| Changing the domain | The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinates do not change | Compare the domain endpoints before and after |
| Forgetting the reflection when <InlineMath math="a < 0" /> | A negative sign flips every height | The lowest point must become the highest point |
| Confusing <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="f(ax)" /> | A factor outside multiplies outputs, a factor inside multiplies inputs | Compare the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercepts: <InlineMath math="a f(0)" /> differs from <InlineMath math="f(0)" /> when <InlineMath math="f(0) \ne 0" /> |
| Leaving a horizontal asymptote at its old height | The asymptote height <InlineMath math="y_1" /> is multiplied too | Multiply the asymptote height by <InlineMath math="a" /> |

In <InlineMath math="H(x) = -2\left(|x - 1| + 2\right) = -2|x - 1| - 4" />, the shift of <InlineMath math="2" /> units up was already inside <InlineMath math="h" /> before the multiplication, so the dilation turns that shift into <InlineMath math="-4" />. If you multiply <InlineMath math="|x - 1|" /> by <InlineMath math="-2" /> first and then shift <InlineMath math="2" /> units up, you get <InlineMath math="-2|x - 1| + 2" /> with vertex <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" />, a different graph. In [combined function transformations](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/combined-transformation-function), you write down the function after every step so that a change like this is not missed.
