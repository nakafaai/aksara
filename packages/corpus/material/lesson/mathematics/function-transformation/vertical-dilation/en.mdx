## Multiplying Every Output by the Same Factor

A **vertical dilation** by a factor <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />, also called a vertical stretch or compression, turns <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" /> into <InlineMath math="y = a f(x)" />. The factor multiplies the output after the rule <InlineMath math="f" /> has acted, so every point of the graph moves straight up or down:

<BlockMath math="(x_0, y_0) \longmapsto (x_0,\ a y_0)" />

The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays, and every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />. If <InlineMath math="a > 1" />, the graph is **stretched away from the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis**, and if <InlineMath math="0 < a < 1" />, it is compressed toward the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. A negative factor also reflects the graph across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis do not move, because their height is zero and <InlineMath math="a \cdot 0 = 0" />.

In the 3D layer view, several graphs <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> are stacked by their value of <InlineMath math="a" />. You can turn that view and watch every point travel along a straight line as <InlineMath math="a" /> changes.

## Heights Scale but Zeros Stay Put

At the same <InlineMath math="x" />-value, the new graph is always <InlineMath math="a" /> times as high as the old one. A zero <InlineMath math="x_0" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="f(x_0) = 0" />, so <InlineMath math="a f(x_0) = 0" /> as well. Conversely, with <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />, the product <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> is zero only when <InlineMath math="f(x) = 0" />, so no new zero appears. Together, these two facts mean that **zeros do not move**. For <InlineMath math="a = 0" />, every <InlineMath math="x" /> would become a zero, which is why a dilation needs <InlineMath math="a \ne 0" />.

A [vertical shift](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-translation) adds the same number to every height, so points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis move up or down too, and zeros can move, appear, or disappear. Multiplication keeps a height of zero at zero, while addition turns it into the number that was added.

Apply the factor <InlineMath math="a = 3" /> to <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 1)(x - 3)" />. The graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> crosses the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis at <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 3" />, has its vertex at <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" />, and meets the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis at <InlineMath math="(0, 3)" />. Multiply the whole function by <InlineMath math="3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(x) &= 3f(x) \\ &= 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) \\ &= 3x^2 - 12x + 9 \end{aligned}" />

Check the three special points. The values <InlineMath math="g(1) = 3 - 12 + 9 = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="g(3) = 27 - 36 + 9 = 0" /> show that the zeros stay. The vertex of <InlineMath math="g" /> lies at <InlineMath math="x = \frac{12}{2 \cdot 3} = 2" /> with <InlineMath math="g(2) = -3 = 3 \cdot (-1)" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept becomes <InlineMath math="(0, 9)" /> because <InlineMath math="g(0) = 9 = 3 \cdot 3" />. **Every height triples**, negative heights included.

## A Negative Factor Flips the Graph

A negative factor can be read as two steps. First multiply by <InlineMath math="|a|" /> to stretch or compress, then multiply by <InlineMath math="-1" /> to **reflect the graph across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis**. For <InlineMath math="a = -1" />, only the [vertical reflection](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-reflection) <InlineMath math="y = -f(x)" /> remains.

Take <InlineMath math="h(x) = |x - 1| + 2" />. Its vertex <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" /> is the lowest point, so the range of <InlineMath math="h" /> is <InlineMath math="[2, \infty)" />. Multiply the whole function by <InlineMath math="a = -2" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} H(x) &= -2\,h(x) \\ &= -2\left(|x - 1| + 2\right) \\ &= -2|x - 1| - 4 \end{aligned}" />

The vertex moves to <InlineMath math="(1, -4)" /> and is now the highest point. The range changes from <InlineMath math="[2, \infty)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-\infty, -4]" />, because **multiplying by a negative number reverses the order of the endpoints**. Check one more point: <InlineMath math="h(3) = 4" />, and <InlineMath math="H(3) = -2 \cdot 2 - 4 = -8 = -2 \cdot 4" />.

## Range and Asymptote After Multiplying

Because only heights change, **every feature set by a height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />**, while every feature set by an <InlineMath math="x" />-value stays.

| Feature | In <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point <InlineMath math="(x_0, y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(x_0, a y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="x" /> kept, height multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Zeros | unchanged | a height of zero stays zero |
| Domain | unchanged | the domain holds <InlineMath math="x" />-values |
| Range <InlineMath math="[c, d]" /> for <InlineMath math="a > 0" /> | <InlineMath math="[ac, ad]" /> | every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Range <InlineMath math="[c, d]" /> for <InlineMath math="a < 0" /> | <InlineMath math="[ad, ac]" /> | the endpoints change order |
| <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept | <InlineMath math="(0, a f(0))" /> | its height <InlineMath math="f(0)" /> is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" /> |
| Horizontal asymptote <InlineMath math="y = y_1" /> | <InlineMath math="y = a y_1" /> | a horizontal line is fixed by a height |

The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2^x - 4" /> approaches the [horizontal asymptote](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/asymptote) <InlineMath math="y = -4" /> and has range <InlineMath math="(-4, \infty)" />. With <InlineMath math="a = \frac{1}{2}" />, the function becomes <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^x - 2" />. Its asymptote is <InlineMath math="y = -2" />, its range is <InlineMath math="(-2, \infty)" />, and its <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept changes from <InlineMath math="(0, -3)" /> to <InlineMath math="\left(0, -\frac{3}{2}\right)" />. For <InlineMath math="a \ne 1" />, the only horizontal asymptote that stays in place is <InlineMath math="y = 0" />, because <InlineMath math="a \cdot 0 = 0" />.

## One Graph From Two Transformations

For certain functions, a vertical dilation gives exactly the same graph as a horizontal dilation or a shift. **The match comes from the algebra of the function**, so each row of the table takes one short calculation to prove.

| <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> | Vertical dilation | Same graph written as | Read as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="4x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="(2x)^2" /> | horizontal compression with <InlineMath math="b = 2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{x}" /> | <InlineMath math="\sqrt{\frac{x}{4}}" /> | horizontal stretch with <InlineMath math="b = \frac{1}{4}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2^x" /> | <InlineMath math="2 \cdot 2^x" /> | <InlineMath math="2^{x + 1}" /> | shift of <InlineMath math="1" /> unit to the left |

The first row uses <InlineMath math="(2x)^2 = 4x^2" />, the second uses <InlineMath math="\sqrt{\frac{x}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{4}}" /> for <InlineMath math="x \ge 0" />, and the third uses <InlineMath math="2^{x + 1} = 2^x \cdot 2" />. These matches depend on the function. For <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 1" />, <InlineMath math="4f(x) = 4x^2 + 4" /> while <InlineMath math="f(2x) = 4x^2 + 1" />, so the two graphs differ.

The first row also explains the shape of <InlineMath math="y = a x^2" />. When <InlineMath math="a > 0" />, <InlineMath math="a x^2 = \left(\sqrt{a}\, x\right)^2" />, so the leading coefficient that controls how narrow a parabola is in the [quadratic function characteristics](/en/subjects/mathematics/quadratic-function/quadratic-function-characteristics) can be read as a vertical dilation by <InlineMath math="a" /> or as a horizontal dilation with input multiplier <InlineMath math="\sqrt{a}" />.

## Mistakes When Multiplying the Output

Every mistake in this table breaks the same rule: **the <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate stays**, and **every height is multiplied by <InlineMath math="a" />**.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiplying only the first term, as in <InlineMath math="3x^2 - 4x + 3" /> for <InlineMath math="3f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3" /> | The factor multiplies the whole output | At <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> the result is <InlineMath math="2" />, although the zero must stay <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Moving the zeros | <InlineMath math="a \cdot 0 = 0" /> | Substitute the old zeros into the new function |
| Changing the domain | The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinates do not change | Compare the domain endpoints before and after |
| Forgetting the reflection when <InlineMath math="a < 0" /> | A negative sign flips every height | The lowest point must become the highest point |
| Confusing <InlineMath math="a f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="f(ax)" /> | A factor outside multiplies outputs, a factor inside multiplies inputs | Compare the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercepts: <InlineMath math="a f(0)" /> differs from <InlineMath math="f(0)" /> when <InlineMath math="f(0) \ne 0" /> |
| Leaving a horizontal asymptote at its old height | The asymptote height <InlineMath math="y_1" /> is multiplied too | Multiply the asymptote height by <InlineMath math="a" /> |

In <InlineMath math="H(x) = -2\left(|x - 1| + 2\right) = -2|x - 1| - 4" />, the shift of <InlineMath math="2" /> units up was already inside <InlineMath math="h" /> before the multiplication, so the dilation turns that shift into <InlineMath math="-4" />. If you multiply <InlineMath math="|x - 1|" /> by <InlineMath math="-2" /> first and then shift <InlineMath math="2" /> units up, you get <InlineMath math="-2|x - 1| + 2" /> with vertex <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" />, a different graph. In [combined function transformations](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/combined-transformation-function), you write down the function after every step so that a change like this is not missed.

## Exercises

The problems move from direct multiplication to finding the factor yourself. In each one, **multiply the whole output by the factor**, constant term included.

1. Given <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x - 8" />, find <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}f(x)" />, its zeros, and its vertex.
2. The graph of <InlineMath math="g(x) = \sqrt{x}" /> is compressed vertically by the factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" />. Find the new equation, the image of the point <InlineMath math="(4, 2)" />, and the range.
3. The function <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2^x + 1" /> is multiplied by the factor <InlineMath math="-3" />. Find the new equation, its horizontal asymptote, its range, and its <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept.
4. The graph of <InlineMath math="y = a(x^2 - 1)" /> passes through <InlineMath math="(2, 12)" />. Find <InlineMath math="a" /> and the zeros of the graph.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. The factor is <InlineMath math="a = \frac{1}{3}" />, so every term of <InlineMath math="f" /> is multiplied by <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(x) &= \tfrac{1}{3}\left(x^2 - 2x - 8\right) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{3}x^2 - \tfrac{2}{3}x - \tfrac{8}{3} \end{aligned}" />

The zeros of <InlineMath math="f(x) = (x + 2)(x - 4)" /> are <InlineMath math="-2" /> and <InlineMath math="4" />, and **both remain zeros of <InlineMath math="g" />**. The vertex of <InlineMath math="f" /> lies exactly midway between the two zeros, at <InlineMath math="x = \frac{-2 + 4}{2} = 1" />, and <InlineMath math="f(1) = 1 - 2 - 8 = -9" />. The factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}" /> is positive, so the lowest point of <InlineMath math="f" /> stays the lowest point of <InlineMath math="g" /> with the same <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate. The vertex of <InlineMath math="g" /> is <InlineMath math="\left(1, \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot (-9)\right) = (1, -3)" />. Check: <InlineMath math="g(1) = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{2}{3} - \frac{8}{3} = -3" />.

**Problem 2**. The factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" /> compresses the graph toward the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis:

<BlockMath math="G(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{x}" />

The point <InlineMath math="(4, 2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(4, 1)" /> because <InlineMath math="G(4) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1" />. **The range stays <InlineMath math="[0, \infty)" />**, because every number <InlineMath math="r \ge 0" /> is still reached, namely at <InlineMath math="x = 4r^2" />: <InlineMath math="G(4r^2) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2r = r" />.

**Problem 3**. Multiply the whole function, including the constant <InlineMath math="1" />, by <InlineMath math="-3" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} H(x) &= -3\left(2^x + 1\right) \\ &= -3 \cdot 2^x - 3 \end{aligned}" />

The asymptote <InlineMath math="y = 1" /> becomes <InlineMath math="y = -3" />. Because <InlineMath math="2^x + 1 > 1" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" />, multiplying by <InlineMath math="-3" /> **reverses the inequality** to <InlineMath math="H(x) < -3" />, so the range is <InlineMath math="(-\infty, -3)" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept is <InlineMath math="(0, -6)" /> because <InlineMath math="H(0) = -3 \cdot 2 = -6" />.

**Problem 4**. Substitute the point <InlineMath math="(2, 12)" /> into the equation of the graph:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} a\left(2^2 - 1\right) &= 12 \\ 3a &= 12 \\ a &= 4 \end{aligned}" />

The equation is <InlineMath math="y = 4(x^2 - 1)" />. **Its zeros stay at <InlineMath math="x = -1" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 1" />**, the same as the zeros of <InlineMath math="x^2 - 1" />. Check the given point: <InlineMath math="4(2^2 - 1) = 4 \cdot 3 = 12" />.
