## Flipping a Graph Across the x-Axis

A vertical reflection flips the graph of a function across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. To get it, multiply every output of the function by <InlineMath math="-1" />, so <InlineMath math="y=f(x)" /> becomes <InlineMath math="y=-f(x)" />. Each point <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(x_0,-y_0)" />, so **the horizontal position stays and the height changes sign**. As a result, the zeros stay where they are, the highest point becomes the lowest, and a range <InlineMath math="[c,d]" /> becomes <InlineMath math="[-d,-c]" />.

In the 3D view, two mirrors run along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis. Turn the view to see which mirror produces <InlineMath math="-f(x)" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="y=f(x)\;\longrightarrow\;y=-f(x)" />
  <BlockMath math="(x_0,\,y_0)\;\longmapsto\;(x_0,\,-y_0)" />
</MathContainer>

Points above the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis move below it at the same distance from the axis, points below move above it, and **points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis stay where they are**. It is called a vertical reflection because every point moves up or down, even though the mirror itself is the horizontal <InlineMath math="x" />-axis.

Try one point. The point <InlineMath math="(1,2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="y=2^x" /> because <InlineMath math="2^1=2" />. Its image, <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" />, lies on <InlineMath math="y=-2^x" /> because <InlineMath math="-2^1=-\left(2^1\right)=-2" />. The expression <InlineMath math="-2^x" /> means <InlineMath math="-\left(2^x\right)" />, because the power is evaluated before the minus sign. The point rule is the same as in [reflecting a point over the horizontal axis](/en/subjects/mathematics/geometric-transformation/reflection-over-x-axis), but here you reflect a whole graph and write the result as a new equation.

## A Flat Mirror and an Upright Mirror

Both the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis can act as mirrors, and each one changes the sign of a different coordinate. The flat mirror on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis changes the sign of the height, so <InlineMath math="y=2^x" /> becomes <InlineMath math="y=-2^x" />. The upright mirror on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis changes the sign of the horizontal position, so <InlineMath math="y=2^x" /> becomes <InlineMath math="y=2^{-x}" />. When a point is reflected in **both mirrors, one after the other**, both of its coordinates change sign:

| Mirror | Point rule | Image of <InlineMath math="y=2^x" /> | Image of <InlineMath math="P(1,2)" /> |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| flat, on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis | <InlineMath math="(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)" /> | <InlineMath math="y=-2^x" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" /> |
| upright, on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis | <InlineMath math="(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)" /> | <InlineMath math="y=2^{-x}" /> | <InlineMath math="(-1,2)" /> |
| both mirrors | <InlineMath math="(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)" /> | <InlineMath math="y=-2^{-x}" /> | <InlineMath math="(-1,-2)" /> |

In the 3D view, both mirrors are frames, and each axis runs right through the middle of its mirror frame. The four curves from the table lie in the plane of the graph.

## Multiplying Every Output by Negative One

The formula <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> takes each output of <InlineMath math="f" /> and changes its sign, while the input <InlineMath math="x" /> is left alone. Write the original formula inside parentheses so that **the minus sign reaches every term**. For example, reflect <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2-2x-3" /> across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis:

<BlockMath math="-f(x)=-\left(x^2-2x-3\right)=-x^2+2x+3" />

To check the result, write <InlineMath math="f" /> in factored form and in vertex form, then multiply each form by <InlineMath math="-1" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(x) &= (x+1)(x-3) \\ -f(x) &= -(x+1)(x-3) \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(x) &= (x-1)^2-4 \\ -f(x) &= -(x-1)^2+4 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The factored form shows that the zeros <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> stay, because zero times <InlineMath math="-1" /> is still zero. The vertex form shows that the vertex <InlineMath math="(1,-4)" />, the lowest point of <InlineMath math="f" />, becomes <InlineMath math="(1,4)" />, the **highest point** of <InlineMath math="-f" />. The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept changes sign too, from <InlineMath math="(0,-3)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0,3)" />.

Multiplying by <InlineMath math="-1" /> is the special case <InlineMath math="a=-1" /> of the [vertical dilation](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/vertical-dilation) <InlineMath math="y=a\,f(x)" />. The distance from each point to the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis stays the same, and only the sign of the height flips.

## Range and Asymptote That Flip Direction

Take the function <InlineMath math="f(x)=2^x-1" />. As <InlineMath math="x" /> gets smaller, <InlineMath math="2^x" /> approaches <InlineMath math="0" />, so <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> approaches <InlineMath math="-1" /> without ever reaching it. The line <InlineMath math="y=-1" /> is the horizontal [asymptote](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-modeling/asymptote) of the graph of <InlineMath math="f" />. The graph of <InlineMath math="f" /> also passes through <InlineMath math="(0,0)" /> because <InlineMath math="2^0-1=0" />. Reflect <InlineMath math="f" /> across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis:

<BlockMath math="-f(x)=-\left(2^x-1\right)=-2^x+1" />

To find the new range, start from the range of <InlineMath math="2^x" />, which is all positive numbers. Subtract <InlineMath math="1" /> from every value, then multiply each result by <InlineMath math="-1" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 2^x &\in (0,\infty) \\ 2^x-1 &\in (-1,\infty) \\ -\left(2^x-1\right) &\in (-\infty,1) \end{aligned}" />

Subtracting <InlineMath math="1" /> shifts the whole interval <InlineMath math="(0,\infty)" /> down by <InlineMath math="1" />, so the range of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="(-1,\infty)" />. Multiplying by <InlineMath math="-1" /> changes the sign of every value and **flips the inequality sign**: values that were greater than <InlineMath math="-1" /> become less than <InlineMath math="1" />, and the range of <InlineMath math="-f" /> is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,1)" />.

The asymptote is reflected too, from <InlineMath math="y=-1" /> to <InlineMath math="y=1" />, and the point <InlineMath math="(0,0)" /> stays because it lies on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Check two points: <InlineMath math="f(1)=1" /> becomes <InlineMath math="-f(1)=-1" />, and <InlineMath math="f(-1)=-\tfrac{1}{2}" /> becomes <InlineMath math="-f(-1)=\tfrac{1}{2}" />. Both heights change sign, and the new values <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{2}" /> lie below <InlineMath math="1" />, as the range of <InlineMath math="-f" /> requires.

## Peaks and Valleys That Swap Places

Over one period <InlineMath math="[0,2\pi]" />, the graph of <InlineMath math="y=\sin x" /> has its highest point, a peak, at <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},1\right)" /> and its lowest point, a valley, at <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2},-1\right)" />. A reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis changes the sign of every height, so **peaks and valleys swap places**: the peak <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},1\right)" /> becomes the valley <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},-1\right)" />, and the valley <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2},-1\right)" /> becomes the peak <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2},1\right)" />.

Features set by horizontal position do not change. The zeros <InlineMath math="0" />, <InlineMath math="\pi" />, and <InlineMath math="2\pi" /> stay, and the period stays <InlineMath math="2\pi" />. The amplitude also stays <InlineMath math="1" />, because the amplitude is half the difference in height between a peak and a valley, and that difference does not change. On the graph, <InlineMath math="\tfrac{\pi}{2}\approx 1.57" /> and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\approx 4.71" />.

On <InlineMath math="[0,2\pi]" />, <InlineMath math="y=\sin x" /> rises, falls, and rises again, while <InlineMath math="y=-\sin x" /> falls, rises, and falls again.

## Telling the Outside Minus From the Inside Minus

A minus sign outside the function, <InlineMath math="-f(x)" />, changes the output. A minus sign inside the parentheses, <InlineMath math="f(-x)" />, changes the input and gives a [reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis](/en/subjects/mathematics/function-transformation/horizontal-reflection). For <InlineMath math="f(x)=2^x" />, the two formulas give different values at <InlineMath math="x=1" />:

| Comparison | <InlineMath math="y=-f(x)" /> | <InlineMath math="y=f(-x)" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What gets the minus sign | the output | the input |
| Mirror | <InlineMath math="x" />-axis | <InlineMath math="y" />-axis |
| Image of the point <InlineMath math="(x_0,y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(x_0,-y_0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(-x_0,y_0)" /> |
| Points that stay fixed | points on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis | points on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis |
| What flips | the range | the domain |
| Value at <InlineMath math="x=1" /> for <InlineMath math="f(x)=2^x" /> | <InlineMath math="-2^1=-2" /> | <InlineMath math="2^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}" /> |

For some functions, the two minus signs give the same graph. This happens exactly when <InlineMath math="f(-x)=-f(x)" /> for every <InlineMath math="x" />, that is, for **odd functions** such as <InlineMath math="x^3" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin x" />. For example, <InlineMath math="(-x)^3=-x^3" />, so reflecting <InlineMath math="y=x^3" /> across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis or across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis gives the same curve <InlineMath math="y=-x^3" />.

## Mistakes in Distributing the Minus Sign

The minus sign in <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> must reach **the whole output**, and it must not touch the input. The first two mistakes in the table break that rule, and the other three come from reading the result:

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="-x^2-2x-3" /> for <InlineMath math="-\left(x^2-2x-3\right)" /> | The minus sign only reaches the first term | The zeros must stay: at <InlineMath math="x=3" /> the result must be <InlineMath math="0" />, but <InlineMath math="-9-6-3=-18" /> |
| Writing <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> | That formula reflects across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis | The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept must change sign to <InlineMath math="(0,-f(0))" /> |
| Turning the range <InlineMath math="[c,d]" /> into <InlineMath math="[-c,-d]" /> | The left endpoint ends up larger than the right one | The left endpoint is always the smaller number, so the result is <InlineMath math="[-d,-c]" /> |
| Moving the zeros | A point at height <InlineMath math="0" /> stays put because <InlineMath math="-0=0" /> | Substitute the zeros into the new formula |
| Leaving the horizontal asymptote where it was | The asymptote <InlineMath math="y=q" /> is reflected to <InlineMath math="y=-q" /> | For <InlineMath math="2^x-1" />, the asymptote <InlineMath math="y=-1" /> must become <InlineMath math="y=1" /> |

## Exercises

Problems 1 to 3 ask where a vertex, a range with its asymptote, and a <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept end up after the reflection. Problem 4 reflects the turning points of a cubic graph and then compares the reflections across both axes. In each problem, **write the original formula inside parentheses** before you multiply it by <InlineMath math="-1" />, then check your answer with one point.

1. Find the equation of the graph of <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2+4x+3" /> after it is reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, then find the vertex of the image.

2. The graph of <InlineMath math="g(x)=3^x+2" /> is reflected across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Find the equation of the resulting graph, its range, and its horizontal asymptote.

3. Reflect <InlineMath math="h(x)=\sqrt{x+1}" /> across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. Find the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept and the range of the resulting graph.

4. The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^3-3x" /> has a local maximum at <InlineMath math="(-1,2)" /> and a local minimum at <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" />. Find the images of both points after a reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, then show that a reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis gives the same graph as the reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. Multiply the whole formula by <InlineMath math="-1" />, with the original formula inside parentheses:

<BlockMath math="-f(x)=-\left(x^2+4x+3\right)=-x^2-4x-3" />

The vertex of a parabola <InlineMath math="y=ax^2+bx+c" /> lies on its axis of symmetry <InlineMath math="x=-\tfrac{b}{2a}" />. For <InlineMath math="f" />, the values <InlineMath math="a=1" /> and <InlineMath math="b=4" /> give <InlineMath math="x=-\tfrac{4}{2\cdot 1}=-2" />, and <InlineMath math="f(-2)=4-8+3=-1" />, so the vertex of <InlineMath math="f" /> is <InlineMath math="(-2,-1)" />. The reflection changes the sign of the height, so **the vertex of the image** is <InlineMath math="(-2,1)" />. Check with the new formula: <InlineMath math="-(-2)^2-4(-2)-3=-4+8-3=1" />. The zeros <InlineMath math="-3" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" /> stay as well, because <InlineMath math="f(x)=(x+1)(x+3)" />. So the image is <InlineMath math="y=-x^2-4x-3" />, with vertex <InlineMath math="(-2,1)" />.

**Problem 2**. Multiply the whole formula by <InlineMath math="-1" />, then find the range, starting from the range of <InlineMath math="3^x" />, which is all positive numbers:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="-g(x)=-\left(3^x+2\right)=-3^x-2" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 3^x &\in (0,\infty) \\ 3^x+2 &\in (2,\infty) \\ -\left(3^x+2\right) &\in (-\infty,-2) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Adding <InlineMath math="2" /> gives the range of <InlineMath math="g" />, which is <InlineMath math="(2,\infty)" />. Multiplying by <InlineMath math="-1" /> in the last step **reverses the inequality sign**, so the range of the result is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,-2)" />, and the horizontal asymptote moves from <InlineMath math="y=2" /> to <InlineMath math="y=-2" />. Check one point: <InlineMath math="g(0)=3" /> becomes <InlineMath math="-3^0-2=-3" />. The height changes sign, and <InlineMath math="-3" /> is indeed less than <InlineMath math="-2" />. So the resulting graph is <InlineMath math="y=-3^x-2" />, with range <InlineMath math="(-\infty,-2)" /> and asymptote <InlineMath math="y=-2" />.

**Problem 3**. The minus sign goes in front of the square root, so the input does not change and the domain stays <InlineMath math="[-1,\infty)" />:

<BlockMath math="H(x)=-h(x)=-\sqrt{x+1}" />

The <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept comes from <InlineMath math="H(0)=-\sqrt{1}=-1" />, so it is <InlineMath math="(0,-1)" />. Because <InlineMath math="\sqrt{x+1}\ge 0" />, the value <InlineMath math="-\sqrt{x+1}" /> is always <InlineMath math="\le 0" />, so **the range flips** from <InlineMath math="[0,\infty)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-\infty,0]" />. Check one point: <InlineMath math="(3,2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="h" /> because <InlineMath math="\sqrt{4}=2" />, and <InlineMath math="H(3)=-2" />. So the <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept is <InlineMath math="(0,-1)" /> and the range is <InlineMath math="(-\infty,0]" />.

**Problem 4**. A reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis changes the sign of each height, so <InlineMath math="(-1,2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-1,-2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(1,2)" />. The local maximum becomes a local minimum, and the other way around. For the reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis, compute <InlineMath math="f(-x)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(-x) &= (-x)^3-3(-x) = -x^3+3x \\ &= -\left(x^3-3x\right) = -f(x) \end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="f" /> is an **odd function**, and both reflections give the same graph, <InlineMath math="y=-x^3+3x" />. Check with the point rule for a reflection across the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis: <InlineMath math="(-1,2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(1,2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,-2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-1,-2)" />, the same two points as the reflection across the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. So the images of the two points are <InlineMath math="(-1,-2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,2)" />.
