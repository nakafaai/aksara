## Mirroring a Graph Across the x-Axis

A **vertical reflection** across the x-axis turns <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" /> into <InlineMath math="y = -f(x)" />. Every point <InlineMath math="(x_0, y_0)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(x_0, -y_0)" />, so **the x-coordinate stays the same** and every height changes sign. Zeros do not move, because their height is zero. A highest point becomes a lowest point. The first graph shows <InlineMath math="x^2 - 1" /> beside its mirror image <InlineMath math="-x^2 + 1" />, so you can follow one point across the x-axis.

<BlockMath math="(x_0, y_0) \longmapsto (x_0,\ -y_0)" />

The formula gives the same result. If the point <InlineMath math="(x_0, y_0)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="f" />, then <InlineMath math="y_0 = f(x_0)" />. The new function gives <InlineMath math="-f(x_0) = -y_0" /> at the same input, so the point <InlineMath math="(x_0, -y_0)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="-f(x)" />.

The arrow shows the rule at work. The height of the point is multiplied by <InlineMath math="-1" />, while its horizontal position does not move. The highest point of the first graph has become the lowest point of the second.

## Multiplying the Whole Output by Negative One

Let <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 1)^2 - 4" />. The vertical reflection **multiplies the whole output by negative one**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(x) &= -f(x) \\ &= -(x^2 - 2x - 3) \\ &= -x^2 + 2x + 3 \end{aligned}" />

Check the three special points. The zeros of <InlineMath math="g" /> are still <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />, because <InlineMath math="g(-1) = -1 - 2 + 3 = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="g(3) = -9 + 6 + 3 = 0" />. The minimum <InlineMath math="(1, -4)" /> of <InlineMath math="f" /> becomes the maximum <InlineMath math="(1, 4)" /> of <InlineMath math="g" />, because <InlineMath math="g(1) = -1 + 2 + 3 = 4" />. The y-intercept moves from <InlineMath math="(0, -3)" /> to <InlineMath math="(0, 3)" />, since <InlineMath math="g(0) = 3" />.

<BlockMath math="g(x) = -f(x) = -2^x + 1" />

The asymptote moves to <InlineMath math="y = 1" />, because the height <InlineMath math="-1" /> becomes <InlineMath math="1" />. **The range flips** to <InlineMath math="(-\infty, 1)" />, since every value of <InlineMath math="f" /> above <InlineMath math="-1" /> becomes a value below <InlineMath math="1" />. The point <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> stays where it is, because it lies on the x-axis. Check two values: <InlineMath math="-f(1) = -(2 - 1) = -1" /> and <InlineMath math="-f(-1) = -\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - 1\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}" />.

## Outside and Inside the Negative Sign

## Mistakes When Negating

The table lists **five mistakes**, why each one fails, and how to catch it.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negating only the first term, so that <InlineMath math="x^2 - 2x - 3" /> becomes <InlineMath math="-x^2 - 2x - 3" /> | Every term changes sign, so the correct result is <InlineMath math="-x^2 + 2x + 3" /> | Check the constant term, which must change from <InlineMath math="-3" /> to <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| Writing <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> for <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> | <InlineMath math="f(-x)" /> reflects across the y-axis instead | Test one point, such as <InlineMath math="(1, f(1))" />, and compare the sign of its height |
| Not swapping the range endpoints | A range <InlineMath math="[c, d]" /> becomes <InlineMath math="[-d, -c]" /> | Find the new range from the new highest and lowest points |
| Thinking the zeros move | The zeros of <InlineMath math="-f(x)" /> are the same as the zeros of <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> | Substitute an old zero into the new rule and check that it gives zero |
| Leaving the asymptote in place | An asymptote <InlineMath math="y = a" /> becomes <InlineMath math="y = -a" /> | Negate the height that the graph approaches |

## Exercises

Start each problem by **writing the whole output multiplied by negative one**. Then simplify, and check one point before you finish.

1. Let <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 4x + 3" />. Find the equation of its vertical reflection and the vertex of the reflection.
2. Let <InlineMath math="g(x) = 3^x + 2" />. Find the equation of its vertical reflection, its range, and its asymptote.
3. Let <InlineMath math="h(x) = \sqrt{x + 1}" />. Find the equation of its vertical reflection, its y-intercept, and its range.
4. The graph of <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^3 - 3x" /> contains <InlineMath math="(-1, 2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1, -2)" />. Find the images of these points under the vertical reflection, and show that <InlineMath math="f(-x) = -f(x)" />.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. **The whole output** is multiplied by negative one, so <InlineMath math="-f(x) = -x^2 - 4x - 3" />.
