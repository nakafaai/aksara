## Turning a Graph around a Fixed Point

<Highlight>Rotation is a geometric transformation that rotates an object around a specific center point with a determined rotation angle.</Highlight> In the context of functions, rotation changes the position of the function graph by rotating every point on the graph.

Imagine a wheel turning. Each point on the wheel moves along a circle centered at the axle. A graph rotation works the same way. Each point moves around the rotation center while keeping the same distance from it.

## Rotation Formula Around Center Point

To rotate point <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> around center <InlineMath math="(a, b)" /> through angle <InlineMath math="\theta" />, first find the point's coordinates relative to the center. Call these coordinates <InlineMath math="u" /> and <InlineMath math="v" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} u &amp;= x-a \\ v &amp;= y-b \end{aligned}" />

Rotate these relative coordinates, then add the center coordinates back. Adding <InlineMath math="a" /> to the horizontal coordinate and <InlineMath math="b" /> to the vertical coordinate places the rotated point in the original coordinate system:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x' &amp;= u\cos\theta-v\sin\theta+a \\ y' &amp;= u\sin\theta+v\cos\theta+b \end{aligned}" />

The symbols in these equations mean:
- <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> is the original point coordinate
- <InlineMath math="(x', y')" /> is the rotated point coordinate
- <InlineMath math="(a, b)" /> is the rotation center point
- <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the rotation angle (positive for counterclockwise direction)

## Special Rotation Around Origin

Rotation around the origin <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" /> is the case students meet first, because substituting <InlineMath math="a = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 0" /> removes the translation terms from the general formula. What remains is a pure change of direction, and the three quarter turns below cover most textbook problems.

Each step is a quarter turn counterclockwise, so applying the rule twice gives the half turn and applying it three times gives the three quarter turn.

1. <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> Rotation

    <BlockMath math="(x, y) \rightarrow (-y, x)" />

2. <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> Rotation

    <BlockMath math="(x, y) \rightarrow (-x, -y)" />

3. <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> Rotation

    <BlockMath math="(x, y) \rightarrow (y, -x)" />

## Properties of Function Rotation

Rotation moves a graph around a fixed point without changing its size, so the graph keeps its shape and only its orientation changes. The table below lists what stays fixed and what changes.

| Property | What happens | Why it holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | The shape of the graph does not change | Rotation moves every point by the same angle around the same center |
| Distances | The distance between two points stays the same | Rotating two points around one center preserves the segment that joins them |
| Angles between sides | Angles in the figure stay the same | A rotation is a rigid motion, so it never bends the figure |
| Orientation | The graph turns toward a new direction | Every point changes its position relative to the center |
| Compounding | Two rotations around the same center add their angles | Turning by <InlineMath math="\theta_1" /> and then by <InlineMath math="\theta_2" /> is a single turn by <InlineMath math="\theta_1 + \theta_2" /> |

Because distances and angles never change, a rotation is called a rigid motion. That single fact explains why shape and size survive, while position and direction are <Highlight>the only things that move</Highlight>.

## Application of Rotation to Various Functions

Rotation does not treat every curve the same way. A straight line and an exponential curve both keep their shape, but the rotated line stays a straight line while the rotated exponential curve changes which axis it approaches. The two cases below show that difference.

### Linear Function Rotation

For a linear function <InlineMath math="y = mx + c" />, every point moves to a new position, so <Highlight>slope and position change together</Highlight>. The <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> counterclockwise rotation around the origin shows how the original line becomes a second line.

## Steps to Determine Rotation Results

When you rotate a whole curve, the rotation formula applies to points, so the work runs point by point. Start by fixing the center and the angle, because both of them appear in every calculation that follows.

Next, choose several points on the original curve, including its endpoints and any turning point, and apply the rotation formula to each one. Plot the rotated points, join them in the original order, and check a few extra points between the samples. The result may no longer be a function of <InlineMath math="x" />, because two rotated points can share the same <InlineMath math="x" /> value.
