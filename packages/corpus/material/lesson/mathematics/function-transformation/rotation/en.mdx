## Turning Every Point of a Graph

A rotation of a function graph turns every point of the graph **through the same angle about one center of rotation**. A positive angle means a counterclockwise turn, and a negative angle means a clockwise turn. About the origin <InlineMath math="O(0, 0)" />, a rotation by <InlineMath math="\theta" /> moves the point <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> to its image <InlineMath math="(x', y')" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x' &= x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \\ y' &= x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta \end{aligned}" />

For multiples of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, the formula reduces to short rules. A turn of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> moves <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> to <InlineMath math="(-y, x)" />, a turn of <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> to <InlineMath math="(-x, -y)" />, and a turn of <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> to <InlineMath math="(y, -x)" />. On the interactive unit circle, you can change <InlineMath math="\theta" /> yourself and watch where the point <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> lands.

For a function graph, the rotation also changes the equation. To get the new equation, write the old coordinates in terms of the new ones and substitute those expressions into the original equation. The rotated curve **need not be the graph of a function**, because after the turn one value of <InlineMath math="x" /> can be paired with two values of <InlineMath math="y" />. The same turning rule moves points and plane shapes in [rotation as a geometric transformation](/en/subjects/mathematics/geometric-transformation/rotation).

## The Rotation Formula from the Unit Circle

Start with the point <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" />, which lies at distance <InlineMath math="1" /> from the origin. Because **a rotation keeps every distance to the center**, its image stays on the circle of radius <InlineMath math="1" />, at the end of the radius that makes the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> with the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis. On the unit circle, the point at the end of the radius with angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> has coordinates <InlineMath math="(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)" />. That is how cosine and sine are defined for any angle.

The angle field starts at <InlineMath math="60" />, so the end of the radius sits at <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)" />, matching the values <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac{1}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}" /> shown below the circle.

Now enter <InlineMath math="90" />, <InlineMath math="180" />, and <InlineMath math="270" />. The end of the radius stops at <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" />, <InlineMath math="(-1, 0)" />, and <InlineMath math="(0, -1)" />, and the point never leaves the circle. If you enter <InlineMath math="-90" />, the radius turns clockwise and stops at <InlineMath math="(0, -1)" />, the same place as a <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> turn.

The point <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" /> already makes an angle of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> with the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, so turning that point by <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the same as **reading the circle at the angle <InlineMath math="\theta + 90^\circ" />**. Enter <InlineMath math="150" />, which is <InlineMath math="60 + 90" />, and the end of the radius sits at <InlineMath math="\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)" />. That pair equals <InlineMath math="(-\sin 60^\circ, \cos 60^\circ)" />.

The same pattern holds for every angle, because <InlineMath math="\cos(\theta + 90^\circ) = -\sin\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin(\theta + 90^\circ) = \cos\theta" />, so the image of <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" /> is <InlineMath math="(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)" />.

These two images are enough to turn any point. You reach <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> from the origin by taking <InlineMath math="x" /> steps in the direction of <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> and then <InlineMath math="y" /> steps in the direction of <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" />. The rotation turns the rectangle formed by those steps as one rigid piece, so you reach the image by taking **the same numbers of steps along the turned directions**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} (x', y') &= x(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) + y(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) \\ &= (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta) \end{aligned}" />

The two components are the rotation formula about the origin. You can also write this formula as one product of a [rotation matrix](/en/subjects/mathematics/geometric-transformation/rotation-matrix) with the vector <InlineMath math="(x, y)" />.

As an example, the point <InlineMath math="P(2, 4)" /> on the graph of <InlineMath math="y = x^2" /> turns by <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> about the origin. The values <InlineMath math="\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac{1}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}" /> appear on the unit circle at <InlineMath math="\theta = 60^\circ" /> and in the table of [special angle values](/en/subjects/mathematics/trigonometry/trigonometric-comparison-special-angle). With <InlineMath math="x = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 4" />, the rotation formula gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x' &= 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} - 4 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 1 - 2\sqrt{3} \\ y' &= 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \sqrt{3} + 2 \end{aligned}" />

The image is <InlineMath math="P'(1 - 2\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{3} + 2)" />, with <InlineMath math="x' \approx -2.46" /> and <InlineMath math="y' \approx 3.73" />. The distance from <InlineMath math="P'" /> to the origin must equal the distance <InlineMath math="OP" />:

<BlockMath math="(1 - 2\sqrt{3})^2 + (\sqrt{3} + 2)^2 = (13 - 4\sqrt{3}) + (7 + 4\sqrt{3}) = 20 = 2^2 + 4^2" />

The direction checks out too. Since <InlineMath math="P" /> has <InlineMath math="y > x > 0" />, the angle of <InlineMath math="OP" /> with the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-axis lies between <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. Adding <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> puts that angle between <InlineMath math="105^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="150^\circ" />, in quadrant II, and <InlineMath math="P'" /> does have <InlineMath math="x' < 0" /> and <InlineMath math="y' > 0" />.

## Turns by Multiples of a Right Angle

At <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />, and <InlineMath math="270^\circ" />, the values of <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin\theta" /> are only <InlineMath math="0" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, or <InlineMath math="-1" />. The rotation formula then shrinks to **changing the signs of the coordinates**, and at <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> the two coordinates also swap places. For <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, substituting <InlineMath math="\cos 90^\circ = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 90^\circ = 1" /> gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x' &= x \cdot 0 - y \cdot 1 = -y \\ y' &= x \cdot 1 + y \cdot 0 = x \end{aligned}" />

In the same way, <InlineMath math="\cos 180^\circ = -1" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 180^\circ = 0" /> give <InlineMath math="(-x, -y)" />, while <InlineMath math="\cos 270^\circ = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 270^\circ = -1" /> give <InlineMath math="(y, -x)" />. The column for the image of <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> in the table is where the end of the radius sits on the unit circle at the same angle, so you can match every rule there.

| Angle | Image of <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> | Image of <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(-y, x)" /> | Quarter turn counterclockwise |
| <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="(-1, 0)" /> | <InlineMath math="(-x, -y)" /> | Half turn, the same in either direction |
| <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> | <InlineMath math="(0, -1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(y, -x)" /> | Same as <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" />, a quarter turn clockwise |

For instance, the point <InlineMath math="P(3, 4)" /> turns <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about the origin. The rule <InlineMath math="(x, y) \mapsto (-y, x)" /> gives:

<BlockMath math="P(3, 4) \longmapsto P'(-4, 3)" />

Check the distance and the angle. Both points lie at the same distance from <InlineMath math="O" />, because <InlineMath math="3^2 + 4^2 = (-4)^2 + 3^2 = 25" />. The slope of <InlineMath math="OP" /> is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{4}{3}" /> and the slope of <InlineMath math="OP'" /> is <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{3}{4}" />. Their product is <InlineMath math="-1" />, so <InlineMath math="OP" /> is perpendicular to <InlineMath math="OP'" />. The image <InlineMath math="P'" /> also lies in quadrant II, one quadrant counterclockwise from <InlineMath math="P" />.

A <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> turn changes the sign of both coordinates at once. ## Rotating about Another Center

The formulas <InlineMath math="x' = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="y' = x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta" /> only turn about the origin. For a center <InlineMath math="C(p, q)" />, first shift the plane so that <InlineMath math="C" /> lands on the origin, turn with the origin formula, and then shift the plane back. The difference <InlineMath math="(x - p, y - q)" /> is the position of the point as seen from <InlineMath math="C" />, and **that difference is what gets turned** before the coordinates of <InlineMath math="C" /> are added back:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x' &= p + (x - p)\cos\theta - (y - q)\sin\theta \\ y' &= q + (x - p)\sin\theta + (y - q)\cos\theta \end{aligned}" />

The center <InlineMath math="C" /> itself does not move, because the difference of <InlineMath math="C" /> from <InlineMath math="C" /> is <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />. Every other point moves on a circle centered at <InlineMath math="C" />, so its distance to <InlineMath math="C" /> stays the same.

Take the point <InlineMath math="P(2, 5)" /> turning <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about <InlineMath math="C(1, 1)" />. The difference of <InlineMath math="P" /> from <InlineMath math="C" /> is <InlineMath math="(2 - 1, 5 - 1) = (1, 4)" />. A quarter turn changes that difference by the rule <InlineMath math="(u, v) \mapsto (-v, u)" />, and then the coordinates of <InlineMath math="C" /> are added back:

<BlockMath math="(1, 4) \longmapsto (-4, 1), \qquad P' = (1 + (-4),\ 1 + 1) = (-3, 2)" />

The distance to the center does not change. Before the turn, <InlineMath math="|CP|^2 = 1^2 + 4^2 = 17" />. After it, <InlineMath math="|CP'|^2 = (-3 - 1)^2 + (2 - 1)^2 = 16 + 1 = 17" />. The slope of <InlineMath math="CP" /> is <InlineMath math="4" /> and the slope of <InlineMath math="CP'" /> is <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{1}{4}" />, with product <InlineMath math="-1" />, so the two segments are perpendicular. Turning <InlineMath math="P" /> about <InlineMath math="O" /> by mistake gives <InlineMath math="(-5, 2)" />. The distance of that point to <InlineMath math="C" /> changes from <InlineMath math="\sqrt{17}" /> to <InlineMath math="\sqrt{37}" />, which tells you the wrong center was used.

For an angle that is not a multiple of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, use the full formula. ## The Equation of a Rotated Curve

The rotation formula gives the image of one point, but the equation of the rotated curve needs the opposite direction. Every new point <InlineMath math="(x', y')" /> comes from an old point <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> that satisfies the original equation. You find that old point by **turning the new point back by <InlineMath math="-\theta" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} x &= x'\cos\theta + y'\sin\theta \\ y &= -x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta \end{aligned}" />

Substitute both expressions into the original equation, simplify, and then drop the primes. The primes only keep the new point apart from the old one during the calculation, so the final equation is written with <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> again.

For the parabola <InlineMath math="y = x^2" /> turned by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, the values <InlineMath math="\cos 90^\circ = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin 90^\circ = 1" /> give <InlineMath math="x = y'" /> and <InlineMath math="y = -x'" />. Substituting into <InlineMath math="y = x^2" /> gives:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y &= x^2 \\ -x' &= (y')^2 \\ x' &= -(y')^2 \end{aligned}" />

After dropping the primes, the rotated curve is **the parabola <InlineMath math="x = -y^2" />, which opens to the left**. Check one point: <InlineMath math="(2, 4)" /> on the original parabola moves to <InlineMath math="(-4, 2)" /> under the rule <InlineMath math="(x, y) \mapsto (-y, x)" />, and that point satisfies <InlineMath math="-4 = -(2)^2" />.

The line <InlineMath math="y = 2x + 1" /> turned by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> works the same way, with <InlineMath math="x = y'" /> and <InlineMath math="y = -x'" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} -x' &= 2y' + 1 \\ y' &= -\frac{x' + 1}{2} \end{aligned}" />

The image line is <InlineMath math="y = -\tfrac{x + 1}{2}" />, or <InlineMath math="y = -\tfrac{1}{2}x - \tfrac{1}{2}" />. Its slope <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{1}{2}" /> is the negative reciprocal of the original slope <InlineMath math="2" />, as expected for a line turned a quarter turn. The point <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-1, 0)" /> and the point <InlineMath math="(1, 3)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-3, 1)" />. Both satisfy the new equation, since <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{-1 + 1}{2} = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{-3 + 1}{2} = 1" />.

For a <InlineMath math="45^\circ" /> turn, the values <InlineMath math="\cos 45^\circ = \sin 45^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}" /> give <InlineMath math="x = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x' + y')" /> and <InlineMath math="y = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(y' - x')" />. <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(y' - x') &= \sqrt{2}(x' + y') + 1 \\ y' - x' &= 2x' + 2y' + \sqrt{2} \\ y' &= -3x' - \sqrt{2} \end{aligned}" />

## Mistakes with Direction and Center

A rotation keeps every point's distance to the center, and a turn by <InlineMath math="\theta" /> about the origin always takes <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> to <InlineMath math="(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)" />. So you can **test a rotation with one easy point** before you use the result. The image of <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> checks the direction, the distance to the center checks the center, and the image of one point of the curve checks the new equation.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to catch it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using <InlineMath math="(y, -x)" /> for a <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise turn | That rule turns <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> clockwise, the same as <InlineMath math="270^\circ" /> | The point <InlineMath math="(1, 0)" /> must land at <InlineMath math="(0, 1)" />, not at <InlineMath math="(0, -1)" /> |
| Turning about the origin when the center is <InlineMath math="C(p, q)" /> | The formula without <InlineMath math="p" /> and <InlineMath math="q" /> only keeps the distance to <InlineMath math="O" /> | The distance to <InlineMath math="C" /> must be the same before and after the turn |
| Swapping <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> and <InlineMath math="\sin\theta" /> | The <InlineMath math="x" />-coordinate of the end of the radius is <InlineMath math="\cos\theta" /> | At <InlineMath math="\theta = 60^\circ" />, the end of the radius is at <InlineMath math="\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)" /> |
| Substituting the forward rule into the original equation | The result is the curve turned the other way, such as <InlineMath math="x = y^2" /> for <InlineMath math="y = x^2" /> | The image of <InlineMath math="(2, 4)" />, which is <InlineMath math="(-4, 2)" />, must satisfy the new equation |
| Assuming the rotated curve is always a function | A <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> turn can give two values of <InlineMath math="y" /> for one <InlineMath math="x" /> | Apply the vertical line test to the rotated curve |

## Exercises

In problem 1 you turn a single point, in problem 2 a line, and in problem 3 a parabola, each about the origin. Problem 4 asks you to turn a parabola about another center. Write down the center and the angle before you calculate, and **test your answer with the distance to the center or with one point** before you read the worked solutions.

1. Find the image of the point <InlineMath math="A(-2, 5)" /> under a rotation by <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" /> about the origin.

2. The line <InlineMath math="y = 3x - 2" /> is turned <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about the origin. Find the equation of the image line.

3. The parabola <InlineMath math="y = x^2 - 4" /> is turned <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> about the origin. Find the equation of the rotated curve and check whether it is still the graph of a function.

4. The parabola <InlineMath math="y = (x - 3)^2 + 1" /> is turned <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> counterclockwise about <InlineMath math="C(1, 2)" />. Find the image of its vertex and the equation of the rotated curve, then check whether the rotated curve is still the graph of a function.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. An angle of <InlineMath math="-90^\circ" /> means **a quarter turn clockwise**, the same as <InlineMath math="270^\circ" />. The rule is <InlineMath math="(x, y) \mapsto (y, -x)" />:

<BlockMath math="A(-2, 5) \longmapsto A'(5, -(-2)) = A'(5, 2)" />

The distance to the origin stays the same, because <InlineMath math="(-2)^2 + 5^2 = 5^2 + 2^2 = 29" />. The slope of <InlineMath math="OA" /> is <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{5}{2}" /> and the slope of <InlineMath math="OA'" /> is <InlineMath math="\tfrac{2}{5}" />, with product <InlineMath math="-1" />. The point <InlineMath math="A" /> lies in quadrant II, and a quarter turn clockwise carries <InlineMath math="A" /> into quadrant I. So the image of <InlineMath math="A" /> is <InlineMath math="A'(5, 2)" />.

**Problem 2**. For a <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> turn, the old point is written through the new one as <InlineMath math="x = y'" /> and <InlineMath math="y = -x'" />. Substitute into <InlineMath math="y = 3x - 2" /> and **solve for <InlineMath math="y'" />**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} -x' &= 3y' - 2 \\ 3y' &= -x' + 2 \\ y' &= -\tfrac{1}{3}x' + \tfrac{2}{3} \end{aligned}" />

Check with two points of the original line. The point <InlineMath math="(0, -2)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(2, 0)" />, and <InlineMath math="-\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 0" />. The point <InlineMath math="(1, 1)" /> moves to <InlineMath math="(-1, 1)" />, and <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 1" />. So the image line is <InlineMath math="y = -\tfrac{1}{3}x + \tfrac{2}{3}" />.

**Problem 4**. The vertex of the original parabola is <InlineMath math="V(3, 1)" />, and its difference from <InlineMath math="C(1, 2)" /> is <InlineMath math="(2, -1)" />. A quarter turn changes that difference into <InlineMath math="(1, 2)" />, and then the coordinates of <InlineMath math="C" /> are added back:

<BlockMath math="(2, -1) \longmapsto (1, 2), \qquad V' = (1 + 1,\ 2 + 2) = (2, 4)" />

The distance to the center is unchanged, because <InlineMath math="|CV|^2 = 2^2 + (-1)^2 = 5" /> and <InlineMath math="|CV'|^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 = 5" />. For the equation of the curve, **write the old point through the new one**. A <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> turn about <InlineMath math="C" /> gives <InlineMath math="x' = 1 - (y - 2)" /> and <InlineMath math="y' = 2 + (x - 1)" />, so <InlineMath math="x = y' - 1" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 3 - x'" />. Substitute into <InlineMath math="y = (x - 3)^2 + 1" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 3 - x' &= \left((y' - 1) - 3\right)^2 + 1 \\ 3 - x' &= (y' - 4)^2 + 1 \\ x' &= 2 - (y' - 4)^2 \end{aligned}" />

Both satisfy the new equation, since <InlineMath math="2 - (3 - 4)^2 = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="2 - (5 - 4)^2 = 1" />.
