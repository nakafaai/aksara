## What Does Non-Mutually Exclusive Mean?

We learned about [mutually exclusive events](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/two-events-mutually-exclusive) that can't happen together (like turning left and right at the same time). Now, let's talk about **Non-Mutually Exclusive Events**. These are two (or more) events that **CAN happen at the same time** in a single experiment.

This means it's possible to get an outcome that belongs to event <InlineMath math="A" /> and also belongs to event <InlineMath math="B" />.

**Simple Examples:**

1.  **Drawing a Card:** You draw one card from a standard deck.

    - Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Getting a **Heart** (<InlineMath math="\heartsuit" />).
    - Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Getting a **King**.
      Can events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> happen together? Absolutely! There's a card that is both a Heart and a King: the **King of Hearts** (<InlineMath math="K\heartsuit" />). Since they can happen together, events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **non-mutually exclusive**.

2.  **Rolling a Die (once):**
    - Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Getting an **even** number (<InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 6\}" />).
    - Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Getting a number **greater than** <InlineMath math="3" /> (<InlineMath math="\{4, 5, 6\}" />).
      Can these happen together? Yes! The numbers <InlineMath math="4" /> and <InlineMath math="6" /> are both even and greater than <InlineMath math="3" />. So, events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **non-mutually exclusive**.

## Why the Intersection Matters

For non-mutually exclusive events, some outcomes may belong to both <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />. These shared outcomes form the **intersection** <InlineMath math="A \cap B" />.

The definition is about the outcome sets themselves: the two events are not mutually exclusive when their intersection is nonempty.

<BlockMath math="A \cap B \ne \varnothing" />

In a finite sample space where every elementary outcome has positive probability, a nonempty intersection also has positive probability:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) > 0" />

This is very different from mutually exclusive events, where <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />.

## Calculating Combined Probability for Non-Mutually Exclusive Events

Because <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> may occur together, we cannot simply add <InlineMath math="P(A) + P(B)" /> to find <InlineMath math="P(A \text{ or} B)" />.

That sum would **count the intersection** <InlineMath math="A \cap B" /> **twice**, once in <InlineMath math="P(A)" /> and once in <InlineMath math="P(B)" />.

We therefore subtract the probability of the intersection once. This gives the **general addition rule**:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ or} B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" />

Using union and intersection symbols:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" />

This formula works generally, for both mutually exclusive and non-mutually exclusive events. (If they are mutually exclusive, <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> is zero, so the formula simplifies back to <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />).

## Calculation Example

Let's use the card example:

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Getting a Heart (<InlineMath math="\heartsuit" />). There are <InlineMath math="13" /> Hearts among the <InlineMath math="52" /> cards. <InlineMath math="P(A) = 13/52" />.
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Getting a King. There are <InlineMath math="4" /> Kings among the <InlineMath math="52" /> cards. <InlineMath math="P(B) = 4/52" />.
- Event <InlineMath math="A" /> **and** B: Getting the King of Hearts (<InlineMath math="K\heartsuit" />). There is only <InlineMath math="1" /> King of Hearts. <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 1/52" />.

So, the probability of getting a Heart OR a King is:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{13}{52} + \frac{4}{52} - \frac{1}{52}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}" />
</MathContainer>

See? We subtract <InlineMath math="1/52" /> so the King of Hearts isn't counted twice.

## Exercise

A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Let <InlineMath math="A=\{2,4,6\}" /> be the event “the result is even” and <InlineMath math="B=\{5,6\}" /> the event “the result is greater than <InlineMath math="4" />.”

1.  Explain why <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are not mutually exclusive.
2.  Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" /> using the general addition rule.
3.  Check the result by listing the outcomes in the union.

### Worked Solution

The intersection contains **one shared outcome**, namely <InlineMath math="6" />:

<BlockMath math="A \cap B=\{6\}" />

The events are therefore **not mutually exclusive**. Their probabilities and the intersection probability are

<BlockMath math="P(A)=\frac{3}{6},\qquad P(B)=\frac{2}{6},\qquad P(A \cap B)=\frac{1}{6}" />

Apply the general addition rule:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A \cap B)" />
  <BlockMath math="=\frac{3}{6}+\frac{2}{6}-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

Directly listing the union gives <InlineMath math="A \cup B=\{2,4,5,6\}" />, which also contains <InlineMath math="4" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> possible outcomes. Both methods therefore give <InlineMath math="2/3" />.
