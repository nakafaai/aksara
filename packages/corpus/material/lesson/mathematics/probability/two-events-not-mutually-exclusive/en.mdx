## The Probability of A or B When Both Can Happen Together

Two events are **not mutually exclusive** when both can happen on the same outcome, that is, their intersection is not empty, <InlineMath math="A \cap B \ne \varnothing" />. For two events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />, the probability of <InlineMath math="A" /> or <InlineMath math="B" /> comes from the [addition rule](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/addition-rule):

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" />

The intersection is subtracted **once** because its outcomes are already counted in <InlineMath math="P(A)" /> and counted again in <InlineMath math="P(B)" />.

For the die, <InlineMath math="A = \{2, 4, 6\}" /> and <InlineMath math="B = \{4, 5, 6\}" />, so <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \{4, 6\}" />. The formula gives <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} - \frac{2}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}" />. Check by listing: <InlineMath math="A \cup B = \{2, 4, 5, 6\}" /> holds <InlineMath math="4" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> results.

## Recognizing Events That Overlap

You can check for an overlap by writing down **the outcomes that meet both conditions at once**. If there is at least one such outcome, the events are not mutually exclusive.

| Experiment | Event <InlineMath math="A" /> | Event <InlineMath math="B" /> | Intersection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Draw one playing card | A heart | A king | The king of hearts |
| Roll one die | <InlineMath math="\{2,4,6\}" /> | <InlineMath math="\{4,5,6\}" /> | <InlineMath math="\{4,6\}" /> |
| Pick one student | Likes mathematics | Likes physics | Likes both |

The opposite case is [mutually exclusive events](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/two-events-mutually-exclusive), whose intersection is empty, so <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />. The formula <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" /> still holds for mutually exclusive events, because the intersection term is zero.

## Why the Intersection Is Subtracted Once

Count with the numbers of elements first. When you add <InlineMath math="n(A)" /> and <InlineMath math="n(B)" />, every element of the intersection enters both counts, so it is counted **twice**. Subtract the intersection once so that every element is recorded exactly once:

<BlockMath math="n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)" />

Divide every term by <InlineMath math="n(S)" /> for a sample space whose outcomes are equally likely, and you get the probability formula. The probability formula itself holds for any sample space, and only the division by <InlineMath math="n(S)" /> needs equally likely outcomes. For the die, <InlineMath math="3 + 3 - 2 = 4" /> elements, the same as the list <InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 5, 6\}" />. Without the subtraction, the value is <InlineMath math="\frac{6}{6} = 1" />, as if every roll landed in <InlineMath math="A" /> or <InlineMath math="B" />, although the results <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> belong to neither.

## Example with Survey Data

Of <InlineMath math="40" /> students, <InlineMath math="25" /> like mathematics, <InlineMath math="18" /> like physics, and <InlineMath math="10" /> like both. One student is picked at random. Let <InlineMath math="M" /> be the event that the student likes mathematics and <InlineMath math="F" /> the event that the student likes physics. Find the probability that the student likes mathematics or physics, and the probability that the student likes neither.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(M \cup F) = \frac{25}{40} + \frac{18}{40} - \frac{10}{40} = \frac{33}{40}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{neither}) = 1 - \frac{33}{40} = \frac{7}{40}" />

The second probability uses the **complement**: a student who likes neither is every student outside <InlineMath math="M \cup F" />. Check with the Venn regions: <InlineMath math="25 - 10 = 15" /> like only mathematics, <InlineMath math="18 - 10 = 8" /> like only physics, and <InlineMath math="10" /> like both. Fill the Venn diagram from the inside out: <InlineMath math="10" /> students like both, so <InlineMath math="25 - 10 = 15" /> like only mathematics and <InlineMath math="18 - 10 = 8" /> only physics. ## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adding <InlineMath math="P(A) + P(B)" /> without subtracting the intersection | Outcomes in the intersection are counted twice | For the die, the value is <InlineMath math="1" />, although <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" /> belong to neither event |
| Subtracting the intersection twice | The intersection is only counted once too often | Count the elements of the union by listing them |
| Treating not mutually exclusive as independent | Not mutually exclusive is about the intersection, independence is about <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)" /> | Check the two conditions separately |
| Forgetting a card that belongs to both events | The king of hearts is both a heart and a king | Write out the intersection before you compute |
| Getting a probability above <InlineMath math="1" /> | Adding without subtracting can pass <InlineMath math="1" /> | The probability of a union is never more than <InlineMath math="1" /> |

The simplest check is **counting each region of the Venn diagram** and adding them. The total must match the formula.

## Practice Problems

Write out the intersection first, then **check your answer by listing or by counting the Venn regions**.

1. A die is rolled once. <InlineMath math="A" /> is the event of a prime number and <InlineMath math="B" /> the event of an odd number. Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />.

2. One card is drawn from a deck of <InlineMath math="52" />. Find the probability of drawing a red card or an ace.

3. Given <InlineMath math="P(A) = 0.5" />, <InlineMath math="P(B) = 0.4" />, and <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = 0.7" />, find <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" />, then decide whether <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive.

4. One number is picked at random from <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="30" />. Find the probability that it is divisible by <InlineMath math="3" /> or by <InlineMath math="5" />.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. <InlineMath math="A = \{2, 3, 5\}" />, <InlineMath math="B = \{1, 3, 5\}" />, and <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \{3, 5\}" />:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} - \frac{2}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="A \cup B = \{1, 2, 3, 5\}" /> holds <InlineMath math="4" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> results.

**Problem 2**. There are <InlineMath math="26" /> red cards, <InlineMath math="4" /> aces, and <InlineMath math="2" /> red aces (hearts and diamonds):

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{26}{52} + \frac{4}{52} - \frac{2}{52} = \frac{28}{52} = \frac{7}{13}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="26" /> red cards plus the <InlineMath math="2" /> black aces make <InlineMath math="28" /> cards.

**Problem 3**. Rearrange the formula:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.7 = 0.2" />

Since <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.2 \ne 0" />, the events are **not mutually exclusive**. Check: <InlineMath math="0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7" />, the given probability of the union.

**Problem 4**. There are <InlineMath math="10" /> multiples of <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="6" /> multiples of <InlineMath math="5" />, and <InlineMath math="2" /> multiples of both, that is of <InlineMath math="15" /> (<InlineMath math="15" /> and <InlineMath math="30" />):

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{10}{30} + \frac{6}{30} - \frac{2}{30} = \frac{14}{30} = \frac{7}{15}" />

Check by listing: <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="6" />, <InlineMath math="9" />, <InlineMath math="10" />, <InlineMath math="12" />, <InlineMath math="15" />, <InlineMath math="18" />, <InlineMath math="20" />, <InlineMath math="21" />, <InlineMath math="24" />, <InlineMath math="25" />, <InlineMath math="27" />, <InlineMath math="30" />, which is <InlineMath math="14" /> numbers.
