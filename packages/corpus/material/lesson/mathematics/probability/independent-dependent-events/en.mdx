## When Chance Stays Put

Picture tossing a coin and then rolling a die. The coin can't whisper to the die, so the coin's result never changes the die's chances. By contrast, if you pull a sweet from a jar and eat it, the jar changes for the next pick.

Two events are **independent** when <InlineMath math="A" /> happening doesn't change the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />. The probability that both happen is the product of their probabilities:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />

Two events are **dependent** when <InlineMath math="A" /> happening changes the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />. You still multiply, but the second factor uses **the probability of <InlineMath math="B" /> after <InlineMath math="A" /> happens**, written <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A)" />:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)" />

> Two events are independent only when the first trial's result leaves the sample space for the second trial untouched.

For independent events, <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A)" /> equals <InlineMath math="P(B)" />, so the second formula simplifies to the first. The area diagram below shows why multiplication works for independent events.

## Testing Two Trials

To decide whether two events are independent, check whether **the first result changes the second event's chance**. Let's test that rule on dice rolls and separate it from the mutually exclusive trap.

### Memoryless Dice Rolls

When you roll two dice, the first die has no effect on the second. The probability that both show <InlineMath math="6" /> for a sum of <InlineMath math="12" /> comes straight from multiplying:

<BlockMath math="P(6 \text{ and } 6) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36}" />

Now compare that with a single die roll. Let <InlineMath math="A" /> be getting an even number and <InlineMath math="B" /> be getting a number above <InlineMath math="3" />. Their overlap is <InlineMath math="\{4, 6\}" />, so compare these two values:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{3}{6} \times \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{4}" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}" /> doesn't equal <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{4}" />, the two events are **dependent**. Check the meaning: once you know the roll is even, only <InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 6\}" /> remain, so <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A)" /> jumps to <InlineMath math="\frac{2}{3}" />.

### Not Mutually Exclusive

People often confuse independent events with [mutually exclusive events](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/two-events-mutually-exclusive), yet they pull in opposite directions. When two mutually exclusive events both have nonzero chance, <InlineMath math="A" /> happening forces **the chance of <InlineMath math="B" /> straight to zero**.

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0 \neq P(A) \times P(B)" />

Because their product is positive while their overlap is zero, two such mutually exclusive events are always dependent. Compare four everyday situations below before choosing a formula.

| Situation | Type | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Tossing a coin twice | Independent | The coin never remembers the first toss |
| Drawing two marbles with replacement | Independent | The first marble goes back, keeping the bag unchanged |
| Drawing two marbles without replacement | Dependent | The bag loses one marble, so the fractions shift |
| Picking two students for two distinct roles | Dependent | The first student chosen can't be picked again |

The flowchart below maps out the quick check to run before multiplying probabilities.

The same rule runs two factory machines that break down independently. Say machine <InlineMath math="A" /> works with probability <InlineMath math="0.9" /> and machine <InlineMath math="B" /> with <InlineMath math="0.8" />. For **at least one machine working**, the complement is fastest:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.9 \times 0.8 = 0.72" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{both fail}) = 0.1 \times 0.2 = 0.02" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{at least one works}) = 1 - 0.02 = 0.98" />
</MathContainer>

Check <InlineMath math="0.98" /> by summing the three separate cases where at least one machine runs:

<BlockMath math="0.9 \times 0.8 + 0.9 \times 0.2 + 0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.72 + 0.18 + 0.08 = 0.98" />

Both routes land on the exact same answer, and the complement saves you from dropping a case.

## Put the Rules to Work

Before solving these three problems, check **whether the sample space shifts** on the second step. If nothing goes back in, lower the denominator by one, then verify every result with the path sum or complement.

1. A box holds <InlineMath math="4" /> blue cards and <InlineMath math="6" /> yellow cards. Two cards are drawn one after another without replacement. Find the probability of two yellow cards and the probability of two different colours.

2. Events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are independent with <InlineMath math="P(A) = 0.3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(B) = 0.6" />. Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> and <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />.

3. An archer hits the target with probability <InlineMath math="0.8" /> on each independent shot and shoots three times. Find the probability that all three hit and the probability that at least one misses.

**Problem 1**. These draws are dependent because the first card stays out, so the card total drops from <InlineMath math="10" /> to <InlineMath math="9" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{yellow, yellow}) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac{1}{3}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{different colours}) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{6}{9} + \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{48}{90} = \frac{8}{15}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the remaining path, two blue cards:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{blue, blue}) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{3}{9} = \frac{12}{90}" />
  <BlockMath math="\frac{30 + 48 + 12}{90} = \frac{90}{90} = 1" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 2**. Multiply for the overlap, then apply the [addition rule](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/addition-rule) for the union:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.18" />
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.6 - 0.18 = 0.72" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the complement where neither <InlineMath math="A" /> nor <InlineMath math="B" /> occurs:

<BlockMath math="1 - (0.7 \times 0.4) = 1 - 0.28 = 0.72" />

**Problem 3**. All three shots are independent, so multiply three times for all hits, then take the complement for at least one miss:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{three hits}) = 0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.512" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{at least one miss}) = 1 - 0.512 = 0.488" />
</MathContainer>

Quick check: the two events are complements, and <InlineMath math="0.512 + 0.488" /> adds right back to <InlineMath math="1" />.
