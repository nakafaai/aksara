## The Probability That A and B Both Happen

Two events are **independent** when <InlineMath math="A" /> happening does not change the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />. For independent events, the probability that both happen is the product of their probabilities:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />

Two events are **dependent** when <InlineMath math="A" /> happening changes the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />. You still multiply, but the second factor is the probability of <InlineMath math="B" /> **after** <InlineMath math="A" /> has happened, written <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A)" />:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)" />

For independent events, <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A) = P(B)" />, so the second formula becomes the first. For example, a coin and a die are tossed. Getting heads does not change the chance that the die shows <InlineMath math="6" />, so <InlineMath math="P(\text{heads and } 6) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{12}" />. Check by listing: there are <InlineMath math="2 \times 6 = 12" /> equally likely outcomes, and only one of them is heads and <InlineMath math="6" />.

## Spotting Dependent Events

Check whether **the first outcome changes the sample space** for the second trial. If it does, the events are dependent.

| Situation | Type | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toss a coin twice | Independent | The coin does not remember the first toss |
| Draw two marbles **with** replacement | Independent | The first marble goes back, so the bag is the same |
| Draw two marbles **without** replacement | Dependent | The bag loses a marble, so the fractions change |
| Choose two students for two different posts | Dependent | The student chosen first cannot be chosen again |

Independent events are **not** the same as [mutually exclusive events](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/two-events-mutually-exclusive). If <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive and both have probability greater than <InlineMath math="0" />, then <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />, but <InlineMath math="P(A) \times P(B) > 0" />. So such mutually exclusive events are always dependent: once <InlineMath math="A" /> happens, the chance of <InlineMath math="B" /> drops to <InlineMath math="0" />.

## Example with Two Independent Machines

A factory has two machines that break down independently. The probability that machine <InlineMath math="A" /> works on a given day is <InlineMath math="0.9" />, and for machine <InlineMath math="B" /> it is <InlineMath math="0.8" />. Find the probability that **both** machines work, and the probability that **at least one** machine works.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.9 \times 0.8 = 0.72" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{both fail}) = 0.1 \times 0.2 = 0.02" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{at least one works}) = 1 - 0.02 = 0.98" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the three cases where at least one machine works: <InlineMath math="0.9 \times 0.8 + 0.9 \times 0.2 + 0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.72 + 0.18 + 0.08 = 0.98" />.

## Example Testing Whether Two Events Are Independent

A die is rolled once. <InlineMath math="A" /> is the event of getting an even number and <InlineMath math="B" /> is the event of getting a number greater than <InlineMath math="3" />. Are <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> independent?

Compare <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> with <InlineMath math="P(A) \times P(B)" />. The intersection is <InlineMath math="\{4, 6\}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{3}{6} \times \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{4}" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3} \ne \frac{1}{4}" />, the events are **dependent**. Check with the meaning: if the result is known to be even, the choices are <InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 6\}" />, and two of them are greater than <InlineMath math="3" />. So <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A) = \frac{2}{3}" />, not <InlineMath math="P(B) = \frac{1}{2}" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using the same fraction on the second draw without replacement | The bag has already lost a marble | The second-stage denominator must drop by one |
| Treating mutually exclusive as independent | Mutually exclusive gives <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />, not <InlineMath math="P(A)P(B)" /> | Compare <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> with <InlineMath math="P(A) \times P(B)" /> |
| Adding probabilities along one branch | One tree path means both events happen, so the probabilities multiply | A path's product cannot exceed its first branch |
| Multiplying the probabilities of two different paths | Different paths cannot happen together, so their probabilities add | All paths must add up to <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Counting at least one by listing only some cases | A missing case is easy to overlook | Use the complement: <InlineMath math="1 - P(\text{none})" /> |

The strongest check is **adding up every path of the tree**. If the total is not <InlineMath math="1" />, a branch is wrong.

## Practice Questions

Decide first whether the events are dependent, then **check each answer with the path total or the complement**.

1. Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that both dice show <InlineMath math="6" />.

2. A box holds <InlineMath math="4" /> blue cards and <InlineMath math="6" /> yellow cards. Two cards are drawn one after the other without replacement. Find the probability that both cards are yellow, and the probability that the cards are different colours.

3. <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are independent events with <InlineMath math="P(A) = 0.3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(B) = 0.6" />. Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> and <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />.

4. An archer hits the target with probability <InlineMath math="0.8" /> on each shot, and the shots are independent. The archer shoots three times. Find the probability that all three shots hit, and the probability that at least one shot misses.

### Solutions

Only question 2 is **dependent**, because its cards are not returned. The other three involve independent events.

**Question 1**. The two dice are independent:

<BlockMath math="P(6 \text{ and } 6) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36}" />

Check: of the <InlineMath math="36" /> result pairs, only <InlineMath math="(6, 6)" /> fits.

**Question 2**. The draws are dependent because the first card is not returned:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{yellow, yellow}) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac{1}{3}" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{different colours}) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{6}{9} + \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{48}{90} = \frac{8}{15}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="P(\text{blue, blue}) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{3}{9} = \frac{12}{90}" />, and <InlineMath math="\frac{30 + 48 + 12}{90} = 1" />.

**Question 3**. Multiply for the intersection, then use the [addition rule](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/addition-rule):

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.18" />
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.6 - 0.18 = 0.72" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the complement: <InlineMath math="P(\text{not } A \text{ and not } B) = 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.28" />, and <InlineMath math="1 - 0.28 = 0.72" />.

**Question 4**. The three shots are independent:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{three hits}) = 0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.512" />
  <BlockMath math="P(\text{at least one miss}) = 1 - 0.512 = 0.488" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the two events are complements of each other, and <InlineMath math="0.512 + 0.488 = 1" />.
