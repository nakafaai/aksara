## Counting Outcomes That Belong to Either Event

Suppose we ask everyone in a class how they traveled to school. We then choose one student at random and ask for the probability that the student came by **bicycle OR motorcycle**.

The word **"OR"** joins the two events. The addition rule calculates the probability that event <InlineMath math="A" /> occurs, event <InlineMath math="B" /> occurs, or both occur when an overlap is possible.

Two cases determine which formula to use:

1.  The events cannot happen in the same trial. These are **mutually exclusive** events.
2.  The events can happen in the same trial. These events are **not mutually exclusive**.

The difference is whether the two events have an overlap.

## Mutually Exclusive Events

Mutually exclusive events, also called _disjoint_ events, **cannot occur together** in a single trial.

If event <InlineMath math="A" /> occurs, event <InlineMath math="B" /> does not occur, and vice versa.

**Examples**:

1.  One coin toss produces either "Heads" or "Tails," never both at once.
2.  Zain traveled to school by motorcycle **OR** by bicycle, assuming each student used one mode of transport.

Rolling two dice: The event "the sum is <InlineMath math="7" />" **OR** the event "getting doubles (same number on both dice)".

| Event | Outcome pairs |
| --- | --- |
| Sum is <InlineMath math="7" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,6)" />, <InlineMath math="(2,5)" />, <InlineMath math="(3,4)" />, <InlineMath math="(4,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(5,2)" />, <InlineMath math="(6,1)" /> |
| Doubles | <InlineMath math="(1,1)" />, <InlineMath math="(2,2)" />, <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(4,4)" />, <InlineMath math="(5,5)" />, <InlineMath math="(6,6)" /> |

No ordered pair belongs to both groups, so the events are mutually exclusive.

### Formula for Mutually Exclusive Events

If events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive, their intersection is empty. The probability of "A OR B" is therefore the sum of their individual probabilities.

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

**Worked example**:

For the two-dice experiment above:

- Probability of sum being <InlineMath math="7" /> (<InlineMath math="P(S=7)" />): there are <InlineMath math="6" /> pairs out of <InlineMath math="36" /> total, so the probability is <InlineMath math="6/36" />
- Probability of getting doubles (<InlineMath math="P(\text{Doubles})" />): there are <InlineMath math="6" /> pairs out of <InlineMath math="36" /> total, so the probability is <InlineMath math="6/36" />

The probability of obtaining a sum of <InlineMath math="7" /> OR doubles is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}P(S=7\text{ or Doubles}) &= P(S=7)+P(\text{Doubles}) \\ &= \frac{6}{36}+\frac{6}{36} \\ &= \frac{12}{36}=\frac{1}{3}\end{aligned}" />

## Not Mutually Exclusive Events

Events that are not mutually exclusive **can occur together** in one trial, so their outcome sets overlap.

**Examples**:

1.  Draw one card from a standard deck. The events "drawing an Ace" and "drawing a Heart (<InlineMath math="\heartsuit" />)" overlap at the Ace of Hearts (<InlineMath math="A\heartsuit" />).

Rolling two dice: The event "the sum is <InlineMath math="8" />" **OR** the event "getting doubles".

| Event | Outcome pairs |
| --- | --- |
| Sum is <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,6)" />, <InlineMath math="(3,5)" />, <InlineMath math="(4,4)" />, <InlineMath math="(5,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(6,2)" /> |
| Doubles | <InlineMath math="(1,1)" />, <InlineMath math="(2,2)" />, <InlineMath math="(3,3)" />, <InlineMath math="(4,4)" />, <InlineMath math="(5,5)" />, <InlineMath math="(6,6)" /> |

The pair <InlineMath math="(4,4)" /> belongs to both groups. The events are therefore **not mutually exclusive**.

**Why simple addition is not enough**

Adding <InlineMath math="P(A) + P(B)" /> would count the intersection twice: once as part of <InlineMath math="A" /> and once as part of <InlineMath math="B" />.

For the "sum is <InlineMath math="8" /> OR doubles" example:

- <InlineMath math="P(S=8)" />: there are <InlineMath math="5" /> pairs, so the probability is <InlineMath math="5/36" />
- <InlineMath math="P(\text{Doubles})" /> is <InlineMath math="6" /> pairs, namely <InlineMath math="6/36" />

Direct addition gives <InlineMath math="5/36 + 6/36 = 11/36" />, but the pair <InlineMath math="(4,4)" /> appears in both groups. Subtracting its probability once removes the duplicate count.

On the grid of two dice, the doubles run along one diagonal and the sums of <InlineMath math="8" /> along another. They cross at <InlineMath math="(4, 4)" />, a double whose sum is <InlineMath math="8" />. Counting both lists would count that dot twice, so <InlineMath math="P = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{5}{36} - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{10}{36}" />.

### Formula for Not Mutually Exclusive Events

For events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> that are not mutually exclusive, the way to calculate the probability of "A OR B" is:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)" />

The term <InlineMath math="P(A \text{ and } B)" /> is the probability of the intersection, where <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> occur together. Subtracting it once <Highlight>corrects the double count</Highlight>.

**Worked example**:

Continue with the "sum is <InlineMath math="8" /> OR doubles" example:

- <InlineMath math="P(S=8) = 5/36" />
- <InlineMath math="P(\text{Doubles}) = 6/36" />
- The probability of the event "sum is <InlineMath math="8" /> AND doubles" (<InlineMath math="P(S=8 \text{ and Doubles})" />) is just one outcome: the pair <InlineMath math="(4, 4)" />. So its probability is <InlineMath math="1/36" />.

The probability of getting a sum of <InlineMath math="8" /> OR doubles is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}P(S=8\text{ or Doubles}) &= P(S=8)+P(\text{Doubles}) \\ &\quad-P(S=8\text{ and Doubles}) \\ &= \frac{5}{36}+\frac{6}{36}-\frac{1}{36} \\ &= \frac{10}{36}=\frac{5}{18}\end{aligned}" />

**How mathematics uses "OR"**

In mathematics, "<InlineMath math="A" /> or <InlineMath math="B" />" includes three possibilities: only <InlineMath math="A" />, only <InlineMath math="B" />, or both when an overlap exists. This is an **inclusive** use of "or," unlike everyday expressions that may require choosing exactly one alternative.

## When to Use Which Formula

The two formulas differ in one question, whether the events can happen in the **same trial**. The table matches each answer to the formula it selects.

| Can the events happen in the same trial? | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| No, the events cannot overlap | <InlineMath math="P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)" /> |
| Yes, the events overlap | <InlineMath math="P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)" /> |

The second formula subtracts the overlap because the outcomes that belong to both events were counted twice, once for <InlineMath math="A" /> and once for <InlineMath math="B" />.

With an overlap the bars no longer stack. Doubles have <InlineMath math="\frac{6}{36}" /> and sums of <InlineMath math="8" /> have <InlineMath math="\frac{5}{36}" />, but <InlineMath math="(4, 4)" /> belongs to both, so either one has <InlineMath math="\frac{6}{36} + \frac{5}{36} - \frac{1}{36} = \frac{10}{36}" />.

## Exercise

In a class of <InlineMath math="30" /> students, <InlineMath math="18" /> belong to the mathematics club, <InlineMath math="14" /> belong to the science club, and <InlineMath math="8" /> belong to both clubs. One student is selected at random.

1.  What is the probability that the selected student belongs to the mathematics club **or** the science club?
2.  What is the probability that the student belongs to neither club?

### Worked Solution

**Problem 1**. Let <InlineMath math="M" /> be the event “belongs to the mathematics club” and <InlineMath math="S" /> the event “belongs to the science club.” The <InlineMath math="8" /> students in both clubs are included in both counts, so adding <InlineMath math="18+14" /> would count them twice. The general addition rule removes that duplicate count:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="n(M \cup S)=n(M)+n(S)-n(M \cap S)=18+14-8=24" />
  <BlockMath math="P(M \cup S)=\frac{24}{30}=\frac{4}{5}" />
</MathContainer>

**Problem 2**. There are <InlineMath math="30-24=6" /> students outside both clubs. Therefore,

<BlockMath math="P(\text{neither club})=\frac{6}{30}=\frac{1}{5}" />

The two probabilities add to <InlineMath math="4/5+1/5=1" />, which checks that the class has been divided into two complementary groups: students in at least one club and students in neither club.

The **overlap must be subtracted exactly once** because it was already included in _both original counts_. This **complement check** is a quick way to catch an omitted or duplicated group _before accepting the result_.
