## Events with No Shared Outcome

Suppose one trial can produce either of two outcomes, but not both. In probability, events with this relationship are **mutually exclusive**, or _disjoint_.

Events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **mutually exclusive** when they **cannot occur together** in a single trial. If <InlineMath math="A" /> occurs, <InlineMath math="B" /> does not occur, and vice versa.

## Characteristics of Mutually Exclusive Events

Their defining property is an **empty intersection**: no outcome belongs to both event <InlineMath math="A" /> and event <InlineMath math="B" />. That property is what allows the two probabilities to be added directly, because no outcome would be counted twice.
### Probability of Two Events Occurring Together

Because events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> cannot occur together, the probability of their intersection is **zero**.

We can write the probability of event "<InlineMath math="A" /> **and** <InlineMath math="B" />" (both occurring) as:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ and } B) = 0" />

Or using the intersection symbol:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />

For mutually exclusive events, the intersection is empty, so its probability is zero.

## Calculating the Combined Probability for Mutually Exclusive Events

To calculate the probability that event <InlineMath math="A" /> **OR** event <InlineMath math="B" /> occurs, use the fact that the events have no shared outcomes.

The two individual probabilities can therefore be **added** without double-counting.

The formula is:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

Or using the union symbol:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

This **special addition rule** <Highlight>applies only to mutually exclusive events</Highlight>. When events overlap, their intersection must also be included in the calculation.

## Examples of Mutually Exclusive Events

For a fair coin, a fair die, and a uniformly random draw from a standard deck, elementary outcomes are **equally likely**. The table counts outcomes for each event. Each event pair has an empty intersection.

| Experiment | Two events | Probability of the union |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair coin | Heads or tails | <InlineMath math="\frac12+\frac12=1" /> |
| Fair die | Three or five | <InlineMath math="\frac16+\frac16=\frac13" /> |
| Fair die | Even or odd | <InlineMath math="\frac36+\frac36=1" /> |
| Standard deck | King or Queen | <InlineMath math="\frac4{52}+\frac4{52}=\frac2{13}" /> |
| Standard deck | Red or club | <InlineMath math="\frac{26}{52}+\frac{13}{52}=\frac34" /> |

For the die, the even outcomes are <InlineMath math="\{2,4,6\}" /> and the odd outcomes are <InlineMath math="\{1,3,5\}" />. Together they cover every face. All clubs are black, so no club belongs to the red-card event. A standard deck has <InlineMath math="52" /> cards, including <InlineMath math="4" /> Kings, <InlineMath math="4" /> Queens, <InlineMath math="26" /> red cards, and <InlineMath math="13" /> clubs.

## Exercise

A **fair six-sided die** is rolled once. Let <InlineMath math="A=\{1,2\}" /> and <InlineMath math="B=\{5,6\}" />.

1.  Show that <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive.
2.  Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />.
3.  Find the probability that neither event occurs.
### Worked Solution

**Problem 1**. The two events have an **empty intersection** and therefore no shared outcome:

<BlockMath math="A \cap B=\varnothing" />

They are therefore **mutually exclusive**.

**Problem 2**. Each event contains <InlineMath math="2" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> equally likely die outcomes, so the special addition rule gives

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}P(A \cup B)&=P(A)+P(B) \\ &=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{6} \\ &=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}\end{aligned}" />

**Problem 3**. The outcomes outside <InlineMath math="A \cup B=\{1,2,5,6\}" /> are <InlineMath math="\{3,4\}" />. Thus,

<BlockMath math="P(\text{neither }A\text{ nor }B)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3}" />

As a check, the probability of the union and the probability of its complement add to <InlineMath math="2/3+1/3=1" />.
