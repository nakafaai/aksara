## Mutually Exclusive Events and Their Probability Rule

Two events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **mutually exclusive** when they cannot happen together in one trial. Their intersection is empty, <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \varnothing" />, so <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />. For mutually exclusive events, the probability of <InlineMath math="A" /> or <InlineMath math="B" /> is simply the sum:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

## Recognizing Mutually Exclusive Events

Ask one question: is there a single outcome that meets **both events at once**? If there is none, the events are mutually exclusive. Every experiment in this table has equally likely basic outcomes.

| Experiment | Two events | Probability of the union |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair coin | Heads or tails | <InlineMath math="\frac12+\frac12=1" /> |
| Fair die | A three or a five | <InlineMath math="\frac16+\frac16=\frac13" /> |
| Fair die | Even or odd | <InlineMath math="\frac36+\frac36=1" /> |
| Playing cards | King or queen | <InlineMath math="\frac4{52}+\frac4{52}=\frac2{13}" /> |
| Playing cards | Red or clubs | <InlineMath math="\frac{26}{52}+\frac{13}{52}=\frac34" /> |

The last row is mutually exclusive because every club is black. On the other hand, red cards and kings are **not** mutually exclusive, because the king of hearts and the king of diamonds belong to both. Such events use the rule for [events that are not mutually exclusive](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/two-events-not-mutually-exclusive).

## Why the Probabilities Can Simply Be Added

For any two events whose intersection is empty, <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" /> holds, because no outcome is counted twice. In a sample space with equally likely outcomes, you can count the reason directly: the number of elements in the union is the sum of the numbers in the two events.

<BlockMath math="n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A \cup B) = \frac{n(A) + n(B)}{n(S)} = P(A) + P(B)" />

This rule is a special case of the general [addition rule](/en/subjects/mathematics/probability/addition-rule) <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" /> with <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />. The same rule works for more than two events, provided **every pair of them is mutually exclusive**: <InlineMath math="P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + P(A_3)" />.

## An Event and Its Complement

An event <InlineMath math="A" /> and its complement <InlineMath math="A^c" /> (not <InlineMath math="A" />) are always mutually exclusive, and **together they make up the whole sample space**. So <InlineMath math="P(A) + P(A^c) = P(S) = 1" />, or

<BlockMath math="P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)" />

The complement rule saves work when the event in question has many outcomes. The probability that a die does not show a <InlineMath math="6" /> is <InlineMath math="1 - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{6}" />, with no need to add five probabilities. Check by listing: the results other than <InlineMath math="6" /> are <InlineMath math="\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}" />, which is <InlineMath math="5" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> results.

## Example with the Sum of Two Dice

Two dice are rolled together. Find the probability that the sum of the two dice is <InlineMath math="7" /> or <InlineMath math="11" />.

The sample space has <InlineMath math="6 \times 6 = 36" /> equally likely pairs. A sum of <InlineMath math="7" /> comes from <InlineMath math="6" /> pairs, and a sum of <InlineMath math="11" /> from <InlineMath math="2" /> pairs. One roll cannot sum to both <InlineMath math="7" /> and <InlineMath math="11" />, so the events are **mutually exclusive**:

<BlockMath math="P(7 \text{ or } 11) = \frac{6}{36} + \frac{2}{36} = \frac{8}{36} = \frac{2}{9}" />

## Mutually Exclusive Is Not the Same as Independent

The terms mutually exclusive and independent are often mixed up. **Mutually exclusive** means the two events cannot happen together. [Independent](/en/subjects/mathematics/combinatorics/probability-of-independent-events) is defined by <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)" />. If <InlineMath math="P(A) > 0" />, this is the same as saying that <InlineMath math="A" /> happening does not change the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />.

If <InlineMath math="P(A) > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="P(B) > 0" />, mutually exclusive events **cannot** be independent. Their intersection has probability <InlineMath math="0" />, while <InlineMath math="P(A)\,P(B) > 0" />. For the die, knowing that <InlineMath math="A" /> happened gives the conditional probability <InlineMath math="P(B \mid A) = 0" />, while <InlineMath math="P(B) = \frac{1}{3}" />, so the events do affect each other.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adding the probabilities of overlapping events | Outcomes in the intersection are counted twice | **Write out the intersection first**, it must be empty |
| Treating mutually exclusive as independent | Mutually exclusive gives <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />, independent gives <InlineMath math="P(A)P(B)" /> | Compare <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> with <InlineMath math="P(A)P(B)" /> |
| Treating red cards and kings as mutually exclusive | The king of hearts and the king of diamonds belong to both | Look for one outcome that meets both conditions |
| Adding three events that are only partly mutually exclusive | The sum rule needs every pair to be mutually exclusive | Check all pairs, not just one |
| Getting a union probability above <InlineMath math="1" /> | That is a sign that probabilities of overlapping events were added, counting the intersection twice | Mutually exclusive events never give a sum above <InlineMath math="1" /> |

## Practice Problems

Make sure the intersection is empty first, then **check your answer by listing the outcomes**.

1. A die is rolled once with <InlineMath math="A = \{1, 2\}" /> and <InlineMath math="B = \{5, 6\}" />. Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" /> and the probability that neither event happens.

2. One card is drawn from a deck of <InlineMath math="52" />. Find the probability of drawing a king or a queen.

3. Two dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is <InlineMath math="2" /> or <InlineMath math="12" />.

4. The events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive with <InlineMath math="P(A) = 0.3" /> and <InlineMath math="P(B) = 0.5" />. Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />, the probability that neither happens, and whether they are independent.

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. The intersection is empty, so <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{2}{3}" />. The results outside <InlineMath math="A \cup B" /> are <InlineMath math="\{3, 4\}" />, so the probability that neither event happens is <InlineMath math="\frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}" />. Check with the complement: <InlineMath math="1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}" />.

**Problem 2**. One card cannot be a king and a queen at once, so

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{4}{52} = \frac{8}{52} = \frac{2}{13}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="8" /> cards qualify, the <InlineMath math="4" /> kings and the <InlineMath math="4" /> queens.

**Problem 3**. A sum of <InlineMath math="2" /> comes only from <InlineMath math="(1, 1)" />, and a sum of <InlineMath math="12" /> only from <InlineMath math="(6, 6)" />. The events are mutually exclusive:

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{1}{36} + \frac{1}{36} = \frac{2}{36} = \frac{1}{18}" />

Check: <InlineMath math="2" /> of the <InlineMath math="36" /> pairs qualify.

**Problem 4**. Since they are mutually exclusive, <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.8" />, and the probability that neither happens is <InlineMath math="1 - 0.8 = 0.2" />. They are **not independent**, because <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" /> while <InlineMath math="P(A)\,P(B) = 0.15" />. Check: <InlineMath math="0.8 + 0.2 = 1" />.
