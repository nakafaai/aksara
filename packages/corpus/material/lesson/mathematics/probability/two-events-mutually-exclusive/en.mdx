## What Does Mutually Exclusive Mean?

Suppose one trial can produce either of two outcomes, but not both. In probability, events with this relationship are **mutually exclusive**, or _disjoint_.

Events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **mutually exclusive** when they **cannot occur together** in a single trial. If <InlineMath math="A" /> occurs, <InlineMath math="B" /> does not occur, and vice versa.

## Characteristics of Mutually Exclusive Events

Their defining property is an empty intersection: no outcome belongs to both event <InlineMath math="A" /> and event <InlineMath math="B" />.

### Probability of Two Events Occurring Together

Because events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> cannot occur together, the probability of their intersection is **zero**.

We can write the probability of event "<InlineMath math="A" /> **and** <InlineMath math="B" />" (both occurring) as:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ and} B) = 0" />

Or using the intersection symbol:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />

For mutually exclusive events, the intersection is empty, so its probability is zero.

## Calculating the Combined Probability for Mutually Exclusive Events

To calculate the probability that event <InlineMath math="A" /> **OR** event <InlineMath math="B" /> occurs, use the fact that the events have no shared outcomes.

The two individual probabilities can therefore be **added** without double-counting.

The formula is:

<BlockMath math="P(A \text{ or} B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

Or using the union symbol:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

This **special addition rule** applies only to mutually exclusive events. When events overlap, their intersection must also be included in the calculation.

## Examples of Mutually Exclusive Events

The following examples show when the special addition rule applies:

1.  **Coin Toss:**

    A single coin toss produces either "Heads" or "Tails," but not both.

    - <InlineMath math="P(\text{Heads}) = 1/2" />
    - <InlineMath math="P(\text{Tails}) = 1/2" />
    - Probability of getting Heads OR Tails is <InlineMath math="P(\text{Heads}) + P(\text{Tails}) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1" /> (One of them must occur).

2.  **Rolling a Die (once):**

    Consider two pairs of events:

    - Getting a <InlineMath math="3" /> and getting a <InlineMath math="5" /> cannot happen at once. Each has probability <InlineMath math="1/6" />, so the probability of getting a <InlineMath math="3" /> or <InlineMath math="5" /> is <InlineMath math="1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3" />.
    - Getting an even number <InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 6\}" /> and getting an odd number <InlineMath math="\{1, 3, 5\}" /> cannot happen at once. Each event has probability <InlineMath math="3/6 = 1/2" />, so the probability of getting even or odd is <InlineMath math="1/2 + 1/2 = 1" />.

3.  **Drawing a Card (once):**

    Use the same idea for card events:

    - Getting a King and getting a Queen are mutually exclusive. There are <InlineMath math="4" /> Kings and <InlineMath math="4" /> Queens in <InlineMath math="52 \text{ cards}" />, so the probability of getting a King or Queen is <InlineMath math="4/52 + 4/52 = 8/52 = 2/13" />.
    - Getting a red card and getting a club <InlineMath math="\clubsuit" /> are mutually exclusive because clubs are black. There are <InlineMath math="26" /> red cards and <InlineMath math="13" /> clubs, so the probability of getting red or club is <InlineMath math="26/52 + 13/52 = 39/52 = 3/4" />.

## Exercise

A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Let <InlineMath math="A=\{1,2\}" /> and <InlineMath math="B=\{5,6\}" />.

1.  Show that <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive.
2.  Find <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B)" />.
3.  Find the probability that neither event occurs.

### Worked Solution

The two events have an **empty intersection** and therefore no shared outcome:

<BlockMath math="A \cap B=\varnothing" />

They are therefore **mutually exclusive**. Each event contains <InlineMath math="2" /> of the <InlineMath math="6" /> equally likely die outcomes, so the special addition rule gives

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P(A \cup B)=P(A)+P(B)" />
  <BlockMath math="=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{6}=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

The outcomes outside <InlineMath math="A \cup B=\{1,2,5,6\}" /> are <InlineMath math="\{3,4\}" />. Thus,

<BlockMath math="P(\text{neither }A\text{ nor }B)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3}" />

As a check, the probability of the union and the probability of its complement add to <InlineMath math="2/3+1/3=1" />.
