## Mutually Exclusive Events

In everyday life, we often face situations where **two events cannot occur simultaneously**. For example, when flipping a coin, we cannot get both heads and tails at the same time in a single flip. Events like these are called mutually exclusive events.

**Mutually exclusive events** are two or more events that cannot occur simultaneously in a single experiment. If one event occurs, then the other event will definitely not occur.

As a simple illustration, imagine you draw one card from a deck. The card you draw cannot be both red and black at the same time. These two events are mutually exclusive because there is no card that has both colors.

## Characteristics of Mutually Exclusive Events

### No Intersection

The main characteristic of mutually exclusive events is **having no intersection or common elements**. In mathematical notation, if <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are mutually exclusive events, then:

<BlockMath math="A \cap B = \emptyset" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="\emptyset" /> indicates an empty set, meaning there are no common elements between the two events.

### Outcomes That Cannot Occur Together

The following pairs cannot occur in the same trial:

- In dice rolling: getting an even number and getting an odd number
- In card drawing: drawing an Ace and drawing a King in a single draw
- In a race: placing first and placing second simultaneously

### Identifying Mutually Exclusive Events

To identify whether two events are mutually exclusive, ask yourself: "Can these two events occur simultaneously in one experiment?" If the answer is no, then the two events are mutually exclusive.

## Formula for Probability of Mutually Exclusive Events

The general addition rule subtracts the overlap <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" />. Mutually exclusive events have <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)=0" />, so that term disappears:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

This formula shows that the probability of event <InlineMath math="A" /> or event <InlineMath math="B" /> occurring equals the sum of the individual probabilities of each event.

Mutually exclusive does **not** mean independent. If <InlineMath math="P(A) > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="P(B) > 0" />, learning that <InlineMath math="A" /> occurred makes <InlineMath math="B" /> impossible. Thus <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />, while independent events would require <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0" />.

### Zero Intersection in the Addition Rule

Unlike events that are not mutually exclusive, in mutually exclusive events we don't need to subtract the intersection because <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0" />. Therefore, the general formula:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)" />

Becomes:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - 0 = P(A) + P(B)" />

## Calculations with the Addition Rule

### Dice Rolling

A die is rolled once. Determine the probability of getting a prime number or a number greater than <InlineMath math="4" />.

**Solution:**

Sample space: <InlineMath math="S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}" />

- Event <InlineMath math="A" /> (prime number): <InlineMath math="A = \{2, 3, 5\}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" /> (<InlineMath math="\text{number} > 4" />): <InlineMath math="B = \{5, 6\}" />

Check intersection: <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \{5\}" />

Since the number <InlineMath math="5" /> lies in both events, they are **not mutually exclusive**. The original question still has a valid answer, but it requires subtracting the overlap:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B)=\frac{3}{6}+\frac{2}{6}-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

The next example shows when the shorter mutually exclusive rule applies.

**Contrasting Mutually Exclusive Example:**

- Event <InlineMath math="A" /> (odd number): <InlineMath math="A = \{1, 3, 5\}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="C" /> (even number): <InlineMath math="C = \{2, 4, 6\}" />

Check intersection: <InlineMath math="A \cap C = \emptyset" />

Since there is no intersection, the two events are **mutually exclusive**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="P(C) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cup C) = P(A) + P(C) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1" />
</MathContainer>

The odd and even outcomes together cover all six faces of the die, so their union has probability <InlineMath math="1" />.

### Rolling Two Dice

Two dice are rolled simultaneously. Determine the probability of getting a sum of <InlineMath math="5" /> or a sum of <InlineMath math="7" />.

**Solution:**

The table lists all <InlineMath math="36" /> ordered outcomes of the two dice:

| Die <InlineMath math="1" /> \\ Die <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |

| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(1,6)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(2,6)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(3,6)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(4,6)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(5,6)" /> |
| <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,1)" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,2)" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,3)" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,4)" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,5)" /> | <InlineMath math="(6,6)" /> |

Total possibilities is <InlineMath math="6 \times 6 = 36" />

**Event identification:**

**Event <InlineMath math="A" />** (<InlineMath math="\text{sum} = 5" />):

From the table above, pairs that produce sum <InlineMath math="5" /> are:
- <InlineMath math="(1,4): 1 + 4 = 5" />
- <InlineMath math="(2,3): 2 + 3 = 5" />
- <InlineMath math="(3,2): 3 + 2 = 5" />
- <InlineMath math="(4,1): 4 + 1 = 5" />

So there are **<InlineMath math="4 \text{ ways}" />** to get sum <InlineMath math="5" />.

**Event <InlineMath math="B" />** (<InlineMath math="\text{sum} = 7" />):

From the table above, pairs that produce sum <InlineMath math="7" /> are:
- <InlineMath math="(1,6): 1 + 6 = 7" />
- <InlineMath math="(2,5): 2 + 5 = 7" />
- <InlineMath math="(3,4): 3 + 4 = 7" />
- <InlineMath math="(4,3): 4 + 3 = 7" />
- <InlineMath math="(5,2): 5 + 2 = 7" />
- <InlineMath math="(6,1): 6 + 1 = 7" />

So there are **<InlineMath math="6 \text{ ways}" />** to get sum <InlineMath math="7" />.

**Check intersection:** It's impossible for the sum to be both <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="7" />, so <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \emptyset" />

Both events are **mutually exclusive**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}" />
<BlockMath math="P(B) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) = \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{6}" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution for fraction addition:**

To add <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{6}" />, we need the least common multiple of <InlineMath math="9" /> and <InlineMath math="6" />.

<InlineMath math="\operatorname{lcm}(9, 6) = 18" />, so:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{9} = \frac{1 \times 2}{9 \times 2} = \frac{2}{18}" />
<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{6} = \frac{1 \times 3}{6 \times 3} = \frac{3}{18}" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{18} + \frac{3}{18} = \frac{5}{18}" />
</MathContainer>

## Problem Solving Strategy

### Check the Intersection Before Adding

To solve probability problems for mutually exclusive events, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the sample space** and determine the total possible outcomes
2. **Define the events** mentioned in the problem clearly
3. **Check intersection** between events to ensure they are mutually exclusive
4. **Calculate the probability of each** event separately
5. **Apply the formula** <InlineMath math="P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)" />

### Checks for the Final Answer

Check three points before accepting the answer:

- Draw the event sets in a diagram or table when their intersection is not yet clear.
- Verify that the final probability lies between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />.
- Read "or" as union unless the problem explicitly states otherwise.

### Events with a Nonempty Intersection

Two events are not mutually exclusive when their intersection contains at least one outcome. The following examples have a nonempty intersection:

**Example <InlineMath math="1" />: Dice Rolling**
- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Getting a prime number is <InlineMath math="\{2, 3, 5\}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Getting an odd number is <InlineMath math="\{1, 3, 5\}" />

**Common mistake**: "Prime and odd are different, so they are mutually exclusive"
**Reality**: <InlineMath math="A \cap B = \{3, 5\} \ne \emptyset" />, so **not mutually exclusive**

**Example <InlineMath math="2" />: Card Drawing**
- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Drawing a red card
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Drawing an Ace

**Common mistake**: "Color and card type are different, so they are mutually exclusive"
**Reality**: There are red Aces (Ace of hearts and Ace of diamonds), so **not mutually exclusive**

**Example <InlineMath math="3" />: Two Student Criteria**
- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: Students who are tall (<InlineMath math=">160 \text{ cm}" />)
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: Students whose score is above <InlineMath math="80" />

**Common mistake**: “Height and test score are different traits, so the events are mutually exclusive”
**Reality**: A student can satisfy both criteria, so the events are **not mutually exclusive**

**Identification Strategy:**
1. **Ask**: "Can one element satisfy both criteria simultaneously?"
2. **Find intersection**: Identify elements that belong to both events
3. **If there is intersection**: Events are **not mutually exclusive**
4. **If there is no intersection**: Events are **mutually exclusive**
