## Conditional Probability and Dependence

Imagine drawing a card from a deck before a friend draws the next one. Does your card change the probability of what your friend can draw? Without replacement, it often does. **Conditional probability** calculates the second probability using what is already known.

In a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes, the **conditional probability** of event <InlineMath math="B" /> given event <InlineMath math="A" /> is the fraction of outcomes in <InlineMath math="A" /> that also belong to <InlineMath math="B" />. If conditioning on <InlineMath math="A" /> changes the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />, the events are dependent. If the probability stays the same, they are independent.

Before calculating the next probability, restrict the possible outcomes using the **condition that is already known**.

## Mathematical Formula and Notation

For events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> with <InlineMath math="P(A) > 0" />, conditional probability is defined by:

<BlockMath math="P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}" />

The quantities in this formula are:
- <InlineMath math="P(B|A)" /> is the probability of <InlineMath math="B" /> under the condition that <InlineMath math="A" /> occurred
- <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" /> is the probability that <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> both occur
- <InlineMath math="P(A)" /> is the probability of the conditioning event <InlineMath math="A" />

Rearranging the definition gives the general multiplication rule:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B|A)" />

If the events are independent, learning <InlineMath math="A" /> does not change the probability of <InlineMath math="B" />:

<BlockMath math="P(B|A) = P(B)" />

## Application to Card Drawing

### Ace and King Scenario

A standard deck has <InlineMath math="52 \text{ cards}" />. It contains <InlineMath math="4 \text{ Aces}" /> and <InlineMath math="4 \text{ Kings}" />. Two cards are drawn without replacement. What is the probability of drawing an Ace first and a King second?

**Step-by-step analysis:**

Let:
- Event <InlineMath math="C" /> is drawing an Ace first
- Event <InlineMath math="D" /> is drawing a King second

Probability calculation:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(C) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}" />
<BlockMath math="P(D|C) = \frac{4}{51}" />
<BlockMath math="P(C \cap D) = P(C) \times P(D|C) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{4}{51} = \frac{1}{13} \times \frac{4}{51} = \frac{4}{663}" />
</MathContainer>

After one Ace is drawn, <InlineMath math="51 \text{ cards}" /> remain. All <InlineMath math="4 \text{ Kings}" /> are still present, so the conditional probability of drawing a King is <InlineMath math="\frac{4}{51}" />.

### Probability of Drawing Two Hearts in Sequence

To see how the favorable count can change, consider drawing two Hearts in a row:

- First draw: <InlineMath math="P(A) = \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4}" />
- Second draw after getting a heart: <InlineMath math="P(B|A) = \frac{12}{51}" />

The probability of drawing Hearts on both draws is therefore:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = \frac{13}{52} \times \frac{12}{51} = \frac{156}{2652} = \frac{1}{17}" />

## Application in Management Selection

A company reviews the distribution of managers by gender and job level. Its data has the following composition:

| Management Position | Male | Female | Total |
|------------------|---------------|---------------|-------|
| Senior (S)       | <InlineMath math="78" /> | <InlineMath math="122" /> | <InlineMath math="200" /> |
| Middle (M)     | <InlineMath math="78" /> | <InlineMath math="122" /> | <InlineMath math="200" /> |
| Junior (J)       | <InlineMath math="44" /> | <InlineMath math="156" /> | <InlineMath math="200" /> |
| **Total**        | <InlineMath math="200" /> | <InlineMath math="400" /> | <InlineMath math="600" /> |

Let <InlineMath math="L" /> denote the event that a selected manager is male.

The company randomly selects two managers without replacement for a committee. The probability that both selected managers are male is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(L_1) = \frac{200}{600} = \frac{1}{3}" />
<BlockMath math="P(L_2|L_1) = \frac{199}{599}" />
<BlockMath math="P(L_1 \cap L_2) = \frac{200}{600} \times \frac{199}{599} = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{199}{599} = \frac{199}{1797}" />
</MathContainer>

After selecting one male manager, there are <InlineMath math="599" /> total managers remaining with <InlineMath math="199" /> male managers, so the probability of selecting a second male manager decreases to <InlineMath math="\frac{199}{599}" />.

## Problem Solving Strategy

### Update the Sample Space Before Multiplying

To solve conditional probability problems:

1. **Identify the events** first and second clearly
2. **Determine the conditions** after the first event occurs
3. **Calculate the probability** of the first event from initial conditions
4. **Calculate the conditional probability** of the second event after the first event
5. **Multiply both probabilities** to get the joint probability

### Difference from Ordinary Probability

For **independent events**, the condition does not change the next probability. For **dependent events**, the known outcome changes either the favorable count, the total count, or both.

**Concrete example: drawing two Aces**

Suppose we want to calculate the probability of getting two aces in succession from a standard deck (<InlineMath math="52 \text{ cards}" />, <InlineMath math="4" /> aces). Let <InlineMath math="A_1" /> denote the event that the first card is an ace and <InlineMath math="A_2" /> the event that the second card is an ace.

1. **Independent Probability (With Replacement):**

    If after drawing the first card, the card is returned and the deck is reshuffled:

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="P(A_1) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}" />
    <BlockMath math="P(A_2) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}" />
    <BlockMath math="P(A_1 \cap A_2) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13} \times \frac{1}{13} = \frac{1}{169}" />
    </MathContainer>

2. **Dependent Draw (Without Replacement):**

    If the first card is not returned:

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="P(A_1) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}" />
    <BlockMath math="P(A_2|A_1) = \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{17}" />
    <BlockMath math="P(A_1 \cap A_2) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{13} \times \frac{1}{17} = \frac{1}{221}" />
    </MathContainer>

**Result Comparison:**
- Independent probability: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{169} = 0.00592" />
- Conditional probability: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{221} = 0.00452" />

In this example, drawing without replacement gives a **smaller** result because the number of remaining Aces decreases from <InlineMath math="4" /> to <InlineMath math="3" />, while the total cards decrease from <InlineMath math="52" /> to <InlineMath math="51" />.

## Exercises

1. A box contains <InlineMath math="8 \text{ red balls}" /> and <InlineMath math="12 \text{ blue balls}" />. If two balls are drawn sequentially without replacement, calculate the probability of getting a red ball on the first draw and a blue ball on the second draw.

2. A deck of cards is shuffled randomly. Three cards are drawn successively without replacement. What is the probability of getting an Ace on the first draw, a King on the second draw, and a Queen on the third draw?

Try each exercise before reading its worked solution. At every draw, write both the favorable count and the new total before multiplying.

### Worked Solutions

1. **Solution:**

   Let <InlineMath math="A" /> be getting a red ball first, and let <InlineMath math="B" /> be getting a blue ball second

   Total initial balls is <InlineMath math="8 + 12 = 20 \text{ balls}" />

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{8}{20} = \frac{2}{5}" />
   <BlockMath math="P(B|A) = \frac{12}{19}" />
   <BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = \frac{8}{20} \times \frac{12}{19} = \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{12}{19} = \frac{24}{95}" />
   </MathContainer>

   After drawing one red ball, there are <InlineMath math="19 \text{ balls}" /> remaining in total (<InlineMath math="8 - 1 = 7" /> red, <InlineMath math="12" /> blue). The number of blue balls does not change (remains <InlineMath math="12" />), so the probability of drawing a blue ball second is <InlineMath math="\frac{12}{19}" />.

2. **Solution:**

   Let <InlineMath math="A" /> be getting an Ace first, let <InlineMath math="K" /> be getting a King second, and let <InlineMath math="Q" /> be getting a Queen third

   A standard deck has <InlineMath math="52 \text{ cards}" />, including <InlineMath math="4" /> Aces, <InlineMath math="4" /> Kings, and <InlineMath math="4" /> Queens.

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}" />
   <BlockMath math="P(K|A) = \frac{4}{51}" />
   <BlockMath math="P(Q|A \cap K) = \frac{4}{50} = \frac{2}{25}" />
   <BlockMath math="P(A \cap K \cap Q) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{4}{51} \times \frac{4}{50} = \frac{1}{13} \times \frac{4}{51} \times \frac{2}{25} = \frac{8}{16575}" />
   </MathContainer>

   Each draw reduces the total number of cards (<InlineMath math="52" />→<InlineMath math="51" />→<InlineMath math="50" />). The next required rank still has all <InlineMath math="4" /> cards available: drawing an Ace does not remove a King, and drawing a King does not remove a Queen.
