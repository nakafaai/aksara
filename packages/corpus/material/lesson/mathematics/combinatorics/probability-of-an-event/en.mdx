## Understanding Probability

Will it rain today? Will a team win its next match? These outcomes are uncertain, but **probability** gives us a precise way to describe that uncertainty.

**Probability** measures how likely an event is. Its value lies between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />:
- Probability <InlineMath math="0" /> means the event is **impossible**
- Probability <InlineMath math="1" /> means the event is **certain**
- Probability <InlineMath math="0.5" /> means the event and its complement are **equally likely**

Probability does not predict a single outcome with certainty. It helps us reason about repeated outcomes and make decisions with uncertainty stated explicitly.

## Sample Space and Events

Two ideas are needed before we calculate a probability:

The **sample space (S)** is the set of every possible outcome of an experiment. When two dice are rolled, for example, each outcome is an ordered pair of face values.

An **event (A)** is a subset of the sample space containing the outcomes we want to examine.

**Concrete example:** for two coin tosses, let <InlineMath math="H" /> denote heads and <InlineMath math="T" /> tails. The sample space is <InlineMath math="\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}" />. The event “at least one tail” is <InlineMath math="A = \{HT, TH, TT\}" />.

## Probability Formula

To calculate the probability of an event, we use the **classical probability formula**:

<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}" />

where:
- <InlineMath math="P(A)" /> = probability of event <InlineMath math="A" /> occurring
- <InlineMath math="n(A)" /> = number of favorable outcomes (members of event <InlineMath math="A" />)
- <InlineMath math="n(S)" /> = number of all possible outcomes (members of sample space)

**Important condition:** This counting formula applies only when all outcomes in the sample space are **equally likely**.

**Fundamental properties:**
- <InlineMath math="0 \leq P(A) \leq 1" /> (probability is always between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />)
- <InlineMath math="P(A) + P(A') = 1" />, where <InlineMath math="A'" /> is the **complement** of event <InlineMath math="A" />

**Complement:** The complement of <InlineMath math="A" /> contains every outcome in the sample space that is **not** in <InlineMath math="A" />. If <InlineMath math="A" /> is “rolling an even number,” then <InlineMath math="A'" /> is “rolling an odd number.”

## Worked Examples

**Analysis of Rolling Two Dice:**

Two fair dice produce <InlineMath math="6 \times 6 = 36" /> equally likely ordered pairs. Find the probability that their sum is <InlineMath math="9" />.

**Systematic solution:**

1. Identify all ways to get sum <InlineMath math="9" />:
   - First die <InlineMath math="3" />, second die <InlineMath math="6" />: <InlineMath math="(3,6)" />
   - First die <InlineMath math="4" />, second die <InlineMath math="5" />: <InlineMath math="(4,5)" />
   - First die <InlineMath math="5" />, second die <InlineMath math="4" />: <InlineMath math="(5,4)" />
   - First die <InlineMath math="6" />, second die <InlineMath math="3" />: <InlineMath math="(6,3)" />

2. Count the favorable outcomes: <InlineMath math="n(A) = 4" />

3. Determine probability:

    <BlockMath math="P(\text{sum} = 9) = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111" />

**Probability-Based Marketing Strategy:**

A beverage company inserts prize coupons into some milk boxes. Historical data estimate the probability that a purchased box contains a prize as <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{32}" />.

**Practical interpretation:** Over many purchases, about <InlineMath math="3" /> of every <InlineMath math="32" /> purchased boxes are expected to contain a prize. The estimate can help the company:
- Plan promotional budgets
- Estimate consumer response
- Determine sales targets

**Comparing Dice Sums:**

For two fair dice, a sum of <InlineMath math="7" /> has more favorable ordered pairs than any other sum:

Ways to get sum <InlineMath math="7" />: <InlineMath math="(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) = 6 \text{ ways}" />

<BlockMath math="P(\text{sum} = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167" />

This is the largest probability among the possible sums. It does not guarantee that the next roll will be <InlineMath math="7" />; it describes the long-run model.

## Practice Problems

Solve each problem before reading the worked solution. Define the sample space and event explicitly before substituting numbers into a formula.

1. In rolling two dice, determine the probability of getting a sum that is an even number.

2. A box contains <InlineMath math="5 \text{ red balls}" />, <InlineMath math="3 \text{ blue balls}" />, and <InlineMath math="2 \text{ green balls}" />. If one ball is drawn randomly, determine the probability of getting a ball that is not red.

3. Three fair coins are tossed independently. Determine the probability of getting exactly two tails.

4. A quality inspection finds exactly <InlineMath math="50" /> defective items in a batch of <InlineMath math="1000 \text{ units}" />. If one item is selected uniformly at random, what is the probability that it is not defective?

### Worked Solutions

1. **Answer: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" />**

   **Systematic solution steps:**

   Sample space for rolling two dice: <InlineMath math="n(S) = 36" />

   **Identify all even sums and ways to obtain them:**
   - Sum <InlineMath math="2" />: <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> → <InlineMath math="1" /> way
   - Sum <InlineMath math="4" />: <InlineMath math="(1,3), (2,2), (3,1)" /> → <InlineMath math="3 \text{ ways}" />
   - Sum <InlineMath math="6" />: <InlineMath math="(1,5), (2,4), (3,3), (4,2), (5,1)" /> → <InlineMath math="5 \text{ ways}" />
   - Sum <InlineMath math="8" />: <InlineMath math="(2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2)" /> → <InlineMath math="5 \text{ ways}" />
   - Sum <InlineMath math="10" />: <InlineMath math="(4,6), (5,5), (6,4)" /> → <InlineMath math="3 \text{ ways}" />
   - Sum <InlineMath math="12" />: <InlineMath math="(6,6)" /> → <InlineMath math="1" /> way

   **Total favorable outcomes:** <InlineMath math="n(A) = 1 + 3 + 5 + 5 + 3 + 1 = 18" />

   **Probability calculation:**

   <BlockMath math="P(\text{even}) = \frac{18}{36} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5" />

2. **Answer: <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}" />**

   **Solution steps:**

   **Count total balls:** <InlineMath math="5 + 3 + 2 = 10" /> balls

   **Identify balls that are not red:** <InlineMath math="3 \text{ blue} + 2 \text{ green} = 5" /> balls

   **Probability calculation:**

   <BlockMath math="P(\text{not red}) = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5" />

3. **Answer: <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{8}" />**

   **Solution steps:**

   **Sample space for three coin tosses:** <InlineMath math="\{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}" />

   **Total possibilities:** <InlineMath math="n(S) = 2^3 = 8" />

   **Event exactly two tails:** <InlineMath math="\{HTT, THT, TTH\}" />

   **Number of favorable outcomes:** <InlineMath math="n(A) = 3" />

   **Probability calculation:**

   <BlockMath math="P(\text{exactly 2 tails}) = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375" />

4. **Answer: <InlineMath math="\frac{19}{20}" /> or <InlineMath math="95\%" />**

   **Solution steps:**

   **Total products:** <InlineMath math="1000 \text{ units}" />

   **Defective products:** <InlineMath math="5\% \times 1000 = 50 \text{ units}" />

   **Non-defective products:** <InlineMath math="1000 - 50 = 950 \text{ units}" />

   **Probability calculation:**

   <BlockMath math="P(\text{not defective}) = \frac{950}{1000} = \frac{19}{20} = 0.95 = 95\%" />

   **Interpretation:** Under uniform random selection from this inspected batch, an item is non-defective with probability <InlineMath math="95\%" />. This does not guarantee the result of one selection.
