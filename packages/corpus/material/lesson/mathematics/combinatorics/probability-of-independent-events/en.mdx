## Learning One Outcome Leaves the Other Probability Unchanged

Some probability models contain events where learning one result **does not change the probability** of another. Toss a fair coin and roll a fair die. Knowing the coin result gives no information about the die result, so the two events are independent.

Events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are **independent** when conditioning on either event <Highlight>leaves the probability of the other unchanged</Highlight>. The probability model determines this property. Events that happen at the same time are not automatically independent.

Events can share a hidden cause even when neither event directly controls the other. Two alarms, for example, can both react to the same incident. Treat events as independent only when the probability model states it or the conditional probabilities confirm it.

## How Independence Shows Up in Probabilities

For independent events, information about one event **does not change the probability** of the other. The events may come from the same trial or separate trials. We must check the probabilities, rather than just the source of the trials.

### Unchanged Conditional Probability

The main characteristic of independent events is that the result of one event **does not change the probability** of another event. In mathematical notation, if <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are independent and <InlineMath math="P(B) > 0" />, then:

<BlockMath math="P(A|B) = P(A)" />

This means the probability of <InlineMath math="A" /> occurring when <InlineMath math="B" /> has already occurred is the same as the probability of <InlineMath math="A" /> occurring in general.
### Separate Random Trials

Examples with a clear **independent mechanism** include:

- tossing two separate fair coins
- drawing one card from each of two independently shuffled decks
- rolling two separate fair dice
- generating two random digits with independent trials
### Identifying Independent Events

To test independence, ask: “Does knowing the first result change the probability distribution of the second?” A suspected causal link alone is not enough. Use the stated model or compare the conditional probability with the ordinary probability.

For an example from **one fair die roll**, take <InlineMath math="A=\{2,4,6\}" /> and <InlineMath math="B=\{3,6\}" />. The table shows membership for all six outcomes.

| Outcome | Belongs to A | Belongs to B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | No | No |
| 2 | Yes | No |
| 3 | No | Yes |
| 4 | Yes | No |
| 5 | No | No |
| 6 | Yes | Yes |

<BlockMath math="P(A)=\frac36=\frac12,\quad P(B)=\frac26=\frac13,\quad P(A\cap B)=\frac16=\frac12\times\frac13" />

The events are independent even though they come from one roll and overlap at <InlineMath math="\{6\}" />.

## Formula for Probability of Independent Events

Since independent events do not affect each other, calculating their joint probability becomes simple. The **basic formula** for the probability of independent events is:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />

This formula shows that the probability of both events <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> occurring together equals the multiplication of the probability of each event.

Picture all outcomes as a unit square of probability. Event <InlineMath math="A" />, an even first die, takes the left half, width <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" />. Event <InlineMath math="B" />, a second die of 1 or 2, takes the bottom third, height <InlineMath math="\tfrac13" />. Because the dice do not affect each other, the overlap is a rectangle of area <InlineMath math="\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac16" />, which is <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B)" />.

### Why Multiplying the Probabilities Works

For independent events, the **conditional probability** equals the ordinary probability. Because <InlineMath math="P(A|B) = P(A)" />, the multiplication rule becomes:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A|B) \times P(B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />

Substituting <InlineMath math="P(A|B)=P(A)" /> into the multiplication rule gives <InlineMath math="P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)" />.
## Calculations with Independent Events

Each calculation below **multiplies the probabilities** of two independent events. The examples move from two dice to a die and a coin, then to two sensor self tests.
### Rolling Two Dice

Two fair dice are rolled independently, one red die and one white die. Find the probability of getting number <InlineMath math="2" /> on the red die and number <InlineMath math="5" /> on the white die.

**Solution**:

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: getting number <InlineMath math="2" /> on the red die
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: getting number <InlineMath math="5" /> on the white die

Both events are **independent** because the result of the red die does not affect the result of the white die.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{1}{6}" />
<BlockMath math="P(B) = \frac{1}{6}" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36}" />
</MathContainer>

So the probability of getting number <InlineMath math="2" /> on the red die and number <InlineMath math="5" /> on the white die is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{36}" />.

### Rolling a Die and Coin

A fair die and a fair coin are thrown independently. Find the probability of getting an even number on the die and tails on the coin.

**Solution**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S_1 = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}" />
<BlockMath math="S_2 = \{0, 1\}" />
</MathContainer>

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: getting an even number on the die is <InlineMath math="\{2, 4, 6\}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: getting tails on the coin, represented by <InlineMath math="1" />, is <InlineMath math="\{1\}" />

Both events are **independent** because the result of the die does not affect the result of the coin.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="P(B) = \frac{1}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}" />
</MathContainer>

Roll a die and toss a coin: there are <InlineMath math="6 \cdot 2 = 12" /> equally likely pairs. The highlighted three are an even number with heads, so <InlineMath math="P = \frac{3}{12} = \frac12 \cdot \frac12" />. Each column of the grid has the same share of heads, which is what independence looks like.

### Independent Sensor Self Tests

Two independently powered monitoring sensors run a daily self-test. Sensor <InlineMath math="A" /> succeeds with probability <InlineMath math="0.98" />, while sensor <InlineMath math="B" /> succeeds with probability <InlineMath math="0.92" />. Under the stated independence model, what is the probability that both tests succeed?

The independence assumption is part of this model. If both sensors shared a power supply or network, we would need to test that assumption again.

**Solution**:

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: the first sensor passes its self-test
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: the second sensor passes its self-test

The problem states that the two self-tests are **independent**, so one result does not change the probability of the other.

**Detailed calculation**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />
<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.98 \times 0.92 = 0.9016" />
</MathContainer>

So the probability that both sensors pass is <InlineMath math="0.9016" /> or <InlineMath math="90.16\%" />.

## How to Work Through an Independence Problem

Multiplying probabilities can give the wrong answer if **independence has not been checked**. The steps below put that check first and end by verifying the answer. If the check fails, the multiplication rule for independent events does not apply at all.

### Check Independence Before Multiplying

To solve probability problems involving independent events, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the events** involved in the problem
2. **Verify independence** from the stated model or the conditional probabilities
3. **Calculate individual probabilities** of each event
4. **Apply the formula** <InlineMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)" />
5. **Check the result** to see if it makes sense in the problem context

### Checks for the Final Answer

The probability that **both events occur** cannot exceed either event probability. The intersection contains only outcomes already included in each event.

<BlockMath math="0\le P(A\cap B)\le\min\{P(A),P(B)\}" />

In the sensor example, <InlineMath math="0.9016\le0.92" />, so the bound holds. This bound **does not prove independence**. Independence must still come from the model or equality of conditional probabilities.

### Recognizing Independent Events

Pay attention to the following characteristics to identify independent events:

**Independent under the stated model**:
- separate fair-coin tosses
- card draws with replacement and reshuffling
- rolls of separate fair dice
- independent random-number trials

**Situations where independence must not be assumed**:
- Drawing cards without replacement
- A person's height and weight, unless a model or data justify independence
- Test scores of the same student in different subjects, unless independence is established
- Air temperature and humidity, unless independence is established for the stated model

## Exercises

1. Two fair coins are tossed independently. Find the probability of getting heads on the first coin and tails on the second coin.

2. A fair die is rolled independently of drawing one card from a shuffled standard deck. Calculate the probability of getting a prime number on the die and a red card.

3. In a class, the probability of a student passing mathematics is <InlineMath math="0.85" /> and the probability of passing physics is <InlineMath math="0.78" />. If both subjects are independent, what is the probability that the student passes both subjects?

4. Three fair coins are tossed independently. Find the probability of getting exactly two tails.

Try each exercise before reading its worked solution. State **why the events are independent** before multiplying their probabilities.
### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: heads on the first coin, <InlineMath math="P(A) = \frac{1}{2}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: tails on the second coin, <InlineMath math="P(B) = \frac{1}{2}" />

Both events are independent because the result of the first coin does not affect the second coin.

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}" />

**Problem 2**

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: prime number on the die is <InlineMath math="\{2, 3, 5\}" />, so <InlineMath math="P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: red card (hearts and diamonds), so <InlineMath math="P(B) = \frac{26}{52} = \frac{1}{2}" />

Both events are independent.

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}" />

**Problem 3**

- Event <InlineMath math="A" />: passing mathematics, <InlineMath math="P(A) = 0.85" />
- Event <InlineMath math="B" />: passing physics, <InlineMath math="P(B) = 0.78" />

Since both subjects are independent:

<BlockMath math="P(A \cap B) = 0.85 \times 0.78 = 0.663" />

So the probability of passing both subjects is <InlineMath math="0.663" /> or <InlineMath math="66.3\%" />.

**Problem 4**

**Method 1: Direct Enumeration**

Use <InlineMath math="0" /> for heads and <InlineMath math="1" /> for tails. The sample space for <InlineMath math="3" /> coins contains <InlineMath math="8" /> outcomes:

<BlockMath math="\left\{\begin{gathered}
000, 001, 010, 011, \\
100, 101, 110, 111
\end{gathered}\right\}" />

Exactly two tails occurs in <InlineMath math="\{011, 101, 110\}" />, so there are <InlineMath math="3" /> favorable outcomes.

<BlockMath math="P(\text{exactly 2 tails}) = \frac{3}{8}" />

**Method 2: Using Binomial Distribution**

For <InlineMath math="n = 3" /> tosses with success probability (tails) <InlineMath math="p = \frac{1}{2}" />, the probability of exactly <InlineMath math="k = 2" /> successes is:

<BlockMath math="P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} \times p^k \times (1-p)^{n-k}" />

Substituting values:

<BlockMath math="P(\text{exactly 2 tails}) = \binom{3}{2} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^1" />

**Step-by-step explanation**:
- <InlineMath math="\binom{3}{2} = 3" /> (ways to choose <InlineMath math="2" /> positions out of <InlineMath math="3" /> for tails)
- <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}" /> (probability of <InlineMath math="2" /> tails)
- <InlineMath math="\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^1 = \frac{1}{2}" /> (probability of <InlineMath math="1" /> head)

So, the calculation is:

<BlockMath math="P(\text{exactly 2 tails}) = 3 \times \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = 3 \times \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8}" />

So the probability of getting exactly two tails is <InlineMath math="\frac{3}{8}" />.
