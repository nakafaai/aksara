## The Formula for Permutations with Identical Objects

If <InlineMath math="n" /> objects split into groups of identical objects, with <InlineMath math="r_1" /> objects of the first kind, <InlineMath math="r_2" /> of the second, and so on, **the number of distinct arrangements** is

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{n!}{r_1! \, r_2! \cdots r_k!}, \qquad r_1 + r_2 + \cdots + r_k = n" />

For example, the letters of MAMA have <InlineMath math="n = 4" /> with two M and two A, so the number of arrangements is <InlineMath math="\frac{4!}{2! \, 2!} = \frac{24}{4} = 6" />. That is **fewer** than <InlineMath math="4! = 24" />, because swapping two identical letters does not create a new arrangement.

## Why You Divide by the Factorial of Each Group

Give the identical letters temporary labels: <InlineMath math="M_1, M_2, A_1, A_2" />. With labels, the four letters are all different and can be arranged in <InlineMath math="4! = 24" /> ways. Once the labels are removed, the arrangements <InlineMath math="M_1 A_1 M_2 A_2" /> and <InlineMath math="M_2 A_1 M_1 A_2" /> become the same word, MAMA.

Each visible arrangement appears exactly <InlineMath math="2! \times 2! = 4" /> times among the 24 labelled ones: <InlineMath math="2!" /> ways to swap the two M and <InlineMath math="2!" /> ways to swap the two A. So the number of visible arrangements is <InlineMath math="\frac{24}{4} = 6" />. You can check this by listing every position of the letter M:

| Positions of M | Visible arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| 1 and 2 | MMAA |
| 1 and 3 | MAMA |
| 1 and 4 | MAAM |
| 2 and 3 | AMMA |
| 2 and 4 | AMAM |
| 3 and 4 | AAMM |

The table holds **exactly six arrangements**, the same as the formula. Dividing by <InlineMath math="r_i!" /> removes every swap inside one group that the eye cannot see.

## Example with the Letters of KALIMANTAN

How many distinct arrangements can be made from the letters of KALIMANTAN?

The word has <InlineMath math="n = 10" /> letters. A appears <InlineMath math="3" /> times, N appears <InlineMath math="2" /> times, and K, L, I, M, T appear once each. Substitute into the formula:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P = \frac{10!}{3! \, 2!} = \frac{3\,628\,800}{6 \times 2}" />
  <BlockMath math="P = \frac{3\,628\,800}{12} = 302\,400" />
</MathContainer>

Check by choosing positions. Choose <InlineMath math="3" /> of the <InlineMath math="10" /> positions for A, then <InlineMath math="2" /> of the remaining <InlineMath math="7" /> for N, then arrange the five other letters in the last five positions:

<BlockMath math="\binom{10}{3} \times \binom{7}{2} \times 5! = 120 \times 21 \times 120 = 302\,400" />

Both methods give **the same result**, so no arrangement is counted twice or missed.

## Example with BANANA and a Condition

The word BANANA has <InlineMath math="n = 6" /> letters: one B, three A, and two N. Without a condition, the number of arrangements is <InlineMath math="\frac{6!}{1! \, 3! \, 2!} = \frac{720}{12} = 60" />.

A condition changes what you arrange. If B must come first, the first position is filled, and only ANANA is left to arrange:

<BlockMath math="\frac{5!}{3! \, 2!} = \frac{120}{12} = 10" />

If the two N must stand together, treat NN as **one block**. The objects to arrange become B, A, A, A, and the block NN, which is five objects with three identical A:

<BlockMath math="\frac{5!}{3!} = \frac{120}{6} = 20" />

The block NN is not multiplied by <InlineMath math="2!" />, because swapping the two N inside the block does not change the word. Check the first result by symmetry. B is one of six letters, so B comes first in <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6}" /> of all arrangements, and <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6} \times 60 = 10" />.

## Two Kinds of Objects and Combinations

When there are only two kinds of objects, **the formula equals a** [combination](/en/subjects/mathematics/combinatorics/combination). Arranging three A and two B means choosing <InlineMath math="2" /> of the <InlineMath math="5" /> positions for B, and the rest are A automatically:

<BlockMath math="\frac{5!}{3! \, 2!} = \binom{5}{2} = 10" />

This link appears in many problems. The number of shortest paths across a <InlineMath math="3 \times 2" /> grid, from the bottom left corner to the top right corner, is the number of arrangements of the steps RRRUU (three right, two up), which is <InlineMath math="10" /> paths.

## How It Differs from Ordinary Permutations

A [permutation of all the objects](/en/subjects/mathematics/combinatorics/permutation-of-n-items-from-n-objects) treats every object as different, so the number of arrangements is <InlineMath math="n!" />. Permutations with identical objects are the more general case: if every group holds a single object, all <InlineMath math="r_i! = 1" /> and the formula turns back into <InlineMath math="n!" />.

| Objects arranged | Formula | Arrangements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A, B, C, D (all different) | <InlineMath math="4!" /> | <InlineMath math="24" /> |
| A, A, B, C (two A identical) | <InlineMath math="\frac{4!}{2!}" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| A, A, B, B (two identical pairs) | <InlineMath math="\frac{4!}{2! \, 2!}" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |

Each extra identical object **divides** the result again, because more swaps become invisible.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using <InlineMath math="n!" /> although some letters repeat | Swapping identical letters is counted as a new arrangement | Count the frequency of each letter first |
| Miscounting a letter's frequency | MATEMATIKA has three A, not two | The frequencies must add up to <InlineMath math="n" /> |
| Adding the factorials in the denominator | The denominator is <InlineMath math="3! \times 2!" />, not <InlineMath math="3! + 2!" /> | Each group swaps independently, so the swaps multiply |
| Multiplying the block NN by <InlineMath math="2!" /> | The two N are identical, so swapping them gives no new word | Multiply by the inner arrangements only when the block holds different objects |
| Forgetting a condition that fixes a position | If B must come first, B is no longer arranged | Reduce <InlineMath math="n" /> and the frequencies for objects whose position is fixed |

The strongest check is **counting by choosing positions**, as in the KALIMANTAN example. The result must match the factorial formula.

## Practice Problems

Count the frequency of each element first, then **make sure the frequencies add up to <InlineMath math="n" />**.

1. How many distinct arrangements can be made from the letters of MATEMATIKA?

2. A flower shop arranges <InlineMath math="8" /> roses in a row. Only their colors are distinguished: <InlineMath math="3" /> red, <InlineMath math="3" /> white, and <InlineMath math="2" /> yellow. How many color arrangements are possible?

3. Six-digit numbers are formed from the digits <InlineMath math="1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3" />. How many numbers can be formed, and how many of them are greater than <InlineMath math="300\,000" />?

4. How many distinct arrangements can be made from the letters of INDONESIA?

### Solutions

**Problem 1**. Count **the frequency of each letter** first. MATEMATIKA has <InlineMath math="10" /> letters: M twice, A three times, T twice, and E, I, K once each. Together that is <InlineMath math="2 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 10" />.

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{10!}{2! \, 3! \, 2!} = \frac{3\,628\,800}{24} = 151\,200" />

Check by choosing positions: <InlineMath math="\binom{10}{3}\binom{7}{2}\binom{5}{2} \times 3! = 120 \times 21 \times 10 \times 6 = 151\,200" />.

**Problem 2**. There are <InlineMath math="8" /> roses in three color groups:

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{8!}{3! \, 3! \, 2!} = \frac{40\,320}{72} = 560" />

Check: choose <InlineMath math="3" /> red positions from <InlineMath math="8" />, then <InlineMath math="3" /> white positions from <InlineMath math="5" />, and the rest are yellow, so <InlineMath math="\binom{8}{3}\binom{5}{3} = 56 \times 10 = 560" />.

**Problem 3**. The digit 1 appears twice, 2 three times, and 3 once, so there are <InlineMath math="\frac{6!}{2! \, 3!} = \frac{720}{12} = 60" /> numbers. A number greater than <InlineMath math="300\,000" /> must start with 3, because any other leading digit gives a number below <InlineMath math="300\,000" />. The rest, <InlineMath math="1, 1, 2, 2, 2" />, can be arranged in <InlineMath math="\frac{5!}{2! \, 3!} = 10" /> ways. Check: the digit 3 comes first in <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6}" /> of all arrangements, and <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6} \times 60 = 10" />.

**Problem 4**. INDONESIA has <InlineMath math="9" /> letters: I twice, N twice, and D, O, E, S, A once each.

<BlockMath math="P = \frac{9!}{2! \, 2!} = \frac{362\,880}{4} = 90\,720" />

Check: the frequencies add up to <InlineMath math="2 + 2 + 5 \times 1 = 9" />, the number of letters.
