## Slope at a Point on a Curve

Picture a bicycle moving along a hilly road. Some stretches climb steeply, while others are almost flat. The road's **slope** changes from point to point. A curved function graph behaves in much the same way.

For a straight line, the slope is the same at every point. For a curved line, the slope changes from point to point. A **derivative** gives the slope or rate of change at **one specific point** on a curve.

## Gradient of a Secant Line

We begin with a **secant line**, a straight line through two different points of a curve.

Suppose the curve represents <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" />. Choose two distinct points, <InlineMath math="P(x, f(x))" /> and <InlineMath math="Q(x+\Delta x, f(x+\Delta x))" />, on that curve. Here, <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> (read "delta x") is the horizontal change from <InlineMath math="P" /> to <InlineMath math="Q" />, so <InlineMath math="\Delta x \ne 0" />.

The slope of the secant line through <InlineMath math="P" /> and <InlineMath math="Q" /> is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} m_{\text{secant}} &amp;= \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \\ &amp;= \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \end{aligned}" />

This slope is the **average rate of change** of <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> between <InlineMath math="P" /> and <InlineMath math="Q" />.

## From Secant Line to Tangent Line

Imagine choosing <InlineMath math="Q" /> on the parabola closer and closer to <InlineMath math="P" />. The horizontal separation <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> then approaches zero. We can calculate the effect of choosing different points while keeping the same parabola.

As <InlineMath math="\Delta x \to 0" /> (read "delta x approaches zero"), the secant lines approach the **tangent line** at <InlineMath math="P" />. The tangent line shows the curve's local direction at that point. It need not intersect the entire curve only once. What matters is its local behavior near <InlineMath math="P" />.

The tangent's slope is the **instantaneous rate of change** of the curve at <InlineMath math="P" />. We find it with a **limit**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\text{tangent}} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \end{aligned}" />

For the plotted parabola, fix <InlineMath math="P=(1,1)" /> and write <InlineMath math="h=\Delta x" />. The second point is <InlineMath math="Q=(1+h,(1+h)^2)" />. As long as <InlineMath math="h\ne0" />, the secant slope simplifies to:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\frac{(1+h)^2-1}{h}&=\frac{2h+h^2}{h}\\&=2+h\end{aligned}" />

We can cancel the factor <InlineMath math="h" /> because the two points are still distinct. The table compares slopes with <InlineMath math="Q" /> to the left and to the right of <InlineMath math="P" />.

| <InlineMath math="h" /> | Secant slope <InlineMath math="2+h" /> |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="-0.5" /> | <InlineMath math="1.5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="-0.1" /> | <InlineMath math="1.9" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0.1" /> | <InlineMath math="2.1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="0.5" /> | <InlineMath math="2.5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |

As the **horizontal separation approaches zero from both sides**, the slope approaches <InlineMath math="2" />. The tangent slope at <InlineMath math="P" /> is therefore <InlineMath math="2" />. We take the limit after simplifying the fraction, so no division by zero occurs.

Tabulate the secant slope <InlineMath math="2 + h" /> as <InlineMath math="Q" /> slides toward <InlineMath math="P" />. From <InlineMath math="h = 1" /> down to <InlineMath math="h = -0.5" /> the bars close in on <InlineMath math="2" /> from both sides, the slope of the tangent at <InlineMath math="P" />.

## Definition of the Derivative

<Highlight>[The limit](/en/subjects/mathematics/limit/concept-of-limit-function) of the secant slopes</Highlight> as <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> approaches zero is called the **derivative**.

The derivative of a function <InlineMath math="f(x)" />, denoted as <InlineMath math="f'(x)" /> (read "f prime x"), is defined as:

<BlockMath math="f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}" />

Finding a derivative is called **differentiation**.

The derivative <InlineMath math="f'(x)" /> is a new function. At every value of <InlineMath math="x" /> where the limit exists, it gives the **instantaneous rate of change** of <InlineMath math="f(x)" />, which is also the slope of the tangent line there. Differential calculus uses the derivative function to analyze both quantities.

As <InlineMath math="Q" /> slides toward <InlineMath math="P" />, the secants turn toward the tangent. <InlineMath math="h = 1" /> gives slope <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="h = \tfrac12" /> gives <InlineMath math="2.5" />, and the tangent at <InlineMath math="P" /> has slope <InlineMath math="2" />: the limit of the secant slopes.
