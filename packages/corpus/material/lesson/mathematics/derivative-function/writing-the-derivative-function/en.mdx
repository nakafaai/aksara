## Leibniz and Lagrange Notation for the Same Derivative

A derivative describes how one quantity changes with respect to another. Mathematicians use several notations for this idea. The two forms most often used for functions are **Leibniz notation** and **Lagrange prime notation**.

The names matter. The prime symbol in <InlineMath math="f'(x)" /> is associated with Joseph-Louis Lagrange. Isaac Newton used dot notation, such as <InlineMath math="\dot{x}" />, mainly for quantities that change with time. The symbols look different, but each notation can describe a derivative when its variable and context are clear.

## Two Common Derivative Notations

Both notations describe <Highlight>the same derivative</Highlight>. Leibniz notation names the changing quantity and the variable of differentiation. Lagrange notation attaches prime symbols to the function name, which keeps repeated derivatives compact.

### Leibniz Notation

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced a notation that shows the variables explicitly. If <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" />, its derivative can be written as:

<BlockMath math="\frac{dy}{dx} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{df}{dx}" />

We read <InlineMath math="\frac{dy}{dx}" /> as "the derivative of <InlineMath math="y" /> with respect to <InlineMath math="x" />." The numerator names the quantity that changes, while the denominator names its reference variable. This distinction identifies the **variable of differentiation** when a problem contains several related variables.

### Lagrange Prime Notation

Lagrange prime notation marks a derivative with a prime symbol. If <InlineMath math="y = f(x)" />, its derivative can be written as:

<BlockMath math="y' \quad \text{or} \quad f'(x)" />

We read these as "<InlineMath math="y" /> prime" and "<InlineMath math="f" /> prime of <InlineMath math="x" />." Prime notation is compact and convenient when the **independent variable** is already clear. A second derivative then becomes <InlineMath math="f''(x)" />, so repeated differentiation is easy to recognize.

## Choosing the Right Notation

Use **Lagrange prime notation**, such as <InlineMath math="f'(x)" />, when the function and its independent variable are already unambiguous. It keeps routine calculations short and readable.

Use **Leibniz notation**, such as <InlineMath math="\frac{dy}{dx}" />, when the relationship between variables matters. The denominator identifies the variable of differentiation, which is useful in chain-rule, related-rate, and multivariable problems.

## Calculating with Both Notations

Take <InlineMath math="y=f(x)=x^3-2x" />. By the power rule, the derivative of <InlineMath math="x^3" /> is <InlineMath math="3x^2" />, and the derivative of <InlineMath math="-2x" /> is <InlineMath math="-2" />. Both notations record the same calculation:

<BlockMath math="f'(x)=\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-2" />

Distinguish the **derivative function** from the **derivative at one point**. To find the slope at <InlineMath math="x=2" />, substitute that value into the derivative function.

<BlockMath math="f'(2)=\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right\rvert_{x=2}=3(2)^2-2=10" />

The vertical bar in Leibniz notation means that the derivative is evaluated at <InlineMath math="x=2" />. Both <InlineMath math="f'(2)" /> and <InlineMath math="\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right\rvert_{x=2}" /> therefore represent the number <InlineMath math="10" />.

| Quantity | Lagrange notation | Leibniz notation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Derivative function | <InlineMath math="f'(x)=3x^2-2" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-2" /> |
| Derivative at one point | <InlineMath math="f'(2)=10" /> | <InlineMath math="\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right\rvert_{x=2}=10" /> |
| Second derivative | <InlineMath math="f''(x)=6x" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=6x" /> |

For the last row, we differentiate <InlineMath math="3x^2-2" /> once more. The superscripts in Leibniz notation indicate **differentiating twice**. They do not mean squaring the first derivative.

Both notations name the same slope function. The orange curve is <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^3 - 2x" /> with its tangent at <InlineMath math="x = 1" />, which rises 1 for every 1 across. The violet curve is <InlineMath math="f'(x) = \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 - 2" />, and its height at <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> is that same slope, <InlineMath math="1" />.
