## Understanding Motion Through Derivatives

Derivatives describe how a quantity **changes**. Motion gives a concrete example: from an object's position function, we can determine its instantaneous velocity and then its acceleration.

## Instantaneous Velocity from Position

As a car moves, its position changes over time. **Velocity** is the rate of change of that position. If <InlineMath math="s(t)" /> describes position, then the instantaneous velocity <InlineMath math="v(t)" /> at time <InlineMath math="t" /> is the first derivative of the position function.

<BlockMath math="v(t) = s'(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}" />

At every time where <InlineMath math="s" /> is differentiable, this derivative gives the velocity at that exact moment. An average velocity uses a time interval.

### Determining the Velocity Function

For example, the motion of a particle is determined by the position function <InlineMath math="s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t" />, where <InlineMath math="s" /> is in meters and <InlineMath math="t" /> is in seconds. To find its velocity function, we simply differentiate the function <InlineMath math="s(t)" /> with respect to <InlineMath math="t" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="v(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t)" />
  <BlockMath math="v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9" />
</MathContainer>

At <InlineMath math="t = 1 \text{ second}" />, the velocity is <InlineMath math="v(1) = 3(1)^2 - 12(1) + 9 = 0 \text{ m/s}" />. A zero velocity means the particle is momentarily at rest, but it does not by itself prove a change of direction. Factor the velocity to inspect its sign on either side of <InlineMath math="t=1" />:

<BlockMath math="v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)" />

For <InlineMath math="0<t<1" />, both factors are negative, so <InlineMath math="v(t)>0" />. For <InlineMath math="1<t<3" />, the factors have opposite signs, so <InlineMath math="v(t)<0" />. The velocity therefore changes sign at <InlineMath math="t=1" />, which proves that the particle reverses direction there.

## Acceleration from Velocity

Velocity can itself change, as when a car speeds up or brakes. Its rate of change is called **acceleration**. The acceleration <InlineMath math="a(t)" /> is the first derivative of velocity and therefore the second derivative of position.

<BlockMath math="a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}" />

### Determining the Acceleration Function

Continuing with the previous particle example, we already have the velocity function <InlineMath math="v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9" />. We can find its acceleration function by differentiating the function <InlineMath math="v(t)" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="a(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(3t^2 - 12t + 9)" />
  <BlockMath math="a(t) = 6t - 12" />
</MathContainer>

From this, we can find the particle's acceleration at any time. For example, at <InlineMath math="t = 3 \text{ seconds}" />, its acceleration is <InlineMath math="a(3) = 6(3) - 12 = 6 \text{ meters/second}^2" />.

## Peak Application in Vertical Motion

A ball is thrown straight up. Its height, <InlineMath math="h(t)" /> in meters, after <InlineMath math="t \text{ seconds}" /> is given by the equation <InlineMath math="h(t) = 50t - 5t^2" />.

First, we can find its velocity and acceleration functions.

Velocity function:

<BlockMath math="v(t) = h'(t) = 50 - 10t" />

Acceleration function:

<BlockMath math="a(t) = v'(t) = -10" />

> This classroom model chooses upward as the positive direction, ignores air resistance, and uses a constant acceleration of <InlineMath math="-10 \text{ m/s}^2" /> near Earth's surface. The negative sign means the acceleration points downward. The commonly used near-surface approximation is <InlineMath math="-9.8 \text{ m/s}^2" />; this model rounds it to <InlineMath math="-10 \text{ m/s}^2" />.

Because <InlineMath math="h(0)=0" /> and <InlineMath math="h(10)=0" />, the modeled flight runs from launch at <InlineMath math="t=0" /> until the ball returns to its starting height at <InlineMath math="t=10" />.

We now determine **when the ball reaches its maximum height**.

The ball reaches its highest point exactly when it stops for a moment to change direction and fall back down. In other words, this occurs when its velocity is exactly zero.

<MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="v(t) = 0" />
    <BlockMath math="50 - 10t = 0" />
    <BlockMath math="10t = 50" />
    <BlockMath math="t = 5" />
</MathContainer>

So, the ball reaches its peak at <InlineMath math="5 \text{ seconds}" />. To find out what the maximum height is, we substitute <InlineMath math="t=5" /> back into the initial height function.

<BlockMath math="h(5) = 50(5) - 5(5)^2 = 250 - 125 = 125" />

Thus, the maximum height is **<InlineMath math="125 \text{ meters}" />**. The calculation follows a complete chain: differentiate position, solve for zero velocity within the physical time interval, and substitute that time into the original height function.
