## An Abacus Out of Room

In **base five**, you count in groups of five. One spike of an abacus may hold only four beads. As soon as a fifth bead arrives, all five beads come off and one bead goes onto the spike to the left.

So **base** <InlineMath math="b" /> uses only the digits <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="b - 1" />, and each place is worth <InlineMath math="b" /> times the place to its right. The numeral <InlineMath math="123_5" /> means one group of <InlineMath math="25" />, two groups of <InlineMath math="5" /> and three ones, worth <InlineMath math="38" />. Move that number between base five and base ten here.

Why does a spike stop at four beads? Five beads on the ones spike already make one group of five, which has its own spike. With five beads allowed, five would have two conflicting spellings. The rule of **at most <InlineMath math="b - 1" /> beads on each spike** gives every number one unique spelling.

Now pick base ten. The same number regroups into three beads on the tens spike and eight beads on the ones spike. **How many things you have doesn't change**, only the size of the groups does.

Go back to base five and slide the number to <InlineMath math="39" />, then <InlineMath math="40" />. At <InlineMath math="40" /> the ones spike is empty again, because five beads have just turned into one bead on the second spike.

> Changing the base only changes the size of each group, never the number of things you are counting.

## Place Value and Digit Value

Every digit in a numeral has two values. Its **place value** is what one bead is worth on the spike where the digit stands. Its **digit value** is the digit times its place value, which is what all the beads on that spike are worth.

Take the digit <InlineMath math="2" /> in <InlineMath math="123_5" />. It stands on the second spike, so its place value is <InlineMath math="5" /> and its digit value is <InlineMath math="2 \times 5 = 10" />.

### Base Powers per Spike

The rightmost spike counts ones. One bead on the next spike stands for a full group from the spike to its right, so it's worth <InlineMath math="b" /> times as much. That makes the place values, from right to left, <InlineMath math="b^0, b^1, b^2, b^3" />: **powers of the base starting from zero**.

| Spike | Power | Base <InlineMath math="2" /> | Base <InlineMath math="5" /> | Base <InlineMath math="8" /> | Base <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fourth | <InlineMath math="b^3" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="125" /> | <InlineMath math="512" /> | <InlineMath math="1{,}000" /> |
| Third | <InlineMath math="b^2" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="25" /> | <InlineMath math="64" /> | <InlineMath math="100" /> |
| Second | <InlineMath math="b^1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| First, the ones | <InlineMath math="b^0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |

Why is the ones spike worth <InlineMath math="b^0 = 1" /> and not <InlineMath math="b" />? A bead there is still a single ungrouped item, and the first group only appears on the second spike.

It's the same pattern as [powers and exponents](/en/subjects/mathematics/exponential-logarithm/basic-concept): each step left multiplies by the base once more. When labeling places, **start the powers at <InlineMath math="0" /> on the right** so every place doesn't shift one power too high.

Try one: what are the place value and digit value of <InlineMath math="4" /> in <InlineMath math="2431_5" />? The <InlineMath math="4" /> stands in the third place from the right (<InlineMath math="5^2 = 25" />), so **its place value is <InlineMath math="25" />** and its digit value is <InlineMath math="4 \times 25 = 100" />. Every digit is under <InlineMath math="5" />, so it's valid.

### Expanded Form as a Sum

Add up all the digit values and you get the number's value in base ten. Writing that sum out is called the **expanded form**. Take <InlineMath math="3214_5" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 3214_5 &= 3 \times 5^3 + 2 \times 5^2 + 1 \times 5^1 + 4 \times 5^0 \\ &= 375 + 50 + 5 + 4 \\ &= 434 \end{aligned}" />

The digit value of <InlineMath math="2" /> here is <InlineMath math="50" />, not <InlineMath math="10" />, because it stands in the <InlineMath math="5^2" /> place. Multiplying every digit by <InlineMath math="5" /> lands far from <InlineMath math="434" />.

<BlockMath math="3 \times 5 + 2 \times 5 + 1 \times 5 + 4 = 34 \neq 434" />

**Pair every digit with its power of the base** before you multiply. Check your answer on the abacus by picking base five and sliding to <InlineMath math="434" />. From the left, the spikes hold three, two, one and four beads.

The second example uses base eight and contains a zero.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 2075_8 &= 2 \times 8^3 + 0 \times 8^2 + 7 \times 8^1 + 5 \times 8^0 \\ &= 1{,}024 + 0 + 56 + 5 \\ &= 1{,}085 \end{aligned}" />

The <InlineMath math="0" /> adds nothing, but **the zero still holds a place** for the other digits. Without it you'd have <InlineMath math="275_8" />, which is only worth <InlineMath math="189" />. Count places before and after copying so four digits stay four digits.

Check the result by taking <InlineMath math="1{,}085" /> apart from the largest place. Since <InlineMath math="61" /> is less than <InlineMath math="64" />, the <InlineMath math="8^2" /> place holds <InlineMath math="0" />, and the digits come back as <InlineMath math="2, 0, 7, 5" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 1{,}085 - 2 \times 512 &= 61 \\ 61 - 0 \times 64 &= 61 \\ 61 - 7 \times 8 &= 5 \end{aligned}" />

Now write <InlineMath math="6053_7" /> in expanded form yourself. Pair each digit with a power of seven, starting from <InlineMath math="7^0" /> on the right.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 6053_7 &= 6 \times 7^3 + 0 \times 7^2 + 5 \times 7^1 + 3 \times 7^0 \\ &= 2{,}058 + 0 + 35 + 3 \\ &= 2{,}096 \end{aligned}" />

Check by taking the number apart from the largest place. Since <InlineMath math="38" /> is less than <InlineMath math="49" />, the <InlineMath math="7^2" /> place holds <InlineMath math="0" /> and **you get the digits back** as <InlineMath math="6, 0, 5, 3" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 2{,}096 - 6 \times 343 &= 38 \\ 38 - 0 \times 49 &= 38 \\ 38 - 5 \times 7 &= 3 \end{aligned}" />

## Same Digits in Other Bases

The numeral <InlineMath math="123" /> has no single fixed value. Its value depends on the base that comes with it, because the base sets how big each place is.

<BlockMath math="123_b = 1 \times b^2 + 2 \times b + 3" />

| Base | Expanded form | Value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="1 \times 16 + 2 \times 4 + 3" /> | <InlineMath math="27" /> |
| <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="1 \times 25 + 2 \times 5 + 3" /> | <InlineMath math="38" /> |
| <InlineMath math="8" /> | <InlineMath math="1 \times 64 + 2 \times 8 + 3" /> | <InlineMath math="83" /> |
| <InlineMath math="10" /> | <InlineMath math="1 \times 100 + 2 \times 10 + 3" /> | <InlineMath math="123" /> |

The bigger the base, the bigger every place, so **the same digits are worth more in a bigger base**. Slide the base on this graph and watch the point climb faster and faster, because the third place grows as <InlineMath math="b^2" />.

Seven beads on the ones spike in base six mean six beads move to one bead on the second spike, while one bead stays. So seven is written <InlineMath math="11_6" />, **never as the single digit** <InlineMath math="7" />.

## Reading a Numeral Aloud

Words like “hundred” and “twenty” name places in base ten: “hundred” means <InlineMath math="10^2" /> and “twenty” means two tens. In <InlineMath math="123_5" /> the <InlineMath math="1" /> is only worth <InlineMath math="25" />, so saying “one hundred twenty-three” gives the wrong value. **Read the digits one by one, then say the base**.

| Numeral | How to read it | Value in base ten |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="123_5" /> | one two three base five | <InlineMath math="38" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1010_2" /> | one zero one zero base two | <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2075_8" /> | two zero seven five base eight | <InlineMath math="1{,}085" /> |

A numeral with no small base written under it is always read in base ten, so <InlineMath math="38" /> is still “thirty-eight”. Reading digit by digit keeps you from calling <InlineMath math="1010_2" /> “one thousand ten” when it's only worth ten.

## Digits a Base Never Uses

There's only one rule: **every digit must be smaller than the base**. To check it, find the largest digit in the numeral and compare it with the base. If the largest digit is <InlineMath math="d" />, the numeral is only valid in base <InlineMath math="d + 1" /> or higher.

| Numeral | Largest digit | Valid or not | Smallest possible base |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="4302_5" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | Valid, since <InlineMath math="4 < 5" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2061_5" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> | Not valid, since <InlineMath math="6 > 5" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> |
| <InlineMath math="1201_2" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | Not valid, since <InlineMath math="2 = 2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="7345_8" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> | Valid, since <InlineMath math="7 < 8" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> |

Look at <InlineMath math="1201_2" />. Base two only has <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />, because two beads on a spike must already move to the left. If a problem hands you a numeral like that, don't calculate: **that numeral doesn't exist in the base it's written in**.

The rule also tells you which base a numeral could belong to. Take <InlineMath math="3504" /> in an unknown base. Its largest digit is <InlineMath math="5" />, so **the smallest base it could be in is <InlineMath math="6" />**, with place values <InlineMath math="216, 36, 6, 1" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 3504_6 &= 3 \times 216 + 5 \times 36 + 0 \times 6 + 4 \times 1 \\ &= 648 + 180 + 0 + 4 \\ &= 832 \end{aligned}" />

Take <InlineMath math="832" /> apart from the largest place to check the result.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 832 - 3 \times 216 &= 184 \\ 184 - 5 \times 36 &= 4 \end{aligned}" />

The <InlineMath math="4" /> left over is smaller than <InlineMath math="6" />, so the <InlineMath math="6^1" /> place holds <InlineMath math="0" /> and the ones digit is <InlineMath math="4" />.
