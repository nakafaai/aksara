## Application of Integrals in Economics

Economic data often describe a rate, such as units sold per month or additional cost per unit produced. An integral adds these changes over a period of time or a range of production.

For example, integrating a sales rate over three months gives the total number of units sold during those three months.

This concept is similar to calculating the area under a curve, where the horizontal axis represents time and the vertical axis represents the rate of change of an economic quantity.

## Sales Analysis Using Integrals

Learn how integrals can help analyze the sales of a product. Suppose a technology company launches a new smartphone, and the analysis team finds that sales follow a certain pattern.

After conducting market research, they found that the sales rate per month can be modeled with the function <InlineMath math="f(t) = 2000\sqrt{t + 1} + 800 \text{ units}" /> per month, where <InlineMath math="t" /> is the time in months after launch.

To find the **total sales** during the first <InlineMath math="3 \text{ months}" />, integrate the sales rate function:

<BlockMath math="S = \int_{0}^{3} (2000\sqrt{t + 1} + 800) \, dt" />

Solve this by separating the integral first:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S = \int_{0}^{3} 2000\sqrt{t + 1} \, dt + \int_{0}^{3} 800 \, dt" />
<BlockMath math="S = 2000 \int_{0}^{3} (t + 1)^{1/2} \, dt + 800t \Big|_{0}^{3}" />
</MathContainer>

For integrals containing square roots, we can use substitution. Let <InlineMath math="u = t + 1" />, then <InlineMath math="du = dt" />. Don't forget to change the integration limits too!

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S = 2000 \int_{1}^{4} u^{1/2} \, du + 2400" />
<BlockMath math="S = 2000 \left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_{1}^{4} + 2400" />
<BlockMath math="S = \frac{4000}{3}(8 - 1) + 2400 = \frac{28000}{3} + \frac{7200}{3} = \frac{35200}{3}" />
</MathContainer>

The continuous model predicts <InlineMath math="S = \frac{35200}{3} \approx 11733.33" /> smartphone units in the first <InlineMath math="3 \text{ months}" />. Interpreted as whole devices, that is approximately <InlineMath math="11733" /> smartphones.

## Revenue Growth Analysis

A second model gives a technology startup's revenue growth rate as <InlineMath math="R'(t) = 5000e^{0.1t}" /> thousand dollars per month, where <InlineMath math="t" /> is measured in months.

To calculate the **total revenue increase** in the first <InlineMath math="6 \text{ months}" />, we integrate the growth rate function:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="R = \int_{0}^{6} 5000e^{0.1t} \, dt" />
<BlockMath math="R = 5000 \int_{0}^{6} e^{0.1t} \, dt" />
<BlockMath math="R = 5000 \left[\frac{e^{0.1t}}{0.1}\right]_{0}^{6}" />
<BlockMath math="R = 50000(e^{0.6} - 1)" />
</MathContainer>

With <InlineMath math="e^{0.6} \approx 1.822" />, the total revenue increase is approximately <InlineMath math="50000 \times 0.822 = 41100" /> thousand dollars or <InlineMath math="41.1 \text{ million}" /> dollars.

## Total Production Cost

In business, companies often need to estimate production costs. Suppose the marginal cost to produce a good is <InlineMath math="MC(x) = 0.3x^2 - 12x + 200" /> thousand dollars per unit, where <InlineMath math="x" /> is the number of units produced.

Now, if the company wants to know the **total variable cost** to produce the first <InlineMath math="20 \text{ units}" />, they simply integrate the marginal cost function:

<BlockMath math="VC = \int_{0}^{20} (0.3x^2 - 12x + 200) \, dx" />

After we integrate and evaluate, we obtain:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="VC = \left[0.1x^3 - 6x^2 + 200x\right]_{0}^{20}" />
<BlockMath math="VC = 800 - 2400 + 4000 = 2400" />
</MathContainer>

Assuming <InlineMath math="VC(0)=0" />, the total variable cost to produce <InlineMath math="20 \text{ units}" /> is <InlineMath math="2.4 \text{ million}" /> dollars. Any fixed cost is separate and is not included in this integral.

> In economic applications, an integral converts a marginal quantity or rate of change into an accumulated total. Integrating marginal cost, for example, gives the change in total cost.

## Consumer Surplus Analysis

Consumer surplus can also be calculated with an integral. Consider a market with demand function <InlineMath math="P = 100 - 0.5Q" /> and equilibrium price <InlineMath math="60 \text{ dollars per unit}" />. The equilibrium quantity on this demand curve follows from:

<BlockMath math="60=100-0.5Q\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad Q=80" />

Consumer surplus adds, across the <InlineMath math="80" /> units traded, the difference between buyers' willingness to pay given by the demand curve and the market price they actually pay:

<BlockMath math="CS = \int_{0}^{80} (100 - 0.5Q - 60) \, dQ = \int_{0}^{80} (40 - 0.5Q) \, dQ" />

After evaluation:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="CS = \left[40Q - 0.25Q^2\right]_{0}^{80}" />
<BlockMath math="CS = 3200 - 1600 = 1600" />
</MathContainer>

A consumer surplus of <InlineMath math="1600 \text{ dollars}" /> measures the total monetary benefit consumers receive above the equilibrium price.

## Exercises

1. A company has a sales rate of <InlineMath math="f(t) = 1000 + 500t \text{ units}" /> per month. Calculate the total sales in the first <InlineMath math="6 \text{ months}" />!

2. If the marginal cost of a product is <InlineMath math="MC(x) = 2x + 50" /> thousand dollars per unit, what is the total variable cost to produce <InlineMath math="25 \text{ units}" />?

3. The investment growth rate function is <InlineMath math="I'(t) = 2000e^{0.05t}\text{ million}" /> dollars per year. Calculate the total investment growth in <InlineMath math="4 \text{ years}" />!

### Worked Solutions

1. **Calculating total sales**

   Since we have the sales rate function, just integrate it:

   <BlockMath math="S = \int_{0}^{6} (1000 + 500t) \, dt" />

   The result is:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="S = \left[1000t + 250t^2\right]_{0}^{6}" />
   <BlockMath math="S = 6000 + 9000 = 15000" />
   </MathContainer>

   Total sales in <InlineMath math="6 \text{ months}" /> is <InlineMath math="15{,}000 \text{ units}" />.

2. **Calculating variable cost**

   Integrate the marginal cost function:

   <BlockMath math="VC = \int_{0}^{25} (2x + 50) \, dx" />

   After evaluation:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="VC = \left[x^2 + 50x\right]_{0}^{25}" />
   <BlockMath math="VC = 625 + 1250 = 1875" />
   </MathContainer>

   Total variable cost is <InlineMath math="1{,}875" /> thousand dollars or <InlineMath math="1.875 \text{ million}" /> dollars.

3. **Calculating investment growth**

   For exponential functions, we integrate:

   <BlockMath math="I = \int_{0}^{4} 2000e^{0.05t} \, dt" />

   The result is:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="I = 2000 \left[\frac{e^{0.05t}}{0.05}\right]_{0}^{4}" />
   <BlockMath math="I = 40000(e^{0.2} - 1) \approx 40000(0.2214) \approx 8856" />
   </MathContainer>

   Total investment growth in <InlineMath math="4 \text{ years}" /> is approximately <InlineMath math="8.86 \text{ billion dollars}" />.
