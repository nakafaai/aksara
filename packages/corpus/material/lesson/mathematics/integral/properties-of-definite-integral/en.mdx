## Identical Integration Limits

If the upper and lower limits of a definite integral are the same, the result is zero.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 0" />

A definite integral accumulates a quantity across an oriented interval. From <InlineMath math="a" /> to the same point <InlineMath math="a" />, the interval has zero width, so the accumulation is zero regardless of the value of the integrand.

## Reversing the Integration Limits

When we swap the lower and upper limits of an integral, the result is the negative of the original integral's value.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = - \int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx" />

The lower and upper bounds determine the orientation of the integration interval. Swapping the bounds reverses that orientation and changes the sign of every contribution to the accumulation. Therefore, the integral becomes the negative of its original value.

## Constant Multiple Rule

Just as with indefinite integrals, a constant can be factored out of the integral to simplify the calculation.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} kf(x) \, dx = k \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx" />

Multiplying every value of a function by a constant <InlineMath math="k" /> multiplies its accumulated integral by the same factor. We may therefore evaluate <InlineMath math="\int f(x)dx" /> first and then multiply by <InlineMath math="k" />.

## Sum and Difference Rule

The integral of a sum or difference of two functions is equal to the sum or difference of their individual integrals.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} [f(x) + g(x)] \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx + \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \, dx" />
<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx - \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \, dx" />
</MathContainer>

By linearity, a complicated integrand can be split into simpler pieces. Integrate <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> and <InlineMath math="g(x)" /> separately, then combine their signed accumulations with the same operation.

## Interval Addition Property

An integration interval can be split at an intermediate point. The signed accumulation over the whole interval equals the sum over the two parts.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx + \int_{b}^{c} f(x) \, dx" />

This identity holds for any order of <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, and <InlineMath math="c" />, provided the function is integrable on the required interval. In words, the accumulation from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="c" /> equals the accumulation from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="b" /> plus the accumulation from <InlineMath math="b" /> to <InlineMath math="c" />. It is especially useful for piecewise functions because each formula can be integrated on the interval where it applies.
