## Identical Integration Limits

A definite integral is zero when its **upper and lower limits coincide**. This rule applies to a function integrable on the interval under consideration.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 0" />

A definite integral accumulates a quantity across an oriented interval. From <InlineMath math="a" /> to the same point <InlineMath math="a" />, the interval has zero width, so the accumulation is zero regardless of the value of the integrand.

For example, <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" /> has value <InlineMath math="3" /> at <InlineMath math="x=3" />. But the interval from <InlineMath math="3" /> to <InlineMath math="3" /> has **zero width**, so the integral is still zero:

<BlockMath math="\int_3^3 x\,dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_3^3=\frac92-\frac92=0" />

## Reversing the Integration Limits

When we swap the lower and upper limits of an integral, the result is the negative of the original integral's value.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = - \int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx" />

The lower and upper bounds determine the orientation of the integration interval. Swapping the bounds reverses that orientation and <Highlight>changes the sign of every contribution</Highlight> to the accumulation. Therefore, the integral becomes the negative of its original value.

For <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" />, an antiderivative is <InlineMath math="x^2/2" />. Calculating in both directions gives the **same magnitude with opposite signs**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\int_0^2 x\,dx&=\frac{2^2}{2}-\frac{0^2}{2}=2\\\int_2^0 x\,dx&=\frac{0^2}{2}-\frac{2^2}{2}=-2\end{aligned}" />

## Constant Multiple Rule

Just as with indefinite integrals, a constant can be factored out of the integral to simplify the calculation.

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} kf(x) \, dx = k \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx" />

Multiplying every value of a function by a constant <InlineMath math="k" /> multiplies its accumulated integral by the same factor. We may therefore evaluate <InlineMath math="\int_a^b f(x)\,dx" /> first and then multiply by <InlineMath math="k" />.

With <InlineMath math="k=3" />, every contribution becomes **three times as large**. Keep the integration limits unchanged when taking the constant outside:

<BlockMath math="\int_0^2 3x\,dx=3\int_0^2 x\,dx=3(2)=6" />

Doubling the function doubles every height, so the area doubles too. On <InlineMath math="[0, 2]" /> the area under <InlineMath math="y = x + 1" /> is <InlineMath math="4" />, and the area under <InlineMath math="y = 2(x + 1)" /> is <InlineMath math="8 = 2 \cdot 4" />.

## Sum and Difference Rule

The integral of a sum or difference of two functions is equal to the sum or difference of their individual integrals.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \int_{a}^{b} [f(x) + g(x)] \, dx &amp;= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \, dx \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \int_{a}^{b} [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx &amp;= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \\ &amp;\quad{}- \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \, dx \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

By linearity, a complicated integrand can be split into simpler pieces. Integrate <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> and <InlineMath math="g(x)" /> separately, then combine their signed accumulations with the same operation.

For the integrand <InlineMath math="x+1" />, calculate the contributions from <InlineMath math="x" /> and the constant <InlineMath math="1" /> separately. **Preserve the addition** when combining them:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}\int_0^2(x+1)\,dx&=\int_0^2x\,dx+\int_0^2 1\,dx\\&=2+[x]_0^2\\&=2+(2-0)=4\end{aligned}" />

Stack the two pieces. Under <InlineMath math="y = x + 1" /> from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="2" />, the violet rectangle is <InlineMath math="\int_0^2 1\,dx = 2" /> and the orange triangle on top of it is <InlineMath math="\int_0^2 x\,dx = 2" />. Together they fill the whole region, so <InlineMath math="\int_0^2 (x + 1)\,dx = 2 + 2 = 4" />.

## Interval Addition Property

An integration interval can be split at an intermediate point. The signed accumulation over the whole interval equals the sum over the two parts.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx &amp;= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \int_{b}^{c} f(x) \, dx \end{aligned}" />

This identity holds for any order of <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, and <InlineMath math="c" />, provided the function is integrable on the required interval. In words, the accumulation from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="c" /> equals the accumulation from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="b" /> plus the accumulation from <InlineMath math="b" /> to <InlineMath math="c" />. It is especially useful for piecewise functions because each formula can be integrated on the interval where it applies.

Split the interval <InlineMath math="[0,2]" /> at <InlineMath math="x=1" />. The table compares the two partial integrals:

| Interval | Calculation | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="[0,1]" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{1^2}{2}-\frac{0^2}{2}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="[1,2]" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac{2^2}{2}-\frac{1^2}{2}" /> | <InlineMath math="\frac32" /> |

The two parts add to <InlineMath math="\frac12+\frac32=2" />, agreeing with <InlineMath math="\int_0^2 x\,dx" />. The **same split point** is the upper limit of the first integral and the lower limit of the second, so no part of the interval is omitted.

Split an interval and the areas add. Under <InlineMath math="y = x + 1" />, the region from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="2" /> has area <InlineMath math="4" /> and the region from <InlineMath math="2" /> to <InlineMath math="4" /> has area <InlineMath math="8" />. Together they make the whole area <InlineMath math="\int_0^4 (x + 1)\,dx = 12" />.
