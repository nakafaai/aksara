## Integrals Accumulate Changing Quantities

Many physical quantities change continuously. A force may vary with position, for example, so one force value cannot represent the entire path. An **integral** <Highlight>adds the contributions over the chosen interval</Highlight>.

The same idea applies whenever a rate or density is known. Integrating a rate of change gives the total change, while integrating a density gives the amount distributed over a length, area, or volume.

## Calculating Work with Integrals

Start with work. A constant force parallel to a displacement does work <InlineMath math="W=Fs" />. More generally, work is the line integral <InlineMath math="W=\int \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}" />. In one dimension, when force and motion both lie along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis, this becomes <InlineMath math="W=\int F(x)\,dx" />.

Imagine a particle at coordinate <InlineMath math="x" />, measured in meters from the origin. A force <InlineMath math="F(x) = x^2 + 2x" /> Newtons acts along the positive <InlineMath math="x" />-direction. How much work does this force do as the particle moves from <InlineMath math="x = 1 \text{ meter}" /> to <InlineMath math="x = 3 \text{ meters}" />?

Because the **force changes with position**, one constant value of <InlineMath math="F" /> cannot be multiplied by the whole displacement. Instead, we accumulate the work over the path:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} W &amp;= \int_{1}^{3} F(x) \, dx \\ &amp;= \int_{1}^{3} (x^2 + 2x) \, dx \end{aligned}" />

Solve it:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="W = \left[\frac{x^3}{3} + x^2\right]_{1}^{3}" />
<BlockMath math="W = \left(\frac{27}{3} + 9\right) - \left(\frac{1}{3} + 1\right)" />
<BlockMath math="W = (9 + 9) - \left(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{3}{3}\right) = 18 - \frac{4}{3}" />
<BlockMath math="W = \frac{54}{3} - \frac{4}{3} = \frac{50}{3} \text{ Joule}" />
</MathContainer>

So, the force does <InlineMath math="\frac{50}{3}" /> Joules of work, approximately <InlineMath math="16.67" /> Joules.

The force <InlineMath math="F(x) = x^2 + 2x" /> grows along the path, from 3 N at <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> to 15 N at <InlineMath math="x = 3" />. The work <InlineMath math="W = \tfrac{50}{3} \approx 16.67" /> J adds up these changing values over the 2 metres.

## Law of Hooke and Spring Energy

This model uses an **ideal spring in its linear elastic range**. Under Hooke's law, a greater extension or compression requires a greater holding force.

Hooke's law says that the spring's restoring force points opposite the displacement. If the spring is stretched or compressed slowly, the external force needed to hold it at displacement <InlineMath math="x" /> has the same magnitude and the opposite direction:

<BlockMath math="F_s(x)=-kx,\qquad F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)=kx" />

Here <InlineMath math="k" /> is the spring constant and <InlineMath math="x" /> is the signed displacement from equilibrium. Integrating <InlineMath math="F_{\mathrm{ext}}" /> gives the external work done on the spring and therefore the increase in elastic potential energy.

Suppose a force of <InlineMath math="40 \text{ N}" /> is required to hold a spring that has been stretched from its original length of <InlineMath math="10 \text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="15 \text{ cm}" />. Now, what is the work required to stretch the spring from <InlineMath math="15 \text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="18 \text{ cm}" />?

First, we determine the spring constant. The displacement from equilibrium is <InlineMath math="15 - 10 = 5 \text{ cm} = 0.05 \text{ m}" />. Since the holding force has magnitude <InlineMath math="F_{\mathrm{ext}} = kx" />:

<BlockMath math="40 = k \times 0.05" />

So <InlineMath math="k = \frac{40}{0.05} = 800 \text{ N/m}" />.

To calculate the external work required to stretch the spring from <InlineMath math="15 \text{ cm}" /> to <InlineMath math="18 \text{ cm}" />, measure both positions from the equilibrium length:

| Total length | Extension from natural length | Integration limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="15\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="(15-10)\text{ cm}=5\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="0.05\text{ m}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="18\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="(18-10)\text{ cm}=8\text{ cm}" /> | <InlineMath math="0.08\text{ m}" /> |

We can calculate the work with integrals:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="W = \int_{0.05}^{0.08} 800x \, dx" />
<BlockMath math="W = 800 \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{0.05}^{0.08}" />
<BlockMath math="W = 400[(0.08)^2 - (0.05)^2]" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} W &amp;= 400[0.0064 - 0.0025] \\ &amp;= 400 \times 0.0039 \\ &amp;= 1.56 \text{ Joule} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Between 0.05 m and 0.08 m the spring force <InlineMath math="F = 800x" /> rises from 40 N to 64 N. The work is the trapezoid under the force line: <InlineMath math="\frac{40 + 64}{2} \times 0.03 = 1.56" /> J, the same as the integral.

## Calculating Mass from Density Functions

If an object's density varies with position, its **mass** is the integral of the density function over the object's length, area, or volume.

Suppose we have a rod of length <InlineMath math="2 \text{ meters}" /> with linear density <InlineMath math="\rho(x) = (3x + 2)\,\text{kg/m}" />, where <InlineMath math="x" /> is the numerical coordinate in meters from one end of the rod. What is the total mass of the rod?

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="m = \int_{0}^{2} \rho(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{2} (3x + 2) \, dx" />
<BlockMath math="m = \left[\frac{3x^2}{2} + 2x\right]_{0}^{2}" />
<BlockMath math="m = \frac{3(4)}{2} + 2(2) = 6 + 4 = 10 \text{ kg}" />
</MathContainer>

## Determining Center of Mass

For an object with nonuniform density, integrals also determine its **center of mass**.

If we have a rod with density <InlineMath math="\rho(x)" /> over interval <InlineMath math="[a, b]" />, then the center of mass coordinate is:

<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{a}^{b} x \rho(x) \, dx}{\int_{a}^{b} \rho(x) \, dx}" />

For the rod with density <InlineMath math="\rho(x) = 3x + 2" /> above:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{0}^{2} x(3x + 2) \, dx}{10}" />
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{0}^{2} (3x^2 + 2x) \, dx}{10}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \bar{x} &amp;= \frac{[x^3 + x^2]_{0}^{2}}{10} \\ &amp;= \frac{8 + 4}{10} \\ &amp;= 1.2 \text{ meter} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

> The center of mass is the coordinate at which the total mass would produce the same first moment as the actual distribution. It is the natural balance point in a uniform gravitational field and describes the translational motion of the system, while rotational behavior still depends on how the mass is distributed.

Density increases from <InlineMath math="2 \text{ kg/m}" /> at the left end to <InlineMath math="8 \text{ kg/m}" /> at the right end. Since the **right side is denser**, the center of mass at <InlineMath math="1.2 \text{ m}" /> lies to the right of the midpoint at <InlineMath math="1 \text{ m}" />.

## Calculating Moment of Inertia

The **moment of inertia** measures how mass is distributed relative to a specified rotation axis. For rotation about a fixed axis, it determines the angular acceleration produced by a given torque through <InlineMath math="\tau=I\alpha" />. For a continuous object, it is calculated with:

<BlockMath math="I = \int r^2 \, dm" />

In this formula, <InlineMath math="r" /> is the distance from the rotation axis and <InlineMath math="dm" /> is the mass element.

For a **thin uniform rod** of mass <InlineMath math="M" /> and length <InlineMath math="L" />, choose an axis through one end **perpendicular to the rod**. Each mass element is distance <InlineMath math="x" /> from this axis, so:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} I &amp;= \int_{0}^{L} x^2 \frac{M}{L} \, dx \\ &amp;= \frac{M}{L} \int_{0}^{L} x^2 \, dx \\ &amp;= \frac{M}{L} \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{L} \\ &amp;= \frac{ML^2}{3} \end{aligned}" />

## Exercises

For work and spring energy, integrate force with respect to displacement. For mass, integrate linear density with respect to length. **Check the units**: newtons times meters give joules, while kilograms per meter times meters give kilograms.

1. A particle moves along the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis with force <InlineMath math="F(x) = 4x - x^2" /> Newtons. Calculate the work done to move the particle from <InlineMath math="x = 0" /> to <InlineMath math="x = 3 \text{ meters}" />!

2. A spring has spring constant <InlineMath math="k = 200 \text{ N/m}" />. How much energy is stored in the spring when stretched <InlineMath math="0.1 \text{ meters}" /> from equilibrium position?

3. A wire of length <InlineMath math="4 \text{ meters}" /> has linear density <InlineMath math="\rho(x) = (2 + x)\,\text{kg/m}" />, where <InlineMath math="x" /> is measured in meters. Determine the total mass of the wire and the position of its center of mass.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. The force depends on position. Add the contributions <InlineMath math="F(x)\,dx" /> from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="3" />, then **subtract the antiderivative values** at the two limits.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="W = \int_{0}^{3} (4x - x^2) \, dx" />
<BlockMath math="W = \left[2x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{3}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} W &amp;= \left(2(3)^2 - \frac{(3)^3}{3}\right) \\ &amp;\quad{}- \left(2(0)^2 - \frac{(0)^3}{3}\right) \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="W = \left(18 - 9\right) - 0 = 9 \text{ Joule}" />
</MathContainer>

The work done is <InlineMath math="9" /> Joules.

**Problem 2**. When the spring is stretched slowly, the required external force increases as <InlineMath math="F(x)=kx" />. The stored **potential energy** equals the work of this force from zero extension to <InlineMath math="0.1" /> metre.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E = \int_{0}^{0.1} kx \, dx = \int_{0}^{0.1} 200x \, dx" />
<BlockMath math="E = 200 \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{0.1}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E &amp;= 200 \times \frac{(0.1)^2 - 0^2}{2} \\ &amp;= 200 \times \frac{0.01}{2} \\ &amp;= 100 \times 0.01 \\ &amp;= 1 \text{ Joule} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The potential energy stored is <InlineMath math="1" /> Joule.

**Problem 3**. Add the mass of each short piece of wire, found by multiplying its linear density by its length:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} m &amp;= \int_{0}^{4} (2 + x) \, dx \\ &amp;= \left[2x + \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{4} \\ &amp;= 8 + 8 \\ &amp;= 16 \text{ kg} \end{aligned}" />

For the center of mass, weight each piece’s mass by its position, then divide the total mass moment by the total mass:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{0}^{4} x(2 + x) \, dx}{16}" />
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{0}^{4} (2x + x^2) \, dx}{16}" />
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{[x^2 + \frac{x^3}{3}]_{0}^{4}}{16} = \frac{16 + \frac{64}{3}}{16}" />
<BlockMath math="\bar{x} = \frac{\frac{48 + 64}{3}}{16} = \frac{\frac{112}{3}}{16} = \frac{112}{48} = \frac{7}{3} \text{ meter}" />
</MathContainer>

The total mass of the wire is <InlineMath math="16 \text{ kg}" /> and its center of mass is located at position <InlineMath math="\frac{7}{3} \text{ meters}" /> from the end.

The center of mass is measured from the end where <InlineMath math="x=0" />. It lies to the right of the midpoint because **density increases toward the other end**.
