## Adding Rectangles to Estimate an Integral

A **Riemann sum** estimates the integral of <InlineMath math="f" /> on <InlineMath math="[a,b]" /> by cutting the interval into <InlineMath math="n" /> strips of equal width, picking one sample point <InlineMath math="x_i^*" /> in each strip, and <Highlight>adding the rectangle terms</Highlight> <InlineMath math="f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x" />:

<BlockMath math="R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}" />

As the strips get thinner, the sum gets closer to the [definite integral](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/definite-integral). For <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" /> on <InlineMath math="[0,7]" />, seven rectangles that take their height from the left edge add up to <InlineMath math="21" />, while the triangle under the line has area <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2}\cdot7\cdot7=24.5" />. More strips shrink that gap.

Picture a plot of land with one curved edge. No single formula gives its area, but you can cut it into equal strips, treat each strip as a rectangle, and add the rectangles. A Riemann sum does exactly that, with the function value as the height of each strip.

## Width and Sample Points

Four quantities fix an **equal-width Riemann sum**:

| Symbol | Meaning |
| :----- | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="[a,b]" /> | Interval divided into subintervals |
| <InlineMath math="n" /> | Number of subintervals |
| <InlineMath math="\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}" /> | Width of each subinterval |
| <InlineMath math="x_i^*" /> | Sample point in subinterval <InlineMath math="i" />, which gives the rectangle height <InlineMath math="f(x_i^*)" /> |

The three common sample points, for <InlineMath math="i = 1, 2, \ldots, n" />, are the left endpoint, the right endpoint, and the midpoint of each strip:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\text{left: } x_i^* = a + (i-1)\Delta x" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{right: } x_i^* = a + i\,\Delta x" />
  <BlockMath math="\text{midpoint: } x_i^* = a + \left(i-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta x" />
</MathContainer>

**The choice of sample point changes the estimate** but not the limit. When the function rises across the interval, each left endpoint sits at the low side of its strip, so the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates. When the function falls, the roles swap.

**Step 1**: Find the width and the sample points.

<BlockMath math="\Delta x=\frac{7-0}{7}=1" />

The left endpoints are <InlineMath math="0,1,2,3,4,5,6" />. Because <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" />, each rectangle is exactly as tall as its sample point.

**Step 2**: Add the seven terms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_7&=\sum_{i=1}^7f(x_i^*)\Delta x\\&=(0+1+2+3+4+5+6)\cdot1\\&=21\end{aligned}" />

The first rectangle contributes zero, because its height at <InlineMath math="x=0" /> is zero. Check the result against the exact area. The right endpoints <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="7" /> give <InlineMath math="28" />, and the true area <InlineMath math="24.5" /> lies between <InlineMath math="21" /> and <InlineMath math="28" />, as it must for a rising function. The average <InlineMath math="\frac{21+28}{2}=24.5" /> even hits the area exactly here, because the graph is a straight line.

## Comparing Left Right and Midpoint Sums

**Problem**: Estimate the area under <InlineMath math="f(x)=\frac{x^2}{4}" /> on <InlineMath math="[0,4]" /> with <InlineMath math="n=4" />, using left, right, and midpoint sums.

The width is <InlineMath math="\Delta x=\frac{4-0}{4}=1" />, so each sum is simply the **sum of the four heights**:

| Strip | Left height | Right height | Midpoint height |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="[0,1]" /> | <InlineMath math="f(0)=0" /> | <InlineMath math="f(1)=0.25" /> | <InlineMath math="f(0.5)=0.0625" /> |
| <InlineMath math="[1,2]" /> | <InlineMath math="f(1)=0.25" /> | <InlineMath math="f(2)=1" /> | <InlineMath math="f(1.5)=0.5625" /> |
| <InlineMath math="[2,3]" /> | <InlineMath math="f(2)=1" /> | <InlineMath math="f(3)=2.25" /> | <InlineMath math="f(2.5)=1.5625" /> |
| <InlineMath math="[3,4]" /> | <InlineMath math="f(3)=2.25" /> | <InlineMath math="f(4)=4" /> | <InlineMath math="f(3.5)=3.0625" /> |

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="L_4 = 0+0.25+1+2.25 = 3.5" />
  <BlockMath math="R_4 = 0.25+1+2.25+4 = 7.5" />
  <BlockMath math="M_4 = 0.0625+0.5625+1.5625+3.0625 = 5.25" />
</MathContainer>

The exact area comes from the [fundamental theorem of calculus](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/fundamental-theorem-of-calculus): <InlineMath math="\int_0^4 \frac{x^2}{4}\,dx = \frac{4^3}{12} = \frac{16}{3} \approx 5.333" />. The left sum misses by about <InlineMath math="1.83" /> and the right sum by about <InlineMath math="2.17" />, but the midpoint sum misses by only <InlineMath math="0.083" />. In each strip, the midpoint rectangle cuts off a thin sliver above the curve and adds one under it, and the two nearly cancel.

## From the Sum to the Definite Integral

For an integrable function, the Riemann sums approach one number as the strips get thinner, whichever sample points you choose. **That limit is the definite integral**:

<BlockMath math="\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x" />

**Problem**: Find the right sum of <InlineMath math="f(x)=x" /> on <InlineMath math="[0,7]" /> for any <InlineMath math="n" />, then let <InlineMath math="n" /> grow.

With <InlineMath math="\Delta x = \frac{7}{n}" /> and right endpoints <InlineMath math="x_i = \frac{7i}{n}" />, the sum <InlineMath math="1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}" /> closes the expression:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}R_n &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{7i}{n}\cdot\frac{7}{n} = \frac{49}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} \\ &= \frac{49}{2}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n}\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="n=7" /> this gives <InlineMath math="\frac{49}{2}\cdot\frac{8}{7}=28" />, the right sum from the first example. For <InlineMath math="n=70" /> it gives about <InlineMath math="24.85" />, and for <InlineMath math="n=700" /> about <InlineMath math="24.535" />. As <InlineMath math="n\to\infty" />, the factor <InlineMath math="\frac{n+1}{n}" /> tends to <InlineMath math="1" />, so the sums tend to <InlineMath math="\frac{49}{2}=24.5" />, the area of the triangle.

Adding one more rectangle does not always move a sum closer to the integral, but the limit always arrives. The [properties of the definite integral](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/properties-of-definite-integral) all follow from this limit of sums.

## Negative Values in a Riemann Sum

## Common Mistakes

Most wrong Riemann sums go wrong at **the width or the sample points**. The arithmetic itself is rarely the problem.

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adding the heights without multiplying by <InlineMath math="\Delta x" /> | Each term is a rectangle, height times width | Multiply the sum of heights by <InlineMath math="\Delta x" />, which only looks skipped when <InlineMath math="\Delta x = 1" /> |
| Using <InlineMath math="n+1" /> sample points | Both ends of <InlineMath math="[a,b]" /> are counted, one rectangle too many | Use <InlineMath math="n" /> points: <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="b-\Delta x" /> on the left, <InlineMath math="a+\Delta x" /> to <InlineMath math="b" /> on the right |
| Mixing left and right endpoints | The formulas differ by one strip | Write <InlineMath math="x_i^*" /> from its formula before you substitute |
| Reading a negative or zero sum as an area | Terms under the x-axis count negative | Split at the zeros and add absolute values for area |
| Expecting every extra rectangle to improve the estimate | Only the limit is guaranteed | Compare with the exact integral, or let <InlineMath math="n\to\infty" /> |

## Exercises

Each problem asks for rectangles under a curve, so fix the number of subintervals and the sample point in each one before you add the terms.

1. Calculate the Riemann sum for <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2 + 1" /> on <InlineMath math="[0, 4]" /> with <InlineMath math="4" /> subintervals of equal width and the **right endpoint** as the sample point.
2. Calculate the left Riemann sum of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2x + 1" /> on <InlineMath math="[1, 3]" /> with <InlineMath math="n = 4" />, and compare it with the exact integral.
3. Write <InlineMath math="\lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{2}{n}\left(\frac{2i}{n}\right)^3" /> as a definite integral and find its value.

2. The width is <InlineMath math="\Delta x = \frac{3-1}{4} = 0.5" />, and the left endpoints are <InlineMath math="1, 1.5, 2, 2.5" /> with heights <InlineMath math="3, 4, 5, 6" />:
