## Recovering Position from Velocity

[Differentiation](/en/subjects/mathematics/derivative-function/concept-of-derivative-function) turns a position function <InlineMath math="s(t)" /> into its velocity <InlineMath math="v(t)=s'(t)" />. **Antidifferentiation** reverses this process. From <InlineMath math="v(t)" />, we find every position function whose derivative equals <InlineMath math="v(t)" />. An initial position is still needed to select one particular position function.

## Role of the Constant of Integration

We are looking for functions whose derivative is <InlineMath math="4x" />. The table compares four candidates. Notice that **different constants** still give the same derivative.

| Function <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> | Derivative <InlineMath math="f(x) = F'(x)" /> |
| :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="F(x) = 2x^2 + 1" /> | <InlineMath math="f(x) = 4x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="F(x) = 2x^2 + 5" /> | <InlineMath math="f(x) = 4x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="F(x) = 2x^2 - 10" /> | <InlineMath math="f(x) = 4x" /> |
| <InlineMath math="F(x) = 2x^2" /> | <InlineMath math="f(x) = 4x" /> |

All four <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> functions have the same derivative, <InlineMath math="f(x) = 4x" />. The reason is that <Highlight>the derivative of a constant</Highlight>, such as <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, or <InlineMath math="-10" />, is zero.

A derivative does not retain the constant from the original function. It cannot tell us whether the original constant was <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="5" />, <InlineMath math="-1000" />, or another value. The symbol <InlineMath math="C" /> represents every possible constant. An indefinite integral therefore includes all antiderivatives that differ only in their constant.

The conclusion about a constant difference applies on **one interval** where the antiderivatives are defined. For these polynomials, that interval can include all real numbers.

## Formal Notation of Integrals

The following notation shows the integrand, the variable of integration, the antiderivative, and the constant of integration:

<BlockMath math="\int f(x) \, dx = F(x) + C" />

Each part has a specific role:
- <InlineMath math="\int" /> is the **integral symbol**. Its shape comes from an elongated letter S for the Latin word *summa*, meaning sum.
- <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> is the function we are going to integrate, called the **integrand**.
- <InlineMath math="dx" /> is called the **differential** of <InlineMath math="x" />. It shows that the integration is with respect to <InlineMath math="x" />. Without it, the integral expression is incomplete.
- <InlineMath math="F(x) + C" /> is the **indefinite integral** of <InlineMath math="f(x)" />. This is our answer, where <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is the antiderivative and <InlineMath math="C" /> is the **constant of integration**.

The defining relationship is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\frac{d}{dx} [F(x)] = f(x) \\ &amp;\iff \\ &amp;\int f(x) \, dx = F(x) + C \end{aligned}" />

> If the derivative of <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is <InlineMath math="f(x)" />, then the indefinite integral of <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> includes every function of the form <InlineMath math="F(x) + C" />.

## Finding the Constant from One Function Value

Suppose <InlineMath math="F'(x)=4x" /> and <InlineMath math="F(1)=7" />. The general antiderivative is <InlineMath math="F(x)=2x^2+C" />. The **known function value** determines the required constant:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}F(1)&=2(1)^2+C=7\\2+C&=7\\C&=5\end{aligned}" />

Thus <InlineMath math="F(x)=2x^2+5" />. Check both conditions: the derivative of <InlineMath math="2x^2+5" /> is <InlineMath math="4x" />, and <InlineMath math="F(1)=2+5=7" />. One function value selects one curve from all the antiderivatives on the same interval.
