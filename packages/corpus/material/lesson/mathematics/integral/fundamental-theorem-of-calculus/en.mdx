## The Bridge Between Derivatives and Integrals

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) connects **derivatives** and **integrals**. It explains why differentiating an accumulation function recovers its rate and why antiderivatives can evaluate definite integrals without recomputing a Riemann-sum limit.

Assume <InlineMath math="f" /> is continuous on the interval being considered. Part One differentiates an integral whose upper bound varies; Part Two evaluates a fixed-bound integral with any antiderivative of the integrand. Continuity is a **sufficient hypothesis** that guarantees both statements apply here.

## Differentiating an Integral Function

Part One states that accumulating a continuous rate and then differentiating returns the original rate:

<BlockMath math="F(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt \implies F'(x) = f(x)" />

This means that the derivative of a function defined as an integral is the function inside the integral itself.

> If <InlineMath math="f(t)" /> is the inflow rate at time <InlineMath math="t" />, then <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is the volume added between the starting time and time <InlineMath math="x" />. Its instantaneous rate of change, <InlineMath math="F'(x)" />, equals the inflow rate <InlineMath math="f(x)" />.

**Example:**

Find the derivative of <InlineMath math="F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} \sqrt{2+t} \, dt" />.

**Solution:**

By FTC Part One, the derivative is obtained by evaluating the integrand at the variable upper bound: replace <InlineMath math="t" /> with <InlineMath math="x" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{d}{dx} \left( \int_{1}^{x} \sqrt{2+t} \, dt \right) = \sqrt{2+x}" />

The result is the original integrand evaluated at the upper limit.

## Evaluating Integrals with Antiderivatives

The **evaluation form of FTC** gives a practical method for calculating a definite integral. If <InlineMath math="F'(x)=f(x)" />, then:

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)" />

Here <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is an **antiderivative** of <InlineMath math="f(x)" />. Subtracting its endpoint values gives the signed accumulation of <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="b" />. For a nonnegative integrand, that value is also the geometric area.

To write this, we often use the bracket notation <InlineMath math="[F(x)]_{a}^{b}" /> which means <InlineMath math="F(b) - F(a)" />.

**Example** <InlineMath math="1" />:

Find the value of <InlineMath math="\int_{1}^{3} x^2 \, dx" />.

**Solution:**

1.  **Find the antiderivative:** The antiderivative of <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2" /> is <InlineMath math="F(x) = \frac{1}{3}x^3" />.
2.  **Evaluate at the limits:** Calculate <InlineMath math="F(3) - F(1)" />.

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="\int_{1}^{3} x^2 \, dx = \left[ \frac{1}{3}x^3 \right]_{1}^{3}" />
    <BlockMath math="= \left( \frac{1}{3}(3)^3 \right) - \left( \frac{1}{3}(1)^3 \right)" />
    <BlockMath math="= \frac{27}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{26}{3}" />
    </MathContainer>

**Example** <InlineMath math="2" />:

Find the value of <InlineMath math="\int_{1}^{5} (2x-1) \, dx" />.

**Solution:**

1.  **Find the antiderivative:** The antiderivative of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2x - 1" /> is <InlineMath math="F(x) = x^2 - x" />.
2.  **Evaluate at the limits:** Calculate <InlineMath math="F(5) - F(1)" />.

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="\int_{1}^{5} (2x-1) \, dx = \left[ x^2 - x \right]_{1}^{5}" />
    <BlockMath math="= (5^2 - 5) - (1^2 - 1)" />
    <BlockMath math="= (25 - 5) - (1 - 1)" />
    <BlockMath math="= 20 - 0 = 20" />
    </MathContainer>

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus simplifies how we calculate areas and accumulations, turning limit problems into algebraic steps with antiderivatives.
