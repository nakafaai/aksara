## How Derivatives and Integrals Are Connected

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) connects **derivatives** and **integrals**. It explains why differentiating an accumulation function recovers its rate and why antiderivatives can evaluate definite integrals without recomputing a [Riemann-sum limit](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/riemann-sum).

Assume <InlineMath math="f" /> is continuous on the interval being considered. Part One differentiates an integral whose upper bound varies. Part Two evaluates a fixed-bound integral with any antiderivative of the integrand. Continuity is a **sufficient hypothesis** that guarantees both statements apply here.

## Differentiating an Integral Function

Part One states that accumulating a continuous rate and then <Highlight>differentiating returns the original rate</Highlight>:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} F(x) &amp;= \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt \\ F'(x)&amp;= f(x) \end{aligned}" />

This means that the derivative of a function defined as an integral is the function inside the integral itself.

> If <InlineMath math="f(t)" /> is the inflow rate at time <InlineMath math="t" />, then <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is the volume added between the starting time and time <InlineMath math="x" />. Its instantaneous rate of change, <InlineMath math="F'(x)" />, equals the inflow rate <InlineMath math="f(x)" />.

**Example**:

Find the derivative of <InlineMath math="F(x) = \int_{1}^{x} \sqrt{2+t} \, dt" />.

**Solution**:

By FTC Part One, the derivative is obtained by evaluating the integrand at the variable upper bound: replace <InlineMath math="t" /> with <InlineMath math="x" />.

<BlockMath math="\frac{d}{dx} \left( \int_{1}^{x} \sqrt{2+t} \, dt \right) = \sqrt{2+x}" />

The square-root integrand is defined for <InlineMath math="t\ge-2" /> and continuous there. The derivative formula applies in the **interior of the domain**, where <InlineMath math="x>-2" />.

### When the Upper Limit Is a Function

If the upper limit is <InlineMath math="x^2" />, the rate at which this limit changes also affects the derivative. Apply the **chain rule** after evaluating the integrand at the upper limit:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}G(x)&=\int_1^{x^2}\sqrt{2+t}\,dt\\G'(x)&=\sqrt{2+x^2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}(x^2)\\&=2x\sqrt{2+x^2}\end{aligned}" />

The factor <InlineMath math="2x" /> is the derivative of the upper limit <InlineMath math="x^2" />. At <InlineMath math="x=0" />, that upper limit has zero rate of change, so the accumulation function also has zero derivative despite a positive integrand.

Evaluate <InlineMath math="G'(x) = 2x\sqrt{2 + x^2}" /> at five inputs from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="2" /> in steps of <InlineMath math="0.5" />. The first bar is <InlineMath math="0" /> because the upper limit <InlineMath math="x^2" /> is not moving at <InlineMath math="x = 0" />. After that both the factor <InlineMath math="2x" /> and the root grow, so the rate climbs quickly, to <InlineMath math="4\sqrt6 \approx 9.8" /> at <InlineMath math="x = 2" />.

## Evaluating Integrals with Antiderivatives

If <InlineMath math="F'(x)=f(x)" />, Part Two of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus evaluates the definite integral by subtracting the values of <InlineMath math="F" /> at the two endpoints:

<BlockMath math="\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)" />

Here <InlineMath math="F(x)" /> is an **antiderivative** of <InlineMath math="f(x)" />. Subtracting its endpoint values gives the signed accumulation of <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> from <InlineMath math="a" /> to <InlineMath math="b" />. For a nonnegative integrand, that value is also the geometric area.

That difference is often written with the bracket notation <InlineMath math="[F(x)]_{a}^{b}" />, which means <InlineMath math="F(b) - F(a)" />.

**Example** 1:

Find the value of <InlineMath math="\int_{1}^{3} x^2 \, dx" />.

**Solution**:

1.  **Find the antiderivative**: The antiderivative of <InlineMath math="f(x) = x^2" /> is <InlineMath math="F(x) = \frac{1}{3}x^3" />.
2.  **Evaluate at the limits**: Calculate <InlineMath math="F(3) - F(1)" />.

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="\int_{1}^{3} x^2 \, dx = \left[ \frac{1}{3}x^3 \right]_{1}^{3}" />
    <BlockMath math="= \left( \frac{1}{3}(3)^3 \right) - \left( \frac{1}{3}(1)^3 \right)" />
    <BlockMath math="= \frac{27}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{26}{3}" />
    </MathContainer>

**Example** 2:

Find the value of <InlineMath math="\int_{1}^{5} (2x-1) \, dx" />.

**Solution**:

1.  **Find the antiderivative**: The antiderivative of <InlineMath math="f(x) = 2x - 1" /> is <InlineMath math="F(x) = x^2 - x" />.
2.  **Evaluate at the limits**: Calculate <InlineMath math="F(5) - F(1)" />.

    <MathContainer>
    <BlockMath math="\int_{1}^{5} (2x-1) \, dx = \left[ x^2 - x \right]_{1}^{5}" />
    <BlockMath math="= (5^2 - 5) - (1^2 - 1)" />
    <BlockMath math="= (25 - 5) - (1 - 1)" />
    <BlockMath math="= 20 - 0 = 20" />
    </MathContainer>

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus calculates a definite integral from an antiderivative. Find the antiderivative, then subtract its value at the lower bound from its value at the upper bound.

Check the second example against a shape you can measure. Under <InlineMath math="y = 2x - 1" /> from <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="5" /> sits a trapezoid with parallel sides <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="9" /> and width <InlineMath math="4" />: area <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1 + 9}{2} \cdot 4 = 20" />. The antiderivative gives the same number, <InlineMath math="F(5) - F(1) = 20 - 0 = 20" /> with <InlineMath math="F(x) = x^2 - x" />.
