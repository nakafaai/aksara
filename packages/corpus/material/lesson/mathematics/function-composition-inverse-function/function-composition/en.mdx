## Applying One Function After Another

A store applies the same discount and fixed price reduction in two different orders:

1.  **Promo A**: <InlineMath math="20\%" /> discount, then another deduction of <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}25{,}000.00" />.
2.  **Promo B**: Price reduction of <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}25{,}000.00" />, then a <InlineMath math="20\%" /> discount.

Do both promotions produce the same final price? We can answer this by writing each operation as a function and composing the two functions in the stated order.

### Definition of Function Composition

Function composition is <Highlight>the sequential combination of two or more functions</Highlight> to produce a new function.

If we have a function <InlineMath math="g: A \to B" /> and a function <InlineMath math="f: B \to C" />, then their composition, written as <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x)" />, is a new function that maps directly from the domain <InlineMath math="A" /> to the codomain <InlineMath math="C" />.

This means we apply function <InlineMath math="g" /> first, and then we input its result into function <InlineMath math="f" />.

Mathematically, this is written as:

<BlockMath math="(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))" />

The composite is defined only for inputs <InlineMath math="x" /> in the domain of <InlineMath math="g" /> whose output <InlineMath math="g(x)" /> lies in the domain of <InlineMath math="f" />:

<BlockMath math="D_{f\circ g}=\{x\in D_g\mid g(x)\in D_f\}" />

Follow each input through the machines: first <InlineMath math="g(x) = x^2 - 3" />, then <InlineMath math="f(u) = 2u + 1" />. The input <InlineMath math="2" /> goes to <InlineMath math="1" /> and then to <InlineMath math="3" />. The inputs <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> both reach <InlineMath math="-2" /> after <InlineMath math="g" />, so they end at the same output <InlineMath math="-3" />.

### Promo Calculation

Calculate the final price for an item worth <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}200{,}000.00" />. This simplified example ignores taxes, minimum-purchase rules, and price floors so that only the order of the two operations changes.

Let <InlineMath math="x" /> be the initial price of the item.

- **<InlineMath math="20\%" /> discount function**: <InlineMath math="d(x) = x - 0.20x = 0.80x" />
- **<InlineMath math="\text{Rp}25{,}000.00" /> reduction function**: <InlineMath math="p(x) = x - 25000" />

Now compose these two functions according to the promo sequence:

**Promo A (Discount first, then price reduction)**: We are looking for <InlineMath math="(p \circ d)(x)" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} (p \circ d)(x) &amp;= p(d(x)) \\ &amp;= p(0.80x) \\ &amp;= 0.80x - 25000 \end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="x = 200000" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;(p \circ d)(200000) \\ &amp;= 0.80(200000) - 25000 \\ &amp;= 160000 - 25000 \\ &amp;= 135000 \end{aligned}" />

So, the final price with Promo A is <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}135{,}000.00" />.

**Promo B (Price reduction first, then discount)**: We are looking for <InlineMath math="(d \circ p)(x)" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(d \circ p)(x) &= d(p(x)) \\ &= d(x - 25000) \\ &= 0.80(x - 25000) \\ &= 0.80x - 20000\end{aligned}" />

For <InlineMath math="x = 200000" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;(d \circ p)(200000) \\ &amp;= 0.80(200000) - 20000 \\ &amp;= 160000 - 20000 \\ &amp;= 140000 \end{aligned}" />

So, the final price with Promo B is <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}140{,}000.00" />.

For this item, Promo A (<InlineMath math="p \circ d" />) gives a final price of <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}135{,}000.00" />, while Promo B (<InlineMath math="d \circ p" />) gives <InlineMath math="\text{Rp}140{,}000.00" />. Reversing the order changes the result, so in general <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x) \neq (g \circ f)(x)" />.

Follow the **intermediate price** after the first operation. In Promo B, the percentage discount also scales the fixed reduction, making its effective deduction smaller.

## Comparing Both Orders of Composition

Suppose we have two functions:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="f(x) = 2x + 1" />
  <BlockMath math="g(x) = x^2 - 3" />
</MathContainer>

Determine <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x)" /> and <InlineMath math="(g \circ f)(x)" />.

**Solution**:

**Finding <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x)" />**:

<BlockMath math="(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^2 - 3)" />

Substitute <InlineMath math="x" /> in <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="g(x)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} f(x^2 - 3) &amp;= 2(x^2 - 3) + 1 \\ &amp;= 2x^2 - 6 + 1 \\ &amp;= 2x^2 - 5 \end{aligned}" />

So, <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x) = 2x^2 - 5" />.

**Finding <InlineMath math="(g \circ f)(x)" />**:

<BlockMath math="(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(2x + 1)" />

Substitute <InlineMath math="x" /> in <InlineMath math="g(x)" /> with <InlineMath math="f(x)" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} g(2x + 1) &amp;= (2x + 1)^2 - 3 \\ &amp;= (4x^2 + 4x + 1) - 3 \\ &amp;= 4x^2 + 4x - 2 \end{aligned}" />

So, <InlineMath math="(g \circ f)(x) = 4x^2 + 4x - 2" />.

Because <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x) \neq (g \circ f)(x)" />, function composition is not commutative.

Both orders as graphs: <InlineMath math="(f \circ g)(x) = 2(x^2 - 3) + 1 = 2x^2 - 5" /> and <InlineMath math="(g \circ f)(x) = (2x + 1)^2 - 3 = 4x^2 + 4x - 2" />. They are two different parabolas. At <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> the first gives <InlineMath math="-3" /> and the second gives <InlineMath math="6" />, so swapping the order changes the function.

## The Domain Must Follow the Order

Composition is not the same as multiplying two functions, and its domain cannot be found by looking only at the **final formula**. Let

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="f(u)=\sqrt{u-1}" />
  <BlockMath math="g(x)=x^2" />
</MathContainer>

Then

<BlockMath math="(f\circ g)(x)=\sqrt{x^2-1}" />

The input must satisfy <InlineMath math="x\in D_g=\mathbb{R}" /> and <InlineMath math="g(x)=x^2\in D_f=[1,\infty)" />. Therefore,

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x^2\ge 1" />
  <BlockMath math="D_{f\circ g}=(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)" />
</MathContainer>

This check explains why values between <InlineMath math="-1" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> are excluded: they make the expression under the square root negative.

### A Simplified Formula Keeps Its Domain Restrictions

**Preserve domain restrictions after simplifying**. Take <InlineMath math="a(u)=1/u" /> and <InlineMath math="b(x)=1/x" />, both defined on the nonzero real numbers:

<BlockMath math="(a\circ b)(x)=\frac1{1/x}=x,\qquad D_{a\circ b}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}" />

The final formula looks like the identity function, but <InlineMath math="x=0" /> is still excluded because the first operation <InlineMath math="b(0)" /> is undefined.
