## Choose the Rule from the Information Given

Solving a triangle means finding every side and angle that is still unknown. For a triangle without a right angle, choose the rule from **the information given**:

| Information given | Rule |
| :--- | :--- |
| Two angles and one side | Sine rule |
| Two sides and an angle that is not between them | Sine rule, check the ambiguous case |
| Two sides and the angle between them | Cosine rule |
| Three sides | Cosine rule |

With sides <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> opposite the angles <InlineMath math="A" />, <InlineMath math="B" /> and <InlineMath math="C" />, the three main formulas are:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}" />
<BlockMath math="a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}bc\sin A" />
</MathContainer>

In these notes you change the angles of a triangle while watching the sine rule ratios, see when one set of information gives two triangles, and find the angle that gives the largest area.

## The Cosine Rule

Use the **cosine rule** when you are given two sides and the angle between them, or three sides. For the angle <InlineMath math="A" /> between the sides <InlineMath math="b" /> and <InlineMath math="c" />:

<BlockMath math="a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A" />

When <InlineMath math="A = 90^\circ" />, <InlineMath math="\cos A = 0" /> and the rule becomes Pythagoras' theorem. Change the angle <InlineMath math="A" /> between the sides <InlineMath math="b = 5" /> and <InlineMath math="c = 7" />.

<BlockMath math="\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab} = \frac{49 + 64 - 81}{2(7)(8)} = \frac{32}{112} \quad \Rightarrow \quad C = 73.40^\circ" />

## The Area of a Triangle

The area of a triangle is half the product of two sides and the sine of the angle between them, <InlineMath math="\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}bc\sin A" />. For <InlineMath math="b = 5" /> and <InlineMath math="c = 7" />, the area is <InlineMath math="17.5\sin A" />. Move the angle <InlineMath math="A" /> and find the largest area.

<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}" />

## Common Mistakes in Solving Triangles

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Giving only <InlineMath math="C = 53.13^\circ" /> in the ambiguous case. | The obtuse angle <InlineMath math="126.87^\circ" /> has the same sine. | Test whether <InlineMath math="180^\circ - C" /> also keeps the angle sum below <InlineMath math="180^\circ" />. |
| Using the cosine rule with an angle that is not between the two sides. | The angle in the formula must be opposite the side you want. | Match the letters: <InlineMath math="a" /> with <InlineMath math="A" />. |
| Dropping the sign of <InlineMath math="-2bc\cos A" /> when <InlineMath math="A" /> is obtuse. | <InlineMath math="\cos A" /> is negative, so the term makes <InlineMath math="a" /> longer. | The side opposite an obtuse angle must be the longest. |
| The calculator is in radian mode. | These angles are given in degrees. | Check that <InlineMath math="\sin 30^\circ = 0.5" /> before you start. |
| Using <InlineMath math="\dfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C" /> with the wrong angle. | The angle must be between the two sides you multiply. | Sketch the triangle and mark the angle between those two sides. |

The first mistake is the most common. **Every time you find an angle with the sine rule**, also check the obtuse angle <InlineMath math="180^\circ - C" />.

## Solution of Triangles Practice

Sketch the triangle and mark the information given so that **the right rule is clear before you calculate**.

1. In triangle <InlineMath math="PQR" />, <InlineMath math="\angle P = 50^\circ" />, <InlineMath math="\angle Q = 70^\circ" /> and <InlineMath math="PQ = 12\text{ cm}" />. Find the length of <InlineMath math="QR" />.

2. Two sides of a triangle are <InlineMath math="6\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="9\text{ cm}" />, with an angle of <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> between them. Find the third side and the area of the triangle.

3. In triangle <InlineMath math="ABC" />, <InlineMath math="\angle A = 40^\circ" />, <InlineMath math="AB = 10\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="BC = 7\text{ cm}" />. Show that two triangles are possible, and find the two values of <InlineMath math="\angle C" />.

4. Use Heron's formula to find the area of the triangle with sides <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />, <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="13\text{ cm}" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The side <InlineMath math="QR" /> is opposite <InlineMath math="\angle P" />, and the side <InlineMath math="PQ" /> is opposite <InlineMath math="\angle R = 180^\circ - 50^\circ - 70^\circ = 60^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="QR = \frac{12\sin 50^\circ}{\sin 60^\circ} = 10.61\text{ cm}" />

**Solution 2**: Use the cosine rule, then the area formula:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="c^2 = 6^2 + 9^2 - 2(6)(9)\cos 120^\circ = 117 + 54 = 171" />
<BlockMath math="c = \sqrt{171} = 13.08\text{ cm}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(6)(9)\sin 120^\circ = 23.38\text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the side of <InlineMath math="13.08\text{ cm}" /> is opposite the obtuse angle, so it is indeed the longest side.

**Solution 3**: Use the sine rule for <InlineMath math="\sin C" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sin C = \frac{10\sin 40^\circ}{7} = 0.9183" />
<BlockMath math="C = 66.67^\circ \quad \text{or} \quad C = 113.33^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Both are valid because <InlineMath math="40^\circ + 113.33^\circ = 153.33^\circ < 180^\circ" />. So two triangles are possible.

**Solution 4**: The semi-perimeter is <InlineMath math="s = \dfrac{5 + 12 + 13}{2} = 15" />:

<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \sqrt{15(10)(3)(2)} = \sqrt{900} = 30\text{ cm}^2" />

Check: <InlineMath math="5^2 + 12^2 = 13^2" />, so this triangle has **a right angle** and its area is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{1}{2}(5)(12) = 30\text{ cm}^2" />.
