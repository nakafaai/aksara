## Turn a Curve into a Straight Line

**Linear law** is the method of turning a non-linear relation between two variables into the straight-line form <InlineMath math="Y = mX + c" />. You choose <InlineMath math="Y" /> and <InlineMath math="X" /> from <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> so that the data points lie on one straight line. The gradient <InlineMath math="m" /> and the <InlineMath math="Y" />-intercept <InlineMath math="c" /> then give the constants in the original relation. A straight line is easy to draw, read and extend, while a curve is hard to judge by eye.

In these notes you adjust a line of best fit until its error is smallest, straighten a quadratic curve and an exponential curve, and find the acceleration due to gravity from pendulum data.

## The Line of Best Fit

Experimental data rarely lie exactly on one line. The **line of best fit** is the line that is closest to all the points. When you draw it by hand, make sure that:

1. Roughly as many points lie above the line as below it.
2. The line passes through as many points as possible, or very close to them.
3. The line passes through the **mean point** <InlineMath math="(\bar{x}, \bar{y})" />, the mean of all the <InlineMath math="x" /> values and the mean of all the <InlineMath math="y" /> values.

This table gives six readings from one experiment:

| <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="y" /> | <InlineMath math="3.2" /> | <InlineMath math="4.8" /> | <InlineMath math="7.1" /> | <InlineMath math="9.0" /> | <InlineMath math="10.8" /> | <InlineMath math="13.1" /> |

## Straighten a Curved Relation

When the graph of <InlineMath math="y" /> against <InlineMath math="x" /> is a curve, rearrange the equation into the **straight-line form** <InlineMath math="Y = mX + c" />. The variables <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" /> may contain only <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />, while <InlineMath math="m" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> may contain only constants.

| Original relation | Linear form | <InlineMath math="Y" /> | <InlineMath math="X" /> | <InlineMath math="m" /> | <InlineMath math="c" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="y = ax^2 + bx" /> | <InlineMath math="\dfrac{y}{x} = ax + b" /> | <InlineMath math="\dfrac{y}{x}" /> | <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="a" /> | <InlineMath math="b" /> |
| <InlineMath math="y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b" /> | <InlineMath math="y = a\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) + b" /> | <InlineMath math="y" /> | <InlineMath math="\dfrac{1}{x}" /> | <InlineMath math="a" /> | <InlineMath math="b" /> |
| <InlineMath math="y = ab^x" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y = (\log_{10} b)x + \log_{10} a" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y" /> | <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} b" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} a" /> |
| <InlineMath math="y = ax^n" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y = n\log_{10} x + \log_{10} a" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} x" /> | <InlineMath math="n" /> | <InlineMath math="\log_{10} a" /> |

| <InlineMath math="x" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> |

## Find Gravity from a Pendulum

The period of a simple pendulum is <InlineMath math="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}" />. Square both sides to get <InlineMath math="T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\,l" />, which has the form <InlineMath math="Y = mX" /> with <InlineMath math="Y = T^2" />, <InlineMath math="X = l" /> and gradient <InlineMath math="\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}" />. A student recorded:

| <InlineMath math="l\ (\text{m})" /> | <InlineMath math="0.2" /> | <InlineMath math="0.4" /> | <InlineMath math="0.6" /> | <InlineMath math="0.8" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="T^2\ (\text{s}^2)" /> | <InlineMath math="0.80" /> | <InlineMath math="1.61" /> | <InlineMath math="2.41" /> | <InlineMath math="3.22" /> | <InlineMath math="4.02" /> |

Change the value of <InlineMath math="g" /> until the line passes through the data points. The gradient reading shows <InlineMath math="\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}" /> for that value.

## Common Mistakes with Linear Law

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Working out the gradient from the original <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> points. | The gradient must be worked out on the transformed <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" /> axes. | Write the coordinates as <InlineMath math="(X, Y)" /> first. |
| Taking the intercept <InlineMath math="1.699" /> as the value of <InlineMath math="a" />. | The intercept is <InlineMath math="\log_{10} a" />, not <InlineMath math="a" />. | Find the antilog: <InlineMath math="a = 10^{1.699}" />. |
| Drawing the line through only the first and last points. | The line of best fit balances all the points. | Make sure the line passes through the mean point. |
| Plotting <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y" /> against <InlineMath math="\log_{10} x" /> for <InlineMath math="y = ab^x" />. | The variable <InlineMath math="x" /> is a power, so the horizontal axis stays <InlineMath math="x" />. | Take logs of both sides and see which terms appear. |
| Reading the intercept off an axis that does not start at zero. | The <InlineMath math="Y" />-intercept is the value of <InlineMath math="Y" /> when <InlineMath math="X = 0" />. | Work out <InlineMath math="c" /> with <InlineMath math="c = Y - mX" />. |

The second mistake is the most common in logarithm questions. **Every constant that appears inside a log** needs its antilog before it goes into the answer.

## Linear Law Practice

Write the form <InlineMath math="Y = mX + c" /> first, then **match <InlineMath math="m" /> and <InlineMath math="c" /> to the original constants**.

1. The graph of <InlineMath math="\dfrac{y}{x}" /> against <InlineMath math="x" /> is a straight line through <InlineMath math="(1, 5)" /> and <InlineMath math="(3, 9)" />. Express <InlineMath math="y" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="x" />.

2. The variables <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> are related by <InlineMath math="y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b" />. The graph of <InlineMath math="y" /> against <InlineMath math="\dfrac{1}{x}" /> has gradient <InlineMath math="4" /> and passes through <InlineMath math="(0.5, 5)" />. Find <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" /> and the value of <InlineMath math="y" /> when <InlineMath math="x = 2" />.

3. For <InlineMath math="y = ab^x" />, the graph of <InlineMath math="\log_{10} y" /> against <InlineMath math="x" /> has gradient <InlineMath math="0.301" /> and intercept <InlineMath math="0.477" />. Find <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" />.

4. In another pendulum experiment, the graph of <InlineMath math="T^2" /> against <InlineMath math="l" /> has gradient <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ s}^2\text{ m}^{-1}" />. Calculate <InlineMath math="g" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Here <InlineMath math="Y = \dfrac{y}{x}" /> and <InlineMath math="X = x" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="m = \frac{9 - 5}{3 - 1} = 2, \quad c = 5 - 2(1) = 3" />
<BlockMath math="\frac{y}{x} = 2x + 3 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = 2x^2 + 3x" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the second point: at <InlineMath math="x = 3" />, <InlineMath math="\dfrac{y}{x} = 2(3) + 3 = 9" />.

**Solution 2**: The gradient is <InlineMath math="a" />, so <InlineMath math="a = 4" />. Find the intercept <InlineMath math="b" /> from the given point:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="5 = 4(0.5) + b \quad \Rightarrow \quad b = 3" />
<BlockMath math="y = \frac{4}{x} + 3, \quad x = 2: \quad y = 2 + 3 = 5" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution 3**: The gradient is <InlineMath math="\log_{10} b" /> and the intercept is <InlineMath math="\log_{10} a" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="b = 10^{0.301} = 2.00, \quad a = 10^{0.477} = 3.00" />
<BlockMath math="y = 3(2)^x" />
</MathContainer>

Check: at <InlineMath math="x = 0" />, <InlineMath math="\log_{10} 3 = 0.477" />, the same as the intercept.

**Solution 4**: The gradient is <InlineMath math="\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}" />, so:

<BlockMath math="g = \frac{4\pi^2}{4.0} = \frac{39.48}{4.0} = 9.87\text{ m s}^{-2}" />

This is close to <InlineMath math="9.81\text{ m s}^{-2}" />. A small difference like this is **normal in a school experiment**.
