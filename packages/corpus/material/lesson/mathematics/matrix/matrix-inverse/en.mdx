## Multiplying a Matrix by Its Inverse

In the set of real numbers, every non-zero number <InlineMath math="a" /> has a **reciprocal**, which is the number <InlineMath math="a^{-1}" />, satisfying the property <InlineMath math="a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = 1" />. A similar concept applies to matrices.

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is a square matrix (e.g., of order <InlineMath math="n \times n" />) and <InlineMath math="I" /> is the identity matrix of the same order, then the inverse of matrix <InlineMath math="A" />, denoted as <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" />, is a matrix that satisfies the property:

<BlockMath math="A \cdot A^{-1} = A^{-1} \cdot A = I" />

The identity matrix <InlineMath math="I" /> is a square matrix where all main diagonal elements are <InlineMath math="1" /> and all other elements are <InlineMath math="0" />. For example, for a <InlineMath math="2 \times 2" /> order:

<BlockMath math="I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

## Nonsingular and Singular Matrices

Not all square matrices have an inverse. A matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> has an inverse if and only if the determinant of the matrix is not equal to zero (<InlineMath math="\det(A) \neq 0" /> or <InlineMath math="|A| \neq 0" />).

- Matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is called a **nonsingular matrix** if <InlineMath math="\det(A) \neq 0" />. A nonsingular matrix always has an inverse.
- Matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is called a **singular matrix** if <InlineMath math="\det(A) = 0" />. A singular matrix does not have an inverse.

## Inverse of a Two by Two Matrix

For a <InlineMath math="2 \times 2" /> matrix <InlineMath math="A" />, let:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}" />

The inverse of matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> can be found using the following formula, provided that <InlineMath math="\det(A) \neq 0" />:

<BlockMath math="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \text{ Adj}(A)" />

Understand each component of this formula:

1.  **Determinant of Matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> (<InlineMath math="\det(A)" /> or <InlineMath math="|A|" />)**:

    Calculated as:

    <BlockMath math="|A| = ad - bc" />

2.  **Adjugate of Matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> (<InlineMath math="\text{Adj}(A)" />)**:

    Obtained by swapping the main diagonal elements and changing the sign of the other diagonal elements:

    <BlockMath math="\text{Adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}" />

So, the complete formula for the inverse of a <InlineMath math="2 \times 2" /> matrix is:

<BlockMath math="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}" />

### Example of a Two by Two Matrix Inverse

Find the inverse of the following matrix <InlineMath math="P" />:

<BlockMath math="P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -7 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" />

**Solution**:

**Step 1**: Identify the elements of matrix <InlineMath math="P" />.

<InlineMath math="a=3, b=-7, c=-1, d=2" />

**Step 2**: Calculate the determinant of matrix <InlineMath math="P" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det(P) &amp;= (3)(2) - (-7)(-1) \\ &amp;= 6 - 7 \\ &amp;= -1 \end{aligned}" />

Since <InlineMath math="\det(P) \neq 0" />, matrix <InlineMath math="P" /> has an inverse.

**Step 3**: Determine the adjugate of matrix <InlineMath math="P" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{Adj}(P) &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 2 &amp; -(-7) \\ -(-1) &amp; 3 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 2 &amp; 7 \\ 1 &amp; 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}" />

**Step 4**: Calculate the inverse of matrix <InlineMath math="P" />.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="P^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(P)} \text{ Adj}(P)" />
  <BlockMath math="P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="P^{-1} = -1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -7 \\ -1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The matrix above is <InlineMath math="P^{-1}" />.

Verify the result by multiplying the matrices:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} PP^{-1} &amp;=\begin{bmatrix}3&amp;-7\\-1&amp;2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-2&amp;-7\\-1&amp;-3\end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;=\begin{bmatrix}1&amp;0\\0&amp;1\end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;=I_2 \end{aligned}" />

## Inverse of a Three by Three Matrix

The basic concept for finding the inverse of a <InlineMath math="3 \times 3" /> matrix is the same as for a <InlineMath math="2 \times 2" /> matrix, i.e., using the formula:

<BlockMath math="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \text{ Adj}(A)" />

However, the calculation of the determinant (<InlineMath math="\det(A)" />) and adjugate (<InlineMath math="\text{Adj}(A)" />) for a <InlineMath math="3 \times 3" /> matrix is more complex.

- **The determinant of a <InlineMath math="3 \times 3" /> matrix** can be calculated using Sarrus's rule or the [cofactor expansion method](/en/subjects/mathematics/matrix/cofactor-expansion-method).
- **The adjugate of a <InlineMath math="3 \times 3" /> matrix** is obtained from the transpose of its cofactor matrix.

For a <InlineMath math="3 \times 3" /> matrix, the process begins by calculating the determinant and adjugate. Both results are then substituted into the inverse formula.

### Calculating Every Cofactor

Take the following triangular matrix. Its determinant is the **product of the main-diagonal entries**, <InlineMath math="1(1)(1)=1" />, so an inverse exists. For each cofactor, delete the corresponding row and column, calculate the remaining determinant, and apply the sign <InlineMath math="(-1)^{i+j}" />.

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{bmatrix}1&amp;2&amp;0\\0&amp;1&amp;3\\0&amp;0&amp;1\end{bmatrix}" />

| Cofactor | Calculation |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="C_{11}" /> | <InlineMath math="+(1(1)-3(0))=1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{12}" /> | <InlineMath math="-(0(1)-3(0))=0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{13}" /> | <InlineMath math="+(0(0)-1(0))=0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{21}" /> | <InlineMath math="-(2(1)-0(0))=-2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{22}" /> | <InlineMath math="+(1(1)-0(0))=1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{23}" /> | <InlineMath math="-(1(0)-2(0))=0" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{31}" /> | <InlineMath math="+(2(3)-0(1))=6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{32}" /> | <InlineMath math="-(1(3)-0(0))=-3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="C_{33}" /> | <InlineMath math="+(1(1)-2(0))=1" /> |

Arrange the cofactors in matrix <InlineMath math="C" />. **Transpose the cofactor matrix** to obtain the adjugate. Division by the determinant, <InlineMath math="1" />, leaves the entries unchanged.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="C=\begin{bmatrix}1&amp;0&amp;0\\-2&amp;1&amp;0\\6&amp;-3&amp;1\end{bmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="A^{-1}=\frac{C^T}{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&amp;-2&amp;6\\0&amp;1&amp;-3\\0&amp;0&amp;1\end{bmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="AA^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&amp;-2+2&amp;6-6\\0&amp;1&amp;-3+3\\0&amp;0&amp;1\end{bmatrix}=I_3" />
</MathContainer>

## Solving a Linear System with an Inverse

A matrix inverse can solve a system of linear equations when its coefficient matrix is invertible. Write the system in matrix form:

<BlockMath math="AX = B" />

In this equation, <InlineMath math="A" /> is the coefficient matrix, <InlineMath math="X" /> is the variable matrix, and <InlineMath math="B" /> is the constant matrix. If <InlineMath math="A" /> has an inverse <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" />, multiply both sides from the left by <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" />. Keep this order because matrix multiplication is not generally commutative:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="A^{-1}(AX)=A^{-1}B" />
  <BlockMath math="(A^{-1}A)X=A^{-1}B" />
  <BlockMath math="IX=A^{-1}B" />
  <BlockMath math="X=A^{-1}B" />
</MathContainer>

Multiplying both sides from the left by <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> gives <InlineMath math="X=A^{-1}B" />. <Highlight>The multiplication order must stay the same</Highlight> because matrix multiplication is not commutative.

Here is <InlineMath math="X=A^{-1}B" /> as a picture. Take the matrix <InlineMath math="P" /> from the example above as <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}" />. The system <InlineMath math="PX = B" /> is the pair of lines <InlineMath math="3x - 7y = -4" /> and <InlineMath math="-x + 2y = 1" />. Multiplying by <InlineMath math="P^{-1}" /> gives <InlineMath math="X = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -7 \\ -1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}" />, exactly where the two lines cross. If the determinant were <InlineMath math="0" />, the lines would be parallel or the same line, and no inverse could pick out one crossing point.

## Exercises

Compare the **inverse of a sum** with the **sum of the two inverses**. Calculate each matrix so that the comparison uses corresponding entries.

Given the following matrices:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="Y = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

1. Determine matrices <InlineMath math="X^{-1}" /> and <InlineMath math="Y^{-1}" />.
2. Determine matrix <InlineMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1}" />.
3. Determine matrix <InlineMath math="(X+Y)^{-1}" />.
4. Is matrix <InlineMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1}" /> equal to matrix <InlineMath math="(X+Y)^{-1}" />? Explain your answer.
### Worked Solutions

1.  **Determining <InlineMath math="X^{-1}" />**:

    <MathContainer>
      <BlockMath math="X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det(X) &amp;= (1)(4) - (-3)(-1) \\ &amp;= 4 - 3 \\ &amp;= 1 \end{aligned}" />
      <BlockMath math="\text{Adj}(X) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="X^{-1} = \frac{1}{1} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />
    </MathContainer>

    **Determining <InlineMath math="Y^{-1}" />**:

    <MathContainer>
      <BlockMath math="Y = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det(Y) &amp;= (-4)(-2) - (2)(3) \\ &amp;= 8 - 6 \\ &amp;= 2 \end{aligned}" />
      <BlockMath math="\text{Adj}(Y) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ -3 & -4 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} Y^{-1} &amp;= \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} -2 &amp; -2 \\ -3 &amp; -4 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} -1 &amp; -1 \\ -3/2 &amp; -2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
    </MathContainer>

2.  **Determining <InlineMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1}" />**:

    <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;X^{-1} + Y^{-1} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 4 &amp; 3 \\ 1 &amp; 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 &amp; -1 \\ -3/2 &amp; -2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 4-1 &amp; 3-1 \\ 1-3/2 &amp; 1-2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 3 &amp; 2 \\ -1/2 &amp; -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}" />

3.  **Determining <InlineMath math="(X+Y)^{-1}" />**:

    First, calculate <InlineMath math="X+Y" />:

    <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;X+Y \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 1 &amp; -3 \\ -1 &amp; 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -4 &amp; 2 \\ 3 &amp; -2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} 1-4 &amp; -3+2 \\ -1+3 &amp; 4-2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} -3 &amp; -1 \\ 2 &amp; 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}" />

    Let <InlineMath math="Z = X+Y" />. Calculate <InlineMath math="Z^{-1}" />:

    <MathContainer>
      <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det(Z) &amp;= (-3)(2) - (-1)(2) \\ &amp;= -6 - (-2) \\ &amp;= -6 + 2 \\ &amp;= -4 \end{aligned}" />
      <BlockMath math="\text{Adj}(Z) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} (X+Y)^{-1} &amp;= Z^{-1} \\ &amp;= \frac{1}{-4} \begin{bmatrix} 2 &amp; 1 \\ -2 &amp; -3 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} -2/4 &amp; -1/4 \\ 2/4 &amp; 3/4 \end{bmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{bmatrix} -1/2 &amp; -1/4 \\ 1/2 &amp; 3/4 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
    </MathContainer>

4.  **Comparison of <InlineMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1}" /> and <InlineMath math="(X+Y)^{-1}" />**:

    From the calculations:

    <MathContainer>
      <BlockMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ -1/2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}" />
      <BlockMath math="(X+Y)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/2 & -1/4 \\ 1/2 & 3/4 \end{bmatrix}" />
    </MathContainer>

    The two matrices differ, so <InlineMath math="X^{-1} + Y^{-1} \neq (X+Y)^{-1}" />. Inversion is not distributive over addition. The same rule already holds for ordinary reciprocals: in general, <InlineMath math="1/x+1/y\neq1/(x+y)" />.
