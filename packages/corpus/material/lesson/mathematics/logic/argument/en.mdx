## From Premises to a Conclusion

An argument is a chain of statements: some statements you accept, called **premises**, lead to one statement you claim, called the **conclusion**. There are two directions to reason in. Deductive reasoning goes from general premises to a specific conclusion. Inductive reasoning goes from specific cases to a general conclusion.

| Direction | Premises | Conclusion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Deductive | The interior angles of every quadrilateral add up to <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />. <InlineMath math="PQRS" /> is a quadrilateral. | The interior angles of <InlineMath math="PQRS" /> add up to <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />. |
| Inductive | <InlineMath math="1 + 3 = 2^2" />, <InlineMath math="1 + 3 + 5 = 3^2" />, <InlineMath math="1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 4^2" /> | The sum of the first <InlineMath math="n" /> odd numbers is <InlineMath math="n^2" />. |

The difference is certainty. If the premises of a deductive argument are true and its form is valid, the conclusion is **certainly true**. An inductive conclusion is only **probably true**, so you test it on new cases. For <InlineMath math="n = 5" /> the pattern still holds, because <InlineMath math="1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25 = 5^2" />. Even so, one more case still isn't proof for every <InlineMath math="n" />.

## Three Valid Argument Forms

Some argument forms always work: if the premises are true, the conclusion can't be false. An argument with a form like that is called **valid**. The three forms in this table are the ones you'll use most.

| Form | Premise 1 | Premise 2 | Conclusion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| I | All <InlineMath math="A" /> are <InlineMath math="B" />. | <InlineMath math="C" /> is <InlineMath math="A" />. | <InlineMath math="C" /> is <InlineMath math="B" />. |
| II, modus ponens | If <InlineMath math="p" />, then <InlineMath math="q" />. | <InlineMath math="p" /> is true. | <InlineMath math="q" /> is true. |
| III, modus tollens | If <InlineMath math="p" />, then <InlineMath math="q" />. | <InlineMath math="\sim q" /> is true. | <InlineMath math="\sim p" /> is true. |

Form I works because if every member of <InlineMath math="A" /> sits inside <InlineMath math="B" />, then any member of <InlineMath math="A" />, including <InlineMath math="C" />, sits inside <InlineMath math="B" /> too. Form III works because an implication and [its contrapositive always share a truth value](/en/subjects/mathematics/logical-reasoning/implication-and-contrapositive). The chain below already holds premise 1 of a Form III argument. Here <InlineMath math="PQRS" /> is a kite with sides <InlineMath math="3\text{ cm}" />, <InlineMath math="3\text{ cm}" />, <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" /> and <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />.

With <InlineMath math="2" /> is a prime number as premise 2, the chain is **Form I** and valid: if every prime really were odd, <InlineMath math="2" /> would have to be odd. The trouble is that premise 1 is false, because <InlineMath math="2" /> is a prime number that's even. The other order, from <InlineMath math="2" /> is odd to <InlineMath math="2" /> is prime, is the fallacy called the **undistributed middle**. All primes being odd doesn't make every odd number prime, just as <InlineMath math="9" /> is odd but not prime. Compare a sound argument: all multiples of <InlineMath math="4" /> are even, <InlineMath math="36" /> is a multiple of <InlineMath math="4" />, so <InlineMath math="36" /> is even. Check: <InlineMath math="36 = 4 \times 9" />.

### Writing a Valid Argument

To write a valid argument about a situation, **choose the form first**, then fill in the sentences. The first example uses Form I. Premise 1: all multiples of <InlineMath math="9" /> have a digit sum divisible by <InlineMath math="9" />. Premise 2: <InlineMath math="7{,}263" /> is a multiple of <InlineMath math="9" />. Conclusion: the digit sum of <InlineMath math="7{,}263" /> is divisible by <InlineMath math="9" />. Check both sides:

<BlockMath math="7{,}263 = 9 \times 807, \qquad 7 + 2 + 6 + 3 = 18 = 9 \times 2" />

The second example asks for a missing premise. Premise 1: if the side of square <InlineMath math="K" /> is <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />, then its area is <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}^2" />. Conclusion: the side of square <InlineMath math="K" /> is not <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />. The conclusion has the shape <InlineMath math="\sim p" />, so the argument is Form III, and premise 2 must be <InlineMath math="\sim q" />: the area of square <InlineMath math="K" /> is not <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}^2" />. Check: a side of <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" /> gives an area of <InlineMath math="5 \times 5 = 25\text{ cm}^2" />, which contradicts premise 2. So the side really isn't <InlineMath math="5\text{ cm}" />.

## Strong and Weak Induction

An inductive argument guesses a general rule from a few cases. If the premises give good reason to believe the rule, the argument is **strong**. If the reason is thin, the argument is **weak**. Here's a weak one: <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="5" /> and <InlineMath math="7" /> are prime, so every odd number greater than <InlineMath math="1" /> is prime. Three cases are too few, and <InlineMath math="9 = 3 \times 3" /> breaks the conclusion straight away.

### When Strong Is Cogent

A strong inductive argument whose **premises are all true** is called cogent. The diagonal argument is cogent, because every count in its premises has been checked. Compare this one. Premises: <InlineMath math="2^2 - 1 = 3" />, <InlineMath math="2^3 - 1 = 7" />, <InlineMath math="2^5 - 1 = 31" />, <InlineMath math="2^7 - 1 = 127" /> and <InlineMath math="2^{11} - 1 = 2{,}047" /> are all prime. Conclusion: <InlineMath math="2^p - 1" /> is prime for every prime number <InlineMath math="p" />.

The last premise is false.

<BlockMath math="2^{11} - 1 = 2{,}047 = 23 \times 89" />

Because one premise is false, **the argument isn't cogent**, however neat the pattern looks. The case <InlineMath math="p = 11" /> is even a counterexample to the conclusion. Check the product: <InlineMath math="23 \times 89 = 23 \times 90 - 23 = 2{,}070 - 23 = 2{,}047" />.

## Which Cards to Turn Over

This puzzle uses every tool at once. Each of four cards has a number on one side and a colour on the other. The faces lying up show <InlineMath math="8" />, <InlineMath math="3" />, red and blue. The rule is: if a card has an even number on one side, then its other side is red. Which cards must you turn over to test the rule?

The rule is an implication, so only a counterexample can break it: a card with **an even number and a colour that isn't red**. Ask that question about each card.

| Card | What could be on the back | Can it break the rule? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="8" /> | red or blue | Yes, if the back is blue |
| <InlineMath math="3" /> | red or blue | No, the antecedent is false |
| red | even or odd | No, the consequent is already true |
| blue | even or odd | Yes, if the back is even |

So you must turn over **the <InlineMath math="8" /> card and the blue card**. The <InlineMath math="8" /> card is tested with Form II: if the rule holds, its back has to be red. The blue card is tested with Form III: if the rule holds, its back can't be even. Many people pick the red card, yet nothing on its back can break the rule. Drawing a conclusion from the red card is affirming the consequent.
