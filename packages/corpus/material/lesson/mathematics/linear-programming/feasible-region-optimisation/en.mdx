## The Best Value Sits at a Vertex

**Linear programming** finds the maximum or minimum of an **objective function** <InlineMath math="k = ax + by" /> when <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> are bound by several linear inequalities called **constraints**. The constraints form the **feasible region**, the set of all points that satisfy every constraint. The best value of the objective function always happens at a **vertex** of that region, so you only need to test the vertices.

The steps are:

1. Write each constraint as an inequality in <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />.
2. Draw the boundary lines and shade the feasible region.
3. Find the coordinates of every vertex.
4. Evaluate the objective function at each vertex, or move the objective line until the last point it touches.

In these notes you test a point against an inequality, build the feasible region of a cake shop, move a profit line, and see how prices change the best choice.

## One Inequality Shades Half the Plane

The inequality <InlineMath math="x + 3y \le 120" /> is shown by the boundary line <InlineMath math="x + 3y = 120" /> and every point on one side of it. A dashed line is used for <InlineMath math="<" /> or <InlineMath math=">" />, and a solid line for <InlineMath math="\le" /> or <InlineMath math="\ge" />. To choose the right side, **test one point that is not on the line**, usually the origin. ## Build the Feasible Region from a Situation

A shop makes <InlineMath math="x" /> chocolate cakes and <InlineMath math="y" /> cheesecakes a day. Its constraints are:

- The oven can bake at most <InlineMath math="60" /> cakes a day.
- Decorating a chocolate cake takes <InlineMath math="1" /> minute and a cheesecake <InlineMath math="3" /> minutes, with at most <InlineMath math="120" /> minutes for decorating.
- The number of cakes cannot be negative.

Write each constraint as an inequality:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x + y \le 60" />
<BlockMath math="x + 3y \le 120" />
<BlockMath math="x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0" />
</MathContainer>

The feasible region is the area that **satisfies all four inequalities at once**. Its vertices are <InlineMath math="(0, 0)" />, <InlineMath math="(60, 0)" />, <InlineMath math="(0, 40)" /> and the point where the two boundary lines cross.

## A Minimum Cost Problem

Linear programming also finds a minimum. A farmer mixes two kinds of chicken feed. One kilogram of feed <InlineMath math="P" /> gives <InlineMath math="2" /> units of protein and <InlineMath math="1" /> unit of vitamins, while one kilogram of feed <InlineMath math="Q" /> gives <InlineMath math="1" /> unit of protein and <InlineMath math="2" /> units of vitamins. The mix needs at least <InlineMath math="8" /> units of protein and <InlineMath math="7" /> units of vitamins. Feed <InlineMath math="P" /> costs RM3 a kilogram and <InlineMath math="Q" /> costs RM4 a kilogram.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2x + y \ge 8, \quad x + 2y \ge 7, \quad x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0" />

## Common Mistakes in Linear Programming

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Writing <InlineMath math="\ge" /> for "at most". | "At most" means not more than, which is <InlineMath math="\le" />. | Test a large value: it must break the constraint. |
| Shading the wrong side. | The shaded side must satisfy the inequality. | Substitute the origin or another point into the inequality. |
| Testing a point in the middle of the region as the answer. | The best value of a linear function is at a vertex. | List every vertex and evaluate each one. |
| Looking for a maximum in an unbounded region. | The value can grow without limit. | Check whether the region is closed in every direction. |
| Giving a fraction for a number of items. | A number of cakes must be a whole number. | Test the nearest whole-number points that are still in the region. |

The first mistake is the most common. **Translate every phrase carefully**: "at most" is <InlineMath math="\le" />, and "at least" is <InlineMath math="\ge" />.

## Linear Programming Practice

Sketch the feasible region for each question so that **every vertex is visible** before you evaluate the objective function.

1. A factory makes <InlineMath math="x" /> chairs and <InlineMath math="y" /> tables. A chair needs <InlineMath math="2" /> hours and a table <InlineMath math="4" /> hours, with at most <InlineMath math="80" /> working hours. Write the inequality for this constraint in its simplest form.

2. A feasible region is defined by <InlineMath math="x + y \le 10" />, <InlineMath math="2x + y \le 14" />, <InlineMath math="x \ge 0" /> and <InlineMath math="y \ge 0" />. Find the maximum value of <InlineMath math="k = 3x + 2y" />.

3. Find the minimum value of <InlineMath math="C = 2x + 5y" /> subject to <InlineMath math="x + y \ge 6" />, <InlineMath math="x + 3y \ge 12" />, <InlineMath math="x \ge 0" /> and <InlineMath math="y \ge 0" />.

4. In the cake shop problem, the profit on a chocolate cake rises to RM10. Find the numbers of cakes that give the maximum profit and the value of that profit.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The total working time is <InlineMath math="2x + 4y" /> hours, and "at most" gives <InlineMath math="\le" />:

<BlockMath math="2x + 4y \le 80 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x + 2y \le 40" />

**Solution 2**: The crossing vertex comes from <InlineMath math="x + y = 10" /> and <InlineMath math="2x + y = 14" />, which give <InlineMath math="x = 4" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 6" />. Evaluate at every vertex:

<BlockMath math="k(0, 0) = 0, \quad k(7, 0) = 21, \quad k(4, 6) = 24, \quad k(0, 10) = 20" />

The maximum value is <InlineMath math="24" /> at <InlineMath math="(4, 6)" />.

**Solution 3**: The crossing vertex from <InlineMath math="x + y = 6" /> and <InlineMath math="x + 3y = 12" /> is <InlineMath math="(3, 3)" />. The other vertices are <InlineMath math="(12, 0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(0, 6)" />:

<BlockMath math="C(12, 0) = 24, \quad C(3, 3) = 21, \quad C(0, 6) = 30" />

The minimum value is <InlineMath math="21" /> at <InlineMath math="(3, 3)" />.

**Solution 4**: The objective function is now <InlineMath math="k = 10x + 8y" />:

<BlockMath math="k(60, 0) = 600, \quad k(30, 30) = 540, \quad k(0, 40) = 320" />

The maximum profit is **RM600** when the shop makes <InlineMath math="60" /> chocolate cakes only. This matches the last diagram, because <InlineMath math="a = 10" /> is greater than <InlineMath math="8" />.
