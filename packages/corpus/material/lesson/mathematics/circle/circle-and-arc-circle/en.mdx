## A Circle and Its Parts

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are **the same distance from one point**. That point is the center, and the shared distance is the radius. An arc is the part of the circle between two points on it, and on the same circle its length is proportional to its central angle: <InlineMath math="l = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />.

The diagram of a circle's parts labels the four parts you need in almost every problem: the center <InlineMath math="O" />, the radius <InlineMath math="r" />, a diameter, and a chord.

The center <InlineMath math="O" /> is the reference point for every radius. A radius is a segment from the center to a point on the circle, and in the diagram it has length <InlineMath math="r = 3" />. A [chord](/en/subjects/mathematics/circle/circle-and-chord) is a segment joining two points on the circle. A diameter is the longest chord, the one that passes through the center, so <InlineMath math="d = 2r = 6" />.

The same equal distance definition also gives the equation of a circle. For center <InlineMath math="(a, b)" /> and radius <InlineMath math="r > 0" />, the distance formula gives:

<BlockMath math="(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2" />

On the coordinate plane, this is the standard form of [the equation of a circle](/en/subjects/mathematics/analytic-geometry/equation-of-circle), and you can read <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, and <InlineMath math="r" /> straight from it.

## Minor Arcs and Major Arcs

Two points on a circle split it into **two arcs at once**. The shorter one is the minor arc and the longer one is the major arc. If the two points are the ends of a diameter, the two arcs are equal and each one is a semicircle.

In the minor and major arc diagram, the minor arc covers <InlineMath math="90^\circ" /> and the major arc covers the rest, <InlineMath math="270^\circ" />. The measures of the two arcs always add up to <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />.

| Kind of arc | Arc measure | Share of the circle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor arc | Less than <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | Less than half |
| Semicircle | Exactly <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | Exactly half |
| Major arc | More than <InlineMath math="180^\circ" /> | More than half |

A minor arc is named with two letters, such as <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" />. A major arc is named with three letters, such as <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{ACB}" />, because point <InlineMath math="C" />, between <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" />, shows which arc is meant. Without a third letter, <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" /> always means the minor arc.

## Arc Length and Sector Area

A sector is the region bounded by two radii and the arc between them. An arc and its sector take **the same share of a full turn**, which is the central angle divided by <InlineMath math="360^\circ" />. Multiply that share by the circumference for the arc length, and by the area of the circle for the sector area.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="l = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi r" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} \times \pi r^2" />
</MathContainer>

In both formulas, <InlineMath math="l" /> is the arc length, <InlineMath math="A" /> is the sector area, <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> is the central angle in degrees, and <InlineMath math="r" /> is the radius.

## Worked Examples on Arcs and Sectors

Every example starts by **finding the share of a full turn** the arc takes. Once you know the share, the arc length and sector area follow.

**Example 1**. A circle has radius <InlineMath math="14\text{ cm}" />. An arc has a central angle of <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />. Find the arc length and the sector area.

The share of a full turn is <InlineMath math="\frac{90}{360} = \frac{1}{4}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="l = \frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi \times 14 = 7\pi \approx 21.99\text{ cm}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{4} \times \pi \times 14^2 = 49\pi \approx 153.94\text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the relationship <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} l r" />. It gives <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{2} \times 7\pi \times 14 = 49\pi" />, which matches the sector area.

**Example 2**. An arc of length <InlineMath math="7\pi\text{ cm}" /> lies on a circle of radius <InlineMath math="21\text{ cm}" />. Find its central angle.

The share of a full turn is the arc length divided by the circumference:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} = \frac{7\pi}{2\pi \times 21} = \frac{7\pi}{42\pi} = \frac{1}{6}" />
  <BlockMath math="\alpha = \frac{1}{6} \times 360^\circ = 60^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Check it in the other direction. A <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> arc on a radius of <InlineMath math="21" /> has length <InlineMath math="\frac{60}{360} \times 42\pi = 7\pi" />.

## Common Mistakes

Before you calculate, separate **arc measure from arc length**.

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Giving an arc length in degrees | Degrees measure the arc, while length uses a unit of length | Check the unit of the answer, such as cm |
| Using the diameter as <InlineMath math="r" /> | The circumference formula <InlineMath math="2\pi r" /> already contains the factor <InlineMath math="2" /> | Halve the diameter before you substitute |
| Using the minor arc angle for a major arc | A major arc covers <InlineMath math="360^\circ" /> minus the minor arc | A major arc must be longer than half the circumference |
| Treating a sector as a segment | A sector is bounded by two radii, a [segment](/en/subjects/mathematics/circle-arc-sector/segment) by a chord | Look at whether the region's edges are radii or a chord |

The diameter mistake doubles the arc length, but the result does not always look wrong. So write <InlineMath math="r = \frac{d}{2}" /> as the first step whenever a problem gives a diameter.

## Practice on Arcs of a Circle

For each problem, first find **the share of a full turn** the arc or sector takes. Problem 4 needs two steps.

1. A circle has diameter <InlineMath math="20\text{ cm}" />. The inscribed angle that intercepts arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" /> measures <InlineMath math="30^\circ" />. Find the measure of arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" /> and its length.

2. On a circle of radius <InlineMath math="21\text{ cm}" />, arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{PQ}" /> has a central angle of <InlineMath math="120^\circ" />. Find the length of arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{PQ}" /> and the area of sector <InlineMath math="POQ" />.

3. On a circle of radius <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />, the minor arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" /> has a central angle of <InlineMath math="72^\circ" />. Find the length of the major arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{ACB}" />.

4. A sector of a circle with radius <InlineMath math="12\text{ cm}" /> has area <InlineMath math="24\pi\text{ cm}^2" />. Find the length of its arc.

### Answers

**Problem 1**. An arc measures twice the inscribed angle that intercepts it, so arc <InlineMath math="\overset{\frown}{AB}" /> measures <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. The radius is **half the diameter**, <InlineMath math="10\text{ cm}" />:

<BlockMath math="l = \frac{60}{360} \times 2\pi \times 10 = \frac{10\pi}{3} \approx 10.47\text{ cm}" />

Check the size. The circumference is <InlineMath math="20\pi \approx 62.83\text{ cm}" />, and a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> arc is one sixth of it.

**Problem 2**. An angle of <InlineMath math="120^\circ" /> takes **one third of a turn**, because <InlineMath math="\frac{120}{360} = \frac{1}{3}" />:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="l = \frac{1}{3} \times 2\pi \times 21 = 14\pi \approx 43.98\text{ cm}" />
  <BlockMath math="A = \frac{1}{3} \times \pi \times 21^2 = 147\pi \approx 461.81\text{ cm}^2" />
</MathContainer>

Check with <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} l r = \frac{1}{2} \times 14\pi \times 21 = 147\pi" />.

**Problem 3**. The major arc covers **the rest of the turn**, <InlineMath math="360^\circ - 72^\circ = 288^\circ" />:

<BlockMath math="l = \frac{288}{360} \times 2\pi \times 10 = 16\pi \approx 50.27\text{ cm}" />

Check with the minor arc. The minor arc has length <InlineMath math="\frac{72}{360} \times 20\pi = 4\pi" />, and <InlineMath math="4\pi + 16\pi = 20\pi" /> is the circumference.

**Problem 4**. First find the share of a full turn from the area, then use the same share for **the arc length**:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\frac{\alpha}{360^\circ} = \frac{24\pi}{\pi \times 12^2} = \frac{24\pi}{144\pi} = \frac{1}{6}" />
  <BlockMath math="l = \frac{1}{6} \times 2\pi \times 12 = 4\pi \approx 12.57\text{ cm}" />
</MathContainer>

The central angle is <InlineMath math="60^\circ" />. Check with <InlineMath math="A = \frac{1}{2} l r = \frac{1}{2} \times 4\pi \times 12 = 24\pi" />.
