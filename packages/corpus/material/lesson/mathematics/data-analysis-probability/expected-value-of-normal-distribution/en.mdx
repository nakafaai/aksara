## The Expected Value Sits at the Center of the Curve

If <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="\mu" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma > 0" />, written <InlineMath math="X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)" />, its expected value equals the parameter <InlineMath math="\mu" />:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu" />

The expected value is the **probability-weighted average** of all values of <InlineMath math="X" />. If you take a very large number of observations from the distribution, their average gets closer and closer to <InlineMath math="\mu" />. The standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> only sets the width of the curve, so **the width of the curve does not change the expected value**.

Exam scores distributed as <InlineMath math="N(75, 10^2)" /> have an expected value of <InlineMath math="75" />. On a normal curve, the mean, median, mode, and expected value all meet at the axis of symmetry. The interactive graph marks that center together with two pairs of equally tall values on its left and right.

## Pairs of Values That Balance Each Other

Why is the expected value exactly <InlineMath math="\mu" />? The normal curve is symmetric about the line <InlineMath math="x = \mu" />. Every value <InlineMath math="\mu + d" /> has a partner <InlineMath math="\mu - d" /> with the same density, because the density formula contains only the square <InlineMath math="(x - \mu)^2" />, and <InlineMath math="(+d)^2 = (-d)^2" />.

The deviations of these two values from the center are <InlineMath math="+d" /> and <InlineMath math="-d" />. With equal weights, their contributions cancel. Every value can be paired this way, so **the average deviation from the center is zero**:

<BlockMath math="E(X - \mu) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E(X) = \mu" />

On the curve of <InlineMath math="N(2, 1)" />, the segments at <InlineMath math="x = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 3" /> both reach <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1/2} \approx 0.242" />. The segments at <InlineMath math="x = 0.5" /> and <InlineMath math="x = 3.5" /> both reach about <InlineMath math="0.130" />. This symmetry argument still needs one condition, namely that the integral for the expected value exists, and the proof with an integral confirms that it does.

## Proving the Formula with an Integral

For a continuous random variable, the expected value is the integral of <InlineMath math="x" /> times its density over the whole number line. Areas under a curve like this come from a [definite integral](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/definite-integral), here with infinite bounds. For a discrete variable, such as the number of successes in a [binomial distribution](/en/subjects/mathematics/data-analysis-probability/expected-value-of-binomial-distribution), the integral becomes a sum.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx" />
  <BlockMath math="f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}" />
</MathContainer>

The substitution <InlineMath math="t = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}" /> **moves the center to zero** and measures distance in standard deviations. It gives <InlineMath math="x = \sigma t + \mu" /> and <InlineMath math="dx = \sigma\,dt" />, so the factor <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> in the denominator cancels:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="E(X) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma t + \mu)\, e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2}\,dt" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E(X) &amp;= \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} t e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The first integral is zero because <InlineMath math="g(t) = t e^{-t^2/2}" /> is an **odd function**, meaning <InlineMath math="g(-t) = -g(t)" />. The signed areas to the left and right of the vertical axis are equal in size and opposite in sign, just as for the [integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/properties-of-definite-integral). Both halves are finite, so this zero does not come from subtracting two infinite values:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\int_0^\infty te^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\left[-e^{-t^2/2}\right]_0^\infty=0-(-1)=1" />
  <BlockMath math="\int_{-\infty}^0 te^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\left[-e^{-t^2/2}\right]_{-\infty}^0=-1-0=-1" />
</MathContainer>

The second integral is the Gaussian integral, with value <InlineMath math="\sqrt{2\pi}" />. The result is:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot 0 + \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi} = \mu" />

Check against the symmetry argument: both give <InlineMath math="\mu" />, and <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> drops out of the result, exactly like the equally tall pairs around the center.

## Expected Value of the Standard Normal Distribution

The standard normal distribution is the normal distribution with <InlineMath math="\mu = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 1" />, written <InlineMath math="Z \sim N(0, 1)" />. The rule <InlineMath math="E(X) = \mu" /> gives the **standard normal expected value <InlineMath math="E(Z) = 0" />** at once.

Every normal variable can be written from the standard normal as <InlineMath math="X = \mu + \sigma Z" />. Linearity of expectation, <InlineMath math="E(aZ + b) = aE(Z) + b" />, gives a second route without an integral:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = E(\mu + \sigma Z) = \mu + \sigma E(Z) = \mu + \sigma \cdot 0 = \mu" />

The same relation explains the z-score <InlineMath math="z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}" />, the distance from the center in standard deviations, whose average is zero. Because <InlineMath math="Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}" /> has the standard normal distribution, <InlineMath math="P(X < x)" /> equals <InlineMath math="\Phi\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)" />, so one table for <InlineMath math="Z" /> serves every [normal distribution](/en/subjects/mathematics/data-analysis-probability/normal-distribution-function).

## Linearity of Expected Value

Two rules settle most problems. For fixed numbers <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" />, **the expected value shifts and scales with the variable**, and the expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="E(aX + b) = a\mu + b" />
  <BlockMath math="E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)" />
</MathContainer>

**Example 1**. The daily mean temperature in Jakarta in June is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="\mu = 28^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 3^\circ\text{C}" />. The conversion to Fahrenheit is <InlineMath math="F = 1.8C + 32" />, the form <InlineMath math="aC + b" /> with <InlineMath math="a = 1.8" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 32" />:

<BlockMath math="E(F) = 1.8 \cdot 28 + 32 = 82.4^\circ\text{F}" />

Check with a symmetric pair of temperatures. The temperatures <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="31^\circ\text{C}" /> lie <InlineMath math="3" /> degrees from the center and become <InlineMath math="77^\circ\text{F}" /> and <InlineMath math="87.8^\circ\text{F}" />. Their midpoint is <InlineMath math="\frac{77 + 87.8}{2} = 82.4" />, the same as the formula.

**Example 2**. The weight of one bag of rice is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="\mu = 1{,}000\text{ g}" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 15\text{ g}" />. The expected total weight of <InlineMath math="20" /> bags is the sum of the expected weights of the bags:

<BlockMath math="E(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_{20}) = 20 \cdot 1{,}000 = 20{,}000\text{ g}" />

Check with ten pairs of bags: each pair has an expected weight of <InlineMath math="1{,}000 + 1{,}000 = 2{,}000\text{ g}" />, and <InlineMath math="10 \cdot 2{,}000 = 20{,}000\text{ g}" />. The standard deviation of <InlineMath math="15\text{ g}" /> plays no part, because it affects only the spread of the total weight, not its center.

On the line <InlineMath math="F = 1.8C + 32" /> the midpoint stays the midpoint. <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="31^\circ\text{C}" /> map to <InlineMath math="77^\circ\text{F}" /> and <InlineMath math="87.8^\circ\text{F}" />, and their center <InlineMath math="28^\circ\text{C}" /> maps to <InlineMath math="82.4^\circ\text{F}" />, the center of the images. That is why <InlineMath math="E(F) = 1.8E(C) + 32" />.

## Finding the Center from Given Probabilities

Sometimes a problem does not state <InlineMath math="\mu" /> directly but gives symmetric probabilities instead. On a normal curve, **two tails with equal areas lie the same distance from the center**.

**Example 3**. A variable <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="P(X < 40) = P(X > 60)" />. The two tails have equal areas, so <InlineMath math="40" /> and <InlineMath math="60" /> lie the same distance from the center, and the center is their midpoint:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu = \frac{40 + 60}{2} = 50" />

Check with one value of the standard deviation, for example <InlineMath math="\sigma = 10" />: <InlineMath math="P(X < 40) = P(Z < -1) \approx 0.1587" /> and <InlineMath math="P(X > 60) = P(Z > 1) \approx 0.1587" />. The two tails are equal, and the same holds for every <InlineMath math="\sigma" />, because <InlineMath math="40" /> and <InlineMath math="60" /> always lie the same distance from <InlineMath math="50" />.

**Example 4**. Half of a bus's trips finish in less than <InlineMath math="42" /> minutes, and the travel time is normally distributed. That statement means <InlineMath math="P(X < 42) = 0.5" />, so <InlineMath math="42" /> minutes is the median. For a normal distribution the median equals the mean, so <InlineMath math="E(X) = 42" /> minutes.

Check with symmetry, for example with <InlineMath math="\sigma = 6" /> minutes: <InlineMath math="P(X < 36) = P(X > 48) \approx 0.1587" />, so the areas to the left and right of <InlineMath math="42" /> are equal.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Giving <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> as the expected value | <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> measures the width of the spread, not the center | The expected value always lies on the axis of symmetry |
| Thinking <InlineMath math="X" /> must equal <InlineMath math="\mu" /> | For a continuous variable, <InlineMath math="P(X = \mu) = 0" /> | The expected value is a long-run average, not the result of one observation |
| Writing <InlineMath math="E(X^2) = \mu^2" /> | Squaring is not linear, so <InlineMath math="E(X^2) = \mu^2 + \sigma^2" /> | For <InlineMath math="Z \sim N(0, 1)" />, <InlineMath math="E(Z^2) = 1" />, not <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Forgetting to add <InlineMath math="b" /> in <InlineMath math="E(aX + b)" /> | The shift <InlineMath math="b" /> moves the center of the curve | Transform a symmetric pair of values and check their midpoint |
| Thinking a sum needs independence | <InlineMath math="E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)" /> holds without it | The variance of a sum equals the sum of the variances only when <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" /> are uncorrelated, for example independent |

For a question about <InlineMath math="E(X)" /> or <InlineMath math="E(aX + b)" />, one question catches the first and fourth mistakes: **does your answer lie on the axis of symmetry of the curve in question**? For <InlineMath math="E(X^2)" />, check with the variance, because <InlineMath math="E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2" /> must equal <InlineMath math="\sigma^2" />.

## Exercises

The first two problems use the rule directly, the third needs its link to the variance, and the fourth combines symmetry with a shift. **Find the value of <InlineMath math="\mu" /> first**, then check your answer before you read the solution.

1. Travel time from home to school is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="25" /> minutes and standard deviation <InlineMath math="5" /> minutes. The trip home has the same distribution. Find the expected time for one trip and for the round trip.

2. A variable <InlineMath math="Z" /> has the standard normal distribution. Find <InlineMath math="E(3Z + 2)" />.

3. A variable <InlineMath math="X" /> has the distribution <InlineMath math="N(10, 2^2)" />. Find <InlineMath math="E(X^2)" /> using <InlineMath math="\operatorname{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2" />.

4. A variable <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="P(X < 35) = P(X > 65)" />. Find <InlineMath math="E(2X - 10)" />.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. The mean of the distribution is <InlineMath math="25" />, so **the expected time for one trip is <InlineMath math="25" /> minutes**. The round trip is the sum of two travel times:

<BlockMath math="E(X_1 + X_2) = 25 + 25 = 50 \text{ minutes}" />

Check: the result is twice the mean of one trip, and the standard deviation of <InlineMath math="5" /> minutes does not affect the center.

**Problem 2**. The standard normal has <InlineMath math="E(Z) = 0" />. By linearity:

<BlockMath math="E(3Z + 2) = 3 \cdot 0 + 2 = 2" />

Check: <InlineMath math="3Z + 2" /> has the distribution <InlineMath math="N(2, 3^2)" />, a normal curve **centered at <InlineMath math="2" />**.

**Problem 3**. From <InlineMath math="N(10, 2^2)" />, the expected value is <InlineMath math="10" /> and the variance is <InlineMath math="4" />. Rearrange the variance formula for <InlineMath math="E(X^2)" />:

<BlockMath math="E(X^2) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + [E(X)]^2 = 4 + 10^2 = 104" />

Check: <InlineMath math="104 > 100 = [E(X)]^2" />, which matches a positive variance. Writing <InlineMath math="E(X^2) = 100" /> is **the mistake of treating squaring as linear**.

**Problem 4**. The two tails have equal areas, so the center is the midpoint of <InlineMath math="35" /> and <InlineMath math="65" />, which is <InlineMath math="\mu = 50" />. By linearity:

<BlockMath math="E(2X - 10) = 2 \cdot 50 - 10 = 90" />

Check with a symmetric pair: <InlineMath math="35" /> becomes <InlineMath math="60" /> and <InlineMath math="65" /> becomes <InlineMath math="120" />. Their midpoint is <InlineMath math="90" />, so **the new center still lies in the middle of the pair**.
