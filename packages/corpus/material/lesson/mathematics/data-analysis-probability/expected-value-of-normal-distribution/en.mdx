## Basic Concept of Expected Value

The **expected value** is the probability-weighted average of a random variable, or the long-run average approached across many independent observations. It is not generally the same as the most frequent value. For a normal distribution, however, the mean, median, mode, and expected value all meet at the same center.

This symmetry gives the normal distribution a simple expected-value rule.

> If <InlineMath math="X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)" /> with <InlineMath math="\sigma>0" />, its expected value equals the mean parameter <InlineMath math="\mu" />.

Mathematically, we can write:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu" />

In this notation, <InlineMath math="X" /> is a normally distributed random variable and <InlineMath math="\mu" /> is the distribution's mean parameter.

The following calculation shows why.

## Mathematical Proof

The expected value of a continuous random variable is defined as:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x) \, dx" />

For a normal distribution with <InlineMath math="\sigma>0" />, the probability density function is:

<BlockMath math="f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}" />

Substitute this function into the expected value formula:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2} \, dx" />

Use the substitution <InlineMath math="t = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}" />. Then <InlineMath math="x = \sigma t + \mu" /> and <InlineMath math="dx = \sigma \, dt" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma t + \mu) \cdot \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \cdot \sigma \, dt" />
<BlockMath math="E(X) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma t + \mu) e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt" />
<BlockMath math="E(X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} t e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt + \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt" />
</MathContainer>

Evaluate the two integrals separately:

- **First integral** <InlineMath math="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} t e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt = 0" /> because <InlineMath math="g(t)=t e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2}" /> is odd. Thus <InlineMath math="g(-t) = -g(t)" />, and the signed areas over the symmetric interval <InlineMath math="(-\infty, \infty)" /> cancel.

- **Second integral** <InlineMath math="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^2} \, dt = \sqrt{2\pi}" /> is the Gaussian integral. After multiplication by the normalizing factor <InlineMath math="1/\sqrt{2\pi}" />, it gives total probability <InlineMath math="1" /> for the standard normal density.

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E(X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot 0 + \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi}" />
<BlockMath math="E(X) = 0 + \mu = \mu" />
</MathContainer>

**The expected value of a normal random variable equals its mean parameter**.

> For every normal distribution <InlineMath math="X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)" />, we have <InlineMath math="E(X) = \mu" />. This means we don't need to perform integration every time we calculate the expected value of a normal distribution. We simply use the parameter value <InlineMath math="\mu" />.

## Practical Interpretation

The result has a direct statistical interpretation:

For a normal random variable with mean <InlineMath math="\mu" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma" />, the **expected value** is exactly <InlineMath math="\mu" />. Across many independent observations from this distribution, the sample average approaches <InlineMath math="\mu" /> under the law of large numbers.

**Example:**

If the height of students in your school is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="165 \text{ cm}" />, then the expected value of the height of a randomly selected student is <InlineMath math="165 \text{ cm}" />.

## Expected Values for Exam Scores and Birth Weights

**Example** <InlineMath math="1" />

Suppose the math exam scores in a class are normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="\mu = 75" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma = 10" />. What is the expected value of a student's exam score?

**Solution:**

Since normal distribution has the property <InlineMath math="E(X) = \mu" />, the expected value of the exam score is:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = 75" />

The expected value of a student's exam score is <InlineMath math="75" />.

**Example** <InlineMath math="2" />

Suppose newborn weights at a hospital are modeled by a normal distribution with mean <InlineMath math="3{,}200 \text{ grams}" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="400 \text{ grams}" />. Determine the expected weight of one randomly selected newborn.

**Solution:**

Given <InlineMath math="\mu = 3{,}200 \text{ grams}" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 400 \text{ grams}" />.

The expected weight is the mean parameter:

<BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu = 3{,}200 \text{ grams}" />

## Exercises

1. Travel time from home to school is modeled by a normal distribution with mean <InlineMath math="25 \text{ minutes}" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="5 \text{ minutes}" />. Determine the expected travel time.

2. Daily air temperature in Jakarta during June is modeled by a normal distribution with mean <InlineMath math="28^\circ\text{C}" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="3^\circ\text{C}" />. What is the expected temperature on a randomly selected day?

3. Physics exam scores of grade <InlineMath math="12" /> students are modeled by a normal distribution with mean <InlineMath math="78" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="12" />. What is the expected score of a randomly selected student?

### Answer Key

1. **Solution to Problem** <InlineMath math="1" />:

   Given: <InlineMath math="\mu = 25 \text{ minutes}" />, <InlineMath math="\sigma = 5 \text{ minutes}" />

   Since normal distribution has the property <InlineMath math="E(X) = \mu" />, then:

   <BlockMath math="E(X) = 25 \text{ minutes}" />

   **Answer:** The expected value of travel time is <InlineMath math="25 \text{ minutes}" />.

2. **Solution to Problem** <InlineMath math="2" />:

   Given: <InlineMath math="\mu = 28^\circ\text{C}" />, <InlineMath math="\sigma = 3^\circ\text{C}" />

   Using the basic property of normal distribution:

   <BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu = 28^\circ\text{C}" />

   **Answer:** The expected value of air temperature is <InlineMath math="28^\circ\text{C}" />.

3. **Solution to Problem** <InlineMath math="3" />:

   Given: <InlineMath math="\mu = 78" />, <InlineMath math="\sigma = 12" />

   A normally distributed variable satisfies <InlineMath math="E(X)=\mu" />. Therefore:

   <BlockMath math="E(X) = \mu = 78" />

   **Answer:** The expected exam score is <InlineMath math="78" />.
