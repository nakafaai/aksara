## A Bell Curve with a Center and a Spread

A normal distribution is a probability model for a continuous variable whose values **cluster around one central value and thin out symmetrically** on both sides. If <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="\mu" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma > 0" />, written <InlineMath math="X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)" />, its curve is bell shaped and symmetric about the line <InlineMath math="x = \mu" />. The probability that <InlineMath math="X" /> falls in an interval equals the area under the curve over that interval, and the total area is <InlineMath math="1" />.

On the interactive graph, the standard normal curve with <InlineMath math="\mu = 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 1" /> peaks at the center. Two vertical segments mark the inflection points at <InlineMath math="\mu - \sigma" /> and <InlineMath math="\mu + \sigma" />, where the curve changes from concave down to concave up.

The mean, median, and mode coincide at <InlineMath math="\mu" />, because the curve is symmetric and has a single peak. The curve never touches the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis: its density is positive for every <InlineMath math="x" /> and approaches zero far from the center. Abraham de Moivre derived this curve in <InlineMath math="1733" /> as an approximation to the [binomial distribution](/en/subjects/mathematics/data-analysis-probability/binomial-distribution-function) for a large number of trials.

## The Normal Density Formula

The height of the curve at each point <InlineMath math="x" /> is given by the density function:

<BlockMath math="f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}, \qquad -\infty < x < \infty" />

Here <InlineMath math="\pi \approx 3.1416" /> and <InlineMath math="e \approx 2.7183" /> are constants, while <InlineMath math="\mu" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma > 0" /> are the parameters of the distribution. The fraction <InlineMath math="\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}" /> measures **the distance from the center in standard deviations**. That distance is squared, so two values of <InlineMath math="x" /> equally far to the left and right of <InlineMath math="\mu" /> get the same density. The factor <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}" /> makes the total area under the curve exactly <InlineMath math="1" />.

A density is not a probability. At the peak of the standard normal curve, <InlineMath math="f(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.399" />. For <InlineMath math="\sigma = 0.25" />, the peak height is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{0.25\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 1.596" />, more than <InlineMath math="1" />, while a probability never exceeds <InlineMath math="1" />. The probability of one exact value is zero as well, <InlineMath math="P(X = a) = 0" />, because the area above a single point is zero. Probabilities always belong to intervals and come from a [definite integral](/en/subjects/mathematics/integral/definite-integral) of the density.

## The Center and Width of a Normal Curve

The parameter <InlineMath math="\mu" /> **shifts the curve without changing its shape**, while <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> sets its width. The area under the curve is always <InlineMath math="1" />, so a wider curve is also lower, with peak height <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}" />. The center of the curve is always at <InlineMath math="\mu" />, so the [expected value of a normal distribution](/en/subjects/mathematics/data-analysis-probability/expected-value-of-normal-distribution) is <InlineMath math="\mu" /> as well.

## Area Around the Center of the Curve

Every normal distribution splits its area in the same way when distances are measured in standard deviations. These percentages are known as **the <InlineMath math="68" />, <InlineMath math="95" />, and <InlineMath math="99.7" /> percent rule**:

| Interval | Area under the curve |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="\mu - \sigma" /> to <InlineMath math="\mu + \sigma" /> | <InlineMath math="68.27\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mu - 2\sigma" /> to <InlineMath math="\mu + 2\sigma" /> | <InlineMath math="95.45\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mu - 3\sigma" /> to <InlineMath math="\mu + 3\sigma" /> | <InlineMath math="99.73\%" /> |

Suppose the heights of a group of teenagers follow <InlineMath math="N(165, 6^2)" /> in centimeters. About <InlineMath math="68\%" /> of the heights lie between <InlineMath math="159" /> and <InlineMath math="171" /> cm, and about <InlineMath math="95\%" /> lie between <InlineMath math="153" /> and <InlineMath math="177" /> cm. Only about <InlineMath math="0.27\%" /> lie more than <InlineMath math="18" /> cm from the mean.

## Z Scores Standardize Every Normal Distribution

A z score turns a value <InlineMath math="x" /> into its distance from the mean in standard deviations:

<BlockMath math="z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}" />

If <InlineMath math="X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)" />, then <InlineMath math="Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}" /> has the **standard normal distribution <InlineMath math="N(0, 1)" />**. As a result, one table of cumulative probabilities <InlineMath math="\Phi(z) = P(Z < z)" /> serves every normal distribution. Tables usually list only positive <InlineMath math="z" />, and the symmetry of the curve gives the rest: <InlineMath math="\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)" />.

Z scores also compare values from different groups. A math score of <InlineMath math="80" /> comes from a distribution with mean <InlineMath math="70" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="5" />, while a physics score of <InlineMath math="90" /> comes from a distribution with mean <InlineMath math="80" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="10" />.

| Subject | Z score |
| --- | --- |
| Math | <InlineMath math="z = \frac{80 - 70}{5} = 2" /> |
| Physics | <InlineMath math="z = \frac{90 - 80}{10} = 1" /> |

The math score is **higher relative to its own group**, even though the raw score of <InlineMath math="80" /> is less than <InlineMath math="90" />. This comparison measures position within each distribution, not how difficult the two exams were.

## Finding Probabilities with a Z Table

A normal probability takes three steps: standardize the bounds, read <InlineMath math="\Phi" /> from the table, then **subtract the cumulative area at the lower bound from the cumulative area at the upper bound**.

**Example 1**. Suppose <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="\mu = 50" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 10" />. The probability <InlineMath math="45 < X < 62" /> comes from the z scores of both bounds:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="z_1 = \frac{45 - 50}{10} = -0.5, \qquad z_2 = \frac{62 - 50}{10} = 1.2" />
  <BlockMath math="P(45 < X < 62) = \Phi(1.2) - \Phi(-0.5) \approx 0.8849 - 0.3085 = 0.5764" />
</MathContainer>

The probability is about <InlineMath math="0.5764" />, or <InlineMath math="57.64\%" />. The interactive graph shows this area on the standard normal curve, between <InlineMath math="z = -0.5" /> and <InlineMath math="z = 1.2" />.

**Example 2**. With heights following <InlineMath math="N(165, 6^2)" />, the probability that a teenager is taller than <InlineMath math="177" /> cm is the area of the right tail:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="z = \frac{177 - 165}{6} = 2" />
  <BlockMath math="P(X > 177) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the <InlineMath math="95\%" /> rule: an area of <InlineMath math="95.45\%" /> lies within two standard deviations, so the remaining <InlineMath math="4.55\%" /> splits equally between the two tails. One tail holds about <InlineMath math="2.28\%" />, which matches <InlineMath math="0.0228" />.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> as a probability | <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> is a density height and can exceed <InlineMath math="1" /> | A probability is an area and always lies between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Standardizing with the variance | A z score uses the standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma" />, not <InlineMath math="\sigma^2" /> | For <InlineMath math="N(50, 100)" />, the standard deviation is <InlineMath math="10" /> |
| Reading <InlineMath math="P(X > a)" /> straight from the table | A z table gives the area to the left, <InlineMath math="P(Z < z)" /> | The right tail is <InlineMath math="1 - \Phi(z)" /> |
| Subtracting in the wrong order | <InlineMath math="P(a < Z < b) = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a)" /> for <InlineMath math="a < b" /> | The resulting probability must be positive |
| Using a normal model for strongly skewed data | A normal model assumes a symmetric spread | Compare the mean and median of the data first |

Before you calculate, **sketch the curve and shade the area the question asks for**. The sketch shows at once whether your answer must be below <InlineMath math="0.5" />, above <InlineMath math="0.5" />, or close to one of the <InlineMath math="68" />, <InlineMath math="95" />, and <InlineMath math="99.7" /> percent figures.

## Exercises

The first problem practices z scores, the second and third find areas, and the fourth reverses the direction, from a probability to a boundary value. Use a z table with four decimal places, then **check your answer with a sketch of the curve** before you read the solution.

1. Exam scores follow <InlineMath math="N(70, 8^2)" />. Find the z score of a score of <InlineMath math="82" /> and explain what it means.

2. With the same score distribution, find the probability that a student scores less than <InlineMath math="58" />.

3. The weight of a sugar package is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="250" /> g and standard deviation <InlineMath math="5" /> g. Find the probability that one package weighs between <InlineMath math="240" /> g and <InlineMath math="260" /> g.

4. The time to finish a test is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="60" /> minutes and standard deviation <InlineMath math="12" /> minutes. Find the time limit that only the slowest <InlineMath math="10\%" /> of test takers exceed.

### Worked Solutions

**Problem 1**. A z score compares the distance from the mean with the standard deviation:

<BlockMath math="z = \frac{82 - 70}{8} = \frac{12}{8} = 1.5" />

A score of <InlineMath math="82" /> lies **one and a half standard deviations above the mean**. Check: <InlineMath math="70 + 1.5 \cdot 8 = 82" />.

**Problem 2**. The z score of <InlineMath math="58" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{58 - 70}{8} = -1.5" />. The table lists only positive z, so use symmetry:

<BlockMath math="P(X < 58) = \Phi(-1.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) \approx 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668" />

Check: <InlineMath math="58" /> and <InlineMath math="82" /> lie the same distance from <InlineMath math="70" />, so **the two tails have equal areas**, and <InlineMath math="P(X > 82) = 1 - 0.9332" /> is also <InlineMath math="0.0668" />.

**Problem 3**. Both bounds lie <InlineMath math="10" /> g from the mean, which gives <InlineMath math="z = \pm 2" />:

<BlockMath math="P(240 < X < 260) = \Phi(2) - \Phi(-2) \approx 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9544" />

Check with the <InlineMath math="95\%" /> rule: the interval reaches exactly **two standard deviations to each side of the mean**, and the exact value is <InlineMath math="0.9545" />. The difference of <InlineMath math="0.0001" /> comes from rounding in the table.

**Problem 4**. The limit <InlineMath math="c" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="P(X > c) = 0.10" />, so the area to its left is <InlineMath math="0.90" />. The table gives <InlineMath math="\Phi(1.28) \approx 0.8997" />, the closest value to <InlineMath math="0.90" />, so <InlineMath math="z \approx 1.28" />. Convert back to minutes:

<BlockMath math="c = \mu + z\sigma \approx 60 + 1.28 \cdot 12 = 75.36" />

The limit is about <InlineMath math="75.4" /> minutes. Check: <InlineMath math="\frac{75.36 - 60}{12} = 1.28" />, and the area to the right of that score is <InlineMath math="1 - 0.8997 \approx 0.10" />, so **only a tenth of the test takers go past the limit**.
