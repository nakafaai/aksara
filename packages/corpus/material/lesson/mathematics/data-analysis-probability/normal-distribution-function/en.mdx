## Normal Distribution

Measurements such as human height or repeated laboratory results often cluster around a central value. In some populations and under suitable conditions, a **normal distribution** can approximate that pattern. Check the model against the observed data before using it.

In <InlineMath math="1733" />, Abraham de Moivre derived a normal approximation to the binomial distribution for large <InlineMath math="n" />. Pierre-Simon Laplace later extended this result, which is now associated with the de Moivre-Laplace theorem and the development of the central limit theorem.

> The normal distribution is one possible model. Use it when observations are roughly symmetric around a mean and the model fits the data well enough for the question being studied.

The curve has four defining characteristics:

1. **Shape and Symmetry**

    The curve is bell-shaped and symmetrical about the vertical line passing through the mean (<InlineMath math="\mu" />). This means the left and right parts of the curve are perfect mirrors of each other.

2. **Central Point**

    The normal density is symmetric and has one peak. Therefore, its mean, median, and mode all coincide at <InlineMath math="\mu" />.

3. **Inflection Points**

    The curve has inflection points at <InlineMath math="x = \mu \pm \sigma" />, which means the curve changes from concave to convex (or vice versa) at a distance of one standard deviation from the mean.

4. **Horizontal Asymptote**

    The density remains positive for every finite <InlineMath math="x" /> and approaches <InlineMath math="0" /> as <InlineMath math="x" /> tends to either infinity.

## Probability Density Function

The formula below gives the probability density at each value of <InlineMath math="x" />.

If <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with mean <InlineMath math="\mu" />, variance <InlineMath math="\sigma^2" />, and <InlineMath math="\sigma>0" />, its density function is:

<BlockMath math="f_{\mu,\sigma}(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}" />

This formula applies to every <InlineMath math="x" /> satisfying <InlineMath math="-\infty < x < \infty" />.

The constants and parameters are:

- <InlineMath math="\pi \approx 3.1416" /> is the circle constant
- <InlineMath math="e \approx 2.7183" /> is Euler's number
- <InlineMath math="\mu" /> is the mean of the distribution
- <InlineMath math="\sigma>0" /> is the standard deviation

> The parameter <InlineMath math="\mu" /> shifts the center of the curve, while <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> controls its spread. Find probabilities from areas under the density curve. The height <InlineMath math="f(x)" /> at one point is a density value.

## Transformation to Standard Normal

In practice, we often use the **standard normal distribution** with mean <InlineMath math="\mu = 0" /> and standard deviation <InlineMath math="\sigma = 1" />. To convert a regular normal distribution to standard normal, we use the transformation:

<BlockMath math="Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}" />

The variable <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the **standard score**, or **z-score**. It measures how many standard deviations <InlineMath math="x" /> lies above or below the mean. Standardization maps every normal distribution to the same <InlineMath math="Z" /> scale, so one standard normal table works for all of them.

**Why use z-score?**

With this transformation, we can compare data from different distributions. For example, you can compare math scores with physics scores, even though their means and standard deviations are different.

## Finding a Normal Probability Between Two Bounds

Suppose <InlineMath math="X" /> is normally distributed with <InlineMath math="\mu = 50" /> and <InlineMath math="\sigma = 10" />. Find the probability that <InlineMath math="X" /> lies between <InlineMath math="45" /> and <InlineMath math="62" />.

**Step** <InlineMath math="1" />: Transform to z-score

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="z_1 = \frac{45 - 50}{10} = \frac{-5}{10} = -0.5" />
<BlockMath math="z_2 = \frac{62 - 50}{10} = \frac{12}{10} = 1.2" />
</MathContainer>

**Step** <InlineMath math="2" />: Use the standard normal distribution table

The transformed interval is <InlineMath math="P(-0.5 < Z < 1.2)" />. Subtract the cumulative probability at the lower boundary from the cumulative probability at the upper boundary:

<BlockMath math="P(a < Z < b) = P(Z < b) - P(Z < a)" />

From the standard normal distribution table:

- <InlineMath math="P(Z < -0.5) = 0.3085" />
- <InlineMath math="P(Z < 1.2) = 0.8849" />

**Step** <InlineMath math="3" />: Calculate the final probability

<BlockMath math="P(45 < X < 62) = P(-0.5 < Z < 1.2) = 0.8849 - 0.3085 = 0.5764" />

So, the probability that <InlineMath math="X" /> lies between <InlineMath math="45" /> and <InlineMath math="62" /> is <InlineMath math="0.5764" /> or approximately <InlineMath math="57.64\%" />.
