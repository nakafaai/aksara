## Understanding Simple Probability

Many everyday situations have uncertain outcomes. A fair coin, for example, can land heads or tails, and the two outcomes are equally likely.

When a finite sample space contains equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event <InlineMath math="A" /> is the number of outcomes in <InlineMath math="A" /> divided by the total number of outcomes in the sample space <InlineMath math="S" />.

<BlockMath math="P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}" />

## Concept of Uniform Distribution

In a discrete uniform distribution, **every possible outcome has exactly the same probability**. This lesson concerns the discrete model on a finite set; a continuous uniform distribution instead uses a density over an interval.

For a fair six-sided die, the possible outcomes are <InlineMath math="1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6" />. Rolling a <InlineMath math="1" /> is just as likely as rolling a <InlineMath math="6" />. Each outcome therefore has probability <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6}" />.

## Mathematical Formula

Suppose a random variable <InlineMath math="X" /> has a finite set <InlineMath math="S" /> of <InlineMath math="k" /> possible values and all of them are equally likely. Then its probability mass function is:

<BlockMath math="P(X=x) = \frac{1}{k}, \qquad x \in S" />

Where:

- <InlineMath math="P(X=x)" /> is the probability that <InlineMath math="X" /> takes the particular value <InlineMath math="x" />

- <InlineMath math="x" /> is one of the possible outcomes (for example, the number <InlineMath math="3" /> on a die)

- <InlineMath math="k" /> is the total number of possible outcomes (for example, <InlineMath math="6" /> for a die)

> Each outcome receives an equal share of the total probability: one divided by the number of possible outcomes.

## Practical Applications

The following examples apply the definition to finite sample spaces.

### Rolling a Die

For example, we have a balanced six-sided die that is rolled once. How do we determine its uniform distribution?

- **Possible Outcomes:** The sample space is <InlineMath math="\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}" />

- **Total Number of Outcomes:** There are <InlineMath math="6" /> possible outcomes, so <InlineMath math="k=6" />

- **Probability Mass Function:**

  <BlockMath math="P(X=x) = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad x \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}" />

- **Meaning:** The probability of getting the numbers <InlineMath math="1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6" /> is each <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{6}" /> or approximately <InlineMath math="16.67\%" />

### Prize Wheel

Suppose a fair spinner is divided into <InlineMath math="8" /> equal sections, numbered <InlineMath math="1" /> through <InlineMath math="8" />. What is the probability that it stops on <InlineMath math="5" />?

- **Possible Outcomes:** <InlineMath math="\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}" />

- **Total Number of Outcomes:** There are <InlineMath math="8" /> sections, so <InlineMath math="k=8" />

- **Probability Mass Function:**

  <BlockMath math="P(X=x) = \frac{1}{8}, \qquad x \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8\}" />

- **Meaning:** The probability that the wheel stops on number <InlineMath math="5" /> (or any other number) is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{8}" /> or <InlineMath math="12.5\%" />

## Exercises

1. A bag contains <InlineMath math="10" /> identical balls except for their colors: <InlineMath math="1 \text{ red ball}" />, <InlineMath math="1 \text{ blue ball}" />, <InlineMath math="1 \text{ green ball}" />, and so on up to <InlineMath math="10" /> different colors. If one ball is drawn randomly, what is the probability of drawing a yellow ball? Also write the probability mass function.

2. In a standard bridge card set containing <InlineMath math="52" /> cards, each card has the same probability of being drawn. What can you conclude about the probability distribution of drawing one card from that deck? What is the probability of drawing the King of Hearts?

### Answer Key

1. **Answer for Colored Ball Problem**

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="1" />: Calculate total possibilities.

      There are <InlineMath math="10" /> balls with different colors, so the total possible outcomes is <InlineMath math="k=10" />.

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="2" />: Determine the probability mass function.

      Because the balls are identical except for color, each one has the same probability of being drawn. Use the discrete uniform formula:

      <BlockMath math="P(X=x) = \frac{1}{10}, \qquad x \in S" />

      Here <InlineMath math="x" /> represents one of the <InlineMath math="10" /> available ball colors.

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="3" />: Calculate the probability of the yellow ball.

      The yellow ball is one of the <InlineMath math="10" /> balls, so its probability is the same as that of every other ball:

      <BlockMath math="P(\text{Yellow Ball}) = \frac{1}{10}" />

      Therefore, the probability of drawing a yellow ball is **<InlineMath math="\frac{1}{10}" />** or **<InlineMath math="10\%" />**

2. **Answer for Bridge Card Problem**

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="1" />: Analyze the type of distribution.

      Since every card has the same probability of being drawn, the probability distribution is a **discrete uniform distribution**.

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="2" />: Calculate total possibilities.

      A standard bridge card set has <InlineMath math="52" /> different cards, so <InlineMath math="k=52" />

    - **Step** <InlineMath math="3" />: Calculate the probability of King of Hearts.

      The King of Hearts is one of the <InlineMath math="52" /> available cards, so using the uniform distribution principle:

      <BlockMath math="P(\text{King of Hearts}) = \frac{1}{52}" />

      Therefore, the probability of drawing the King of Hearts is **<InlineMath math="\frac{1}{52}" />** or approximately **<InlineMath math="1.92\%" />**
