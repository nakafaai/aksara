## Four Tools on the Cartesian Plane

Coordinate geometry solves problems about shapes with numbers. This chapter gives you four tools:

1. **Divisor of a line segment**: the point that divides <InlineMath math="AB" /> in the ratio <InlineMath math="m : n" /> is <InlineMath math="\left(\dfrac{nx_1 + mx_2}{m + n}, \dfrac{ny_1 + my_2}{m + n}\right)" />.
2. **Parallel and perpendicular lines**: parallel lines have equal gradients, <InlineMath math="m_1 = m_2" />, and perpendicular lines satisfy <InlineMath math="m_1 m_2 = -1" />.
3. **Area of a polygon**: list the vertices in order, then use the shoelace formula.
4. **Locus**: write the rule that the moving point <InlineMath math="P(x, y)" /> obeys as distances, then simplify it into an equation.

In these notes you slide a point along a line segment, turn a line until it is perpendicular, drag the vertex of a triangle, and trace a point that moves by a distance rule.

## Divisor of a Line Segment

The point <InlineMath math="P" /> divides the segment <InlineMath math="AB" /> in the ratio <InlineMath math="m : n" /> if <InlineMath math="AP : PB = m : n" />. For <InlineMath math="A(x_1, y_1)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(x_2, y_2)" />:

<BlockMath math="P = \left(\frac{nx_1 + mx_2}{m + n}, \frac{ny_1 + my_2}{m + n}\right)" />

Notice that <InlineMath math="n" /> multiplies the coordinates of <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="m" /> multiplies the coordinates of <InlineMath math="B" />. **The larger part of the ratio pulls <InlineMath math="P" /> towards the point far from it**. Change <InlineMath math="m" /> and <InlineMath math="n" /> below for <InlineMath math="A(-2, 1)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(6, 5)" />.

## Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Two lines are **parallel** if their gradients are equal. Two lines are **perpendicular** if the product of their gradients is <InlineMath math="-1" />, so one gradient is the negative reciprocal of the other. ## Area of a Polygon

For a polygon with vertices <InlineMath math="(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)" /> listed in order, either clockwise or anticlockwise, the area is:

<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|(x_1y_2 + x_2y_3 + \cdots + x_ny_1) - (y_1x_2 + y_2x_3 + \cdots + y_nx_1)\right|" />

This is the **shoelace formula**, because the cross multiplications lace together like a shoelace. Repeat the first vertex at the end of the list so that no pair is missed. Drag the vertex <InlineMath math="C" /> of the triangle below. The area reading becomes zero when <InlineMath math="C" /> lies on the line <InlineMath math="AB" />, because three collinear points do not form a triangle.

## The Equation of a Locus

A **locus** is the path of a point <InlineMath math="P(x, y)" /> that moves by one rule. To find its equation, write the rule with the distance formula <InlineMath math="\sqrt{(x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2}" />, square to remove the square roots, then simplify.

### A Point Equidistant from Two Points

The point <InlineMath math="P" /> moves so that <InlineMath math="PA = PB" />, for <InlineMath math="A(1, 2)" /> and <InlineMath math="B(5, 6)" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = (x - 5)^2 + (y - 6)^2" />
<BlockMath math="-2x + 1 - 4y + 4 = -10x + 25 - 12y + 36" />
<BlockMath math="8x + 8y = 56 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = 7 - x" />
</MathContainer>

This locus is the **perpendicular bisector** of <InlineMath math="AB" />. Move <InlineMath math="P" /> along the line and compare the two distances.

<MathContainer>

## Common Mistakes in Coordinate Geometry

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Multiplying <InlineMath math="m" /> by the coordinates of <InlineMath math="A" /> in the divisor formula. | The part <InlineMath math="m" /> must multiply the coordinates of <InlineMath math="B" />. | The point <InlineMath math="P" /> must be nearer the point with the smaller part. |
| Treating perpendicular gradients as equal and opposite, such as <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="-2" />. | The perpendicular gradient is the negative reciprocal, <InlineMath math="-\dfrac{1}{2}" />. | Multiply the two gradients and make sure the product is <InlineMath math="-1" />. |
| Listing the vertices of a polygon in a random order in the shoelace formula. | Vertices out of order form a polygon whose sides cross. | Sketch the vertices first and follow one direction. |
| Forgetting the absolute value in the area formula. | A clockwise order gives a negative value. | An area is always positive. |
| Not squaring both sides in a ratio locus question. | The ratio <InlineMath math="2 : 1" /> becomes a factor of <InlineMath math="4" /> after squaring. | Write <InlineMath math="PA^2 = 4PB^2" /> before expanding. |

The first mistake is the most common. **The parts of the ratio multiply crosswise**: <InlineMath math="n" /> with <InlineMath math="A" />, <InlineMath math="m" /> with <InlineMath math="B" />.

## Coordinate Geometry Practice

Sketch each question on the Cartesian plane first so that **you can check the answer by eye**.

1. The point <InlineMath math="R" /> divides the line segment joining <InlineMath math="C(1, 7)" /> and <InlineMath math="D(9, -1)" /> in the ratio <InlineMath math="1 : 3" />. Find the coordinates of <InlineMath math="R" />.

2. Show that the lines <InlineMath math="3x - 2y = 6" /> and <InlineMath math="2x + 3y = 12" /> are perpendicular. Then find the equation of the line parallel to <InlineMath math="3x - 2y = 6" /> that passes through <InlineMath math="(0, 5)" />.

3. Work out the area of the triangle with vertices <InlineMath math="(2, 1)" />, <InlineMath math="(8, 3)" /> and <InlineMath math="(4, 7)" />.

4. The point <InlineMath math="P" /> moves so that its distance from <InlineMath math="A(2, 3)" /> is always <InlineMath math="5" /> units. Find the equation of the locus of <InlineMath math="P" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Here <InlineMath math="m = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="n = 3" />:

<BlockMath math="R = \left(\frac{3(1) + 1(9)}{4}, \frac{3(7) + 1(-1)}{4}\right) = \left(\frac{12}{4}, \frac{20}{4}\right) = (3, 5)" />

Check: <InlineMath math="R" /> is nearer <InlineMath math="C" />, because <InlineMath math="CR" /> is only a quarter of <InlineMath math="CD" />.

**Solution 2**: Write each line in the form <InlineMath math="y = mx + c" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="3x - 2y = 6 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = \frac{3}{2}x - 3, \quad m_1 = \frac{3}{2}" />
<BlockMath math="2x + 3y = 12 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 4, \quad m_2 = -\frac{2}{3}" />
<BlockMath math="m_1 m_2 = \frac{3}{2} \times \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -1" />
</MathContainer>

So the two lines are perpendicular. The parallel line has gradient <InlineMath math="\dfrac{3}{2}" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />-intercept <InlineMath math="5" />, so its equation is <InlineMath math="y = \dfrac{3}{2}x + 5" />.

**Solution 3**: Use the shoelace formula:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2(3) + 8(7) + 4(1) = 66" />
<BlockMath math="1(8) + 3(4) + 7(2) = 34" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|66 - 34\right| = 16 \text{ units}^2" />
</MathContainer>

**Solution 4**: The rule <InlineMath math="PA = 5" /> gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\sqrt{(x - 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2} = 5" />
<BlockMath math="(x - 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 25" />
<BlockMath math="x^2 + y^2 - 4x - 6y - 12 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

This locus is **a circle** with centre <InlineMath math="(2, 3)" /> and radius <InlineMath math="5" />. Check with the point <InlineMath math="(7, 3)" />: <InlineMath math="49 + 9 - 28 - 18 - 12 = 0" />.
