## Linear Regression

A [scatter diagram](/en/subjects/mathematics/statistics-regression/scatter-diagram) shows whether paired values of two variables, <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" />, form a common pattern.

When the point cloud follows an approximately increasing or decreasing straight-line trend, we can summarize that trend with a fitted line. This line is the **linear regression line**, and the procedure used to determine it is **linear regression**.

## The Best Fit Line

The linear regression line is often called the _best-fit_ line. Under the least squares criterion, it is selected because it has the smallest possible sum of squared vertical residuals. Data points may lie above or below the line. The regression line summarizes the linear trend in the data.

## Predictions from the Regression Line

One of its main uses is **prediction**. For example, we could use the line to estimate the exam score of a student who studies for <InlineMath math="7 \text{ hours}" />. However, the observed study times only run from <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="6" /> hours. A prediction at <InlineMath math="7 \text{ hours}" /> is therefore an **extrapolation** and is less reliable than a prediction inside the observed range.

## Least Squares Method

The linear regression line (the _best-fit_ line) is found using a method called the **Least Squares Method**. The idea is to find the straight line that **minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances** from each data point to the line.

Mathematically, the linear regression line has the form:

<BlockMath math="\hat{y} = a + bx" />

Where:

- <InlineMath math="\hat{y}" /> (read: <InlineMath math="y" />-hat) is the **predicted value of <InlineMath math="y" />** by
  the regression line.
- <InlineMath math="x" /> is the value of the independent variable.
- <InlineMath math="b" /> is the **slope** of the line, indicating how much <InlineMath math="\hat{y}" /> changes
  for each one-unit change in <InlineMath math="x" />.
- <InlineMath math="a" /> is the **<InlineMath math="y" />-intercept**, which is the predicted value of <InlineMath math="y" /> when <InlineMath math="x = 0" />. This interpretation is useful only when <InlineMath math="x = 0" /> is meaningful in the context.

The values of <InlineMath math="b" /> and <InlineMath math="a" /> are calculated from the <InlineMath math="(x, y)" /> data we have using the following formulas:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="b = \frac{n(\sum xy) - (\sum x)(\sum y)}{n(\sum x^2) - (\sum x)^2}" />
  <BlockMath math="a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}" />
</MathContainer>

Formula key:

- <InlineMath math="n" /> is the number of data pairs.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x" /> is the sum of all <InlineMath math="x" /> values.
- <InlineMath math="\sum y" /> is the sum of all <InlineMath math="y" /> values.
- <InlineMath math="\sum xy" /> is the sum of the product of each <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> pair.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x^2" /> is the sum of the square of each <InlineMath math="x" /> value.
- <InlineMath math="\bar{x}" /> is the mean of the <InlineMath math="x" /> values (<InlineMath math="\frac{\sum x}{n}" />
  ).
- <InlineMath math="\bar{y}" /> is the mean of the <InlineMath math="y" /> values (<InlineMath math="\frac{\sum y}{n}" />
  ).

These formulas determine the unique least-squares line when the observed <InlineMath math="x" /> values are not all equal. If every <InlineMath math="x" /> value is identical, the slope denominator is zero and the data do not determine a unique regression slope.
