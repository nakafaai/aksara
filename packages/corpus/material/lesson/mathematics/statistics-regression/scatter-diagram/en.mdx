## Scatter Diagram

A scatter diagram plots paired values from two numerical variables as points. For example, we can place study time on the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis and exam scores on the <InlineMath math="y" />-axis.

Each point represents one paired observation, such as one student's study time and score. The point cloud shows whether the pairs form a recognizable pattern.

### When Should a Scatter Diagram Be Used

A scatter diagram is especially useful when we want to:

- check whether one numerical variable tends to rise, fall, or show no consistent change as the other variable changes, such as height and weight or study time and scores.
- examine the **direction and form** of that pattern, including a positive, negative, or unclear tendency.

This differs from other diagrams:

- **Bar chart:** compares values between **categories**, such as the number of students in different classes.
- **Line chart:** shows development over **time** or another fixed sequence, such as daily temperatures.
- **Pie chart:** shows **parts of a whole**, such as the distribution of preferred fruit types.

When the main question concerns a possible **relationship between two numerical variables**, a scatter diagram is an appropriate starting point.

## Scatter Diagram Examples and Correlation Patterns

The following examples show three typical point patterns.

### Positive Correlation

If the points tend to rise from the bottom left to the top right, it means there is a **positive correlation**. As the value of <InlineMath math="X" /> increases, the value of <InlineMath math="Y" /> also tends to increase.

The direction, shape, and spread of the points provide an initial description of how two variables vary together. Separate colors or markers distinguish the patterns of different groups. A scatter diagram describes association, but it does not by itself prove that one variable causes the other.
