## Choosing the Line That Best Fits the Data

Suppose a set of observations or experimental results consists of value pairs <InlineMath math="(x, y)" />. When their scatter diagram follows an approximately straight trend, many different lines could be drawn through the point cloud.

The question is, out of the many straight lines we could draw through these points, which is the **best straight line** that best represents the entire dataset?

The Least Squares Method is a **mathematical procedure** used to find the straight line that best fits the set of data points according to a precise criterion: <Highlight>the smallest possible sum of squared vertical residuals</Highlight>. The fitted slope is unique as long as the observed <InlineMath math="x" /> values are not all identical.

## Minimizing Squared Residuals

The fitted line is determined from the vertical **residual** at each observed point:

1.  **Prediction Line:** We try drawing a straight line (<InlineMath math="\hat{y} = a + bx" />) among the data points.
2.  **Residual:** For each observed value <InlineMath math="y_i" />, the line gives a predicted value <InlineMath math="\hat{y}_i" />. Their signed vertical difference is the **residual**:

    <BlockMath math="e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i" />

3.  **Minimize the residual sum of squares:** The least-squares line makes <InlineMath math="\sum e_i^2" /> as small as possible among all candidate lines.

Why square the residuals?

- Squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from canceling one another.
- A residual with twice the magnitude contributes four times as much to the sum, so large departures receive more weight.

## Finding the Line by Minimizing Squared Residuals

Mathematically, we are looking for the line with the equation:

<BlockMath math="\hat{y} = a + bx" />

The intercept <InlineMath math="a" /> and slope <InlineMath math="b" /> are chosen to minimize the following **sum of squared residuals**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \sum e_i^2 &amp;= \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \\ &amp;= \sum (y_i - (a + bx_i))^2 \end{aligned}" />

The coefficients that minimize the residual sum of squares are calculated from sums, products, and squares of the observations:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="b = \frac{n(\sum xy) - (\sum x)(\sum y)}{n(\sum x^2) - (\sum x)^2}" />
  <BlockMath math="a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}" />
</MathContainer>

Formula key:

- <InlineMath math="n" /> = Number of data pairs.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x" />, <InlineMath math="\sum y" /> = Sum of all <InlineMath math="x" /> and
  <InlineMath math="y" /> values.
- <InlineMath math="\sum xy" /> = Sum of the product of each <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> pair.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x^2" /> = Sum of the square of each <InlineMath math="x" /> value.
- <InlineMath math="\bar{x}" /> = Mean of <InlineMath math="x" /> (<InlineMath math="\frac{\sum x}{n}" />
  ).
- <InlineMath math="\bar{y}" /> = Mean of <InlineMath math="y" /> (<InlineMath math="\frac{\sum y}{n}" />
  ).

The least-squares method provides a systematic criterion for the line that represents the linear trend: its residual sum of squares is minimal.

If every <InlineMath math="x" /> value is the same, then <InlineMath math="n(\sum x^2)-(\sum x)^2=0" /> and the slope formula has a zero denominator. In that case, the data cannot determine a unique linear regression slope.

### Calculating the Coefficients from the Data

Use every pair in the plot. The product and square columns keep each observation in the calculation exactly once. The **sum of each column** then enters the slope formula.
