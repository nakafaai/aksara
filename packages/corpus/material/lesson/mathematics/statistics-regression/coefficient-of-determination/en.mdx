## What is the Coefficient of Determination?

After fitting a linear regression line, the next question is: **how much of the observed variation does the model account for?**

The measure that answers this question is the **coefficient of determination**, usually denoted by <InlineMath math="R^2" />. In the simple linear regression model with one predictor and an intercept used in this lesson, <InlineMath math="R^2=r^2" />, so we can obtain it by squaring Pearson's correlation coefficient.

The value <InlineMath math="r^2" /> gives the **proportion or percentage** of the observed variation in the dependent variable <InlineMath math="Y" /> that the fitted linear model accounts for using <InlineMath math="X" />.

## Calculating the Coefficient of Determination

The easiest way to calculate <InlineMath math="r^2" /> is by **squaring the Correlation Coefficient (<InlineMath math="r" />)** that we learned about earlier.

<BlockMath math="r^2 = (r)^2" />

Once <InlineMath math="r" /> is known, square it to obtain <InlineMath math="r^2" />.

For a defined correlation coefficient, <InlineMath math="-1 \le r \le 1" />. Its square therefore lies between zero and one:

<BlockMath math="0 \le r^2 \le 1" />

**Mathematically (using Sum of Squares):**

The value of <InlineMath math="r^2" /> can also be calculated directly using the Sum of Squares values used to calculate <InlineMath math="r" />:

<BlockMath math="r^2 = \frac{(SS_{xy})^2}{SS_{xx} SS_{yy}}" />

This ratio requires variation in both variables, so <InlineMath math="SS_{xx}>0" /> and <InlineMath math="SS_{yy}>0" />. If every <InlineMath math="y" /> value is identical, the total variation is zero and the usual coefficient-of-determination ratio is undefined.

## Interpretation as a Percentage

The value of <InlineMath math="r^2" /> is often converted into a percentage (by multiplying by <InlineMath math="100" />) for easier interpretation.

- If <InlineMath math="r^2 = 0.81" />, the fitted linear model using <InlineMath math="X" /> accounts for **<InlineMath math="81\%" />** of the observed variation in <InlineMath math="Y" />.
- The remaining variation (<InlineMath math="1 - r^2" /> or <InlineMath math="19\%" /> in this example) is **not explained by this linear model**. It may involve omitted variables, a nonlinear pattern, measurement error, or ordinary random variation, but <InlineMath math="r^2" /> alone cannot tell us which explanation is responsible.

A higher <InlineMath math="r^2" /> means that this model accounts for more of the observed variation in <InlineMath math="Y" />. It does not by itself prove causation or guarantee reliable predictions. We must also inspect residuals, the data range, and whether a linear model makes sense in context.
