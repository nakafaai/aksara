## What Is Product Moment Correlation?

Product moment correlation, often called **Pearson correlation** and denoted by **<InlineMath math="r" />**, is a common statistical measure of the direction and strength of the **linear relationship** between two quantitative variables.

The value of <InlineMath math="r" /> tells us whether the two variables tend to move in the same direction (positive), opposite directions (negative), or if there is no linear relationship at all.

## How Is the Correlation Coefficient Calculated?

The Pearson correlation coefficient (<InlineMath math="r" />) compares the paired deviations of two variables, <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" />, with the variation in each variable.

The calculation has three parts:

1.  **Individual Variation:**

    Each variable (<InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" />) has its own variability. Some values fluctuate a lot (large variation), while others are stable (small variation). This is measured by <InlineMath math="SS_{xx}" /> for <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="SS_{yy}" /> for <InlineMath math="Y" /> (formulas below).

2.  **Joint Variation (Cross-deviation):**

    We also need to know how <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" /> vary _together_. When <InlineMath math="X" /> increases, does <InlineMath math="Y" /> also tend to increase or decrease? The sum <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> measures these paired cross-deviations. It is the numerator used to calculate covariance; covariance additionally divides by <InlineMath math="n" /> or <InlineMath math="n-1" />, depending on the convention.

    - If <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> is positive, paired deviations tend to have the same sign.
    - If <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> is negative, paired deviations tend to have opposite signs.
    - If <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> is close to zero, there is little net linear co-variation.

3.  **Standardizing the Measure:**

    The value of <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> is heavily influenced by the units of the data. For example, the cross-deviation sum for height and weight changes if height is measured in meters instead of centimeters, even though the underlying relationship is unchanged.

    To overcome this, we **standardize** the cross-deviation sum by dividing <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> by the geometric combination of the two individual variation measures, <InlineMath math="\sqrt{SS_{xx} SS_{yy}}" />.

    <BlockMath math="r = \frac{\text{How much X and Y vary together}}{\text{Standardized measure of individual X and Y variations}} = \frac{SS_{xy}}{\sqrt{SS_{xx} SS_{yy}}}" />

The result of this division is **<InlineMath math="r" />**, the Pearson correlation coefficient. Provided both variables have nonzero variation, standardization constrains its value to the interval from <InlineMath math="-1" /> to <InlineMath math="+1" />, regardless of the original units. This allows us to compare linear associations across different pairs of variables.

Thus, <InlineMath math="r" /> compares the paired deviations of <InlineMath math="X" /> and <InlineMath math="Y" /> with the separate variation in each variable.

## Product Moment Correlation Formula

To calculate the value of <InlineMath math="r" /> precisely, we use formulas involving the **Sum of Squares**:

<BlockMath math="r = \frac{SS_{xy}}{\sqrt{SS_{xx} SS_{yy}}}" />

**What are <InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" />, <InlineMath math="SS_{xx}" />, and <InlineMath math="SS_{yy}" />?**

These measure how varied our data is:

1.  **<InlineMath math="SS_{xx}" /> (Sum of Squares for <InlineMath math="x" />):** Measures how spread out the <InlineMath math="x" /> data is from its mean.

    <BlockMath math="SS_{xx} = \sum (x - \bar{x})^2 = \sum x^2 - \frac{(\sum x)^2}{n}" />

2.  **<InlineMath math="SS_{yy}" /> (Sum of Squares for <InlineMath math="y" />):** Measures how spread out the <InlineMath math="y" /> data is from its mean.

    <BlockMath math="SS_{yy} = \sum (y - \bar{y})^2 = \sum y^2 - \frac{(\sum y)^2}{n}" />

3.  **<InlineMath math="SS_{xy}" /> (Sum of Products of deviations for <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />):** Measures how <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> vary _together_.

    <BlockMath math="SS_{xy} = \sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y}) = \sum xy - \frac{(\sum x)(\sum y)}{n}" />

Key:

- <InlineMath math="n" />: Number of data pairs <InlineMath math="(x, y)" />.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x" />, <InlineMath math="\sum y" />: Sum of all <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />
  values.
- <InlineMath math="\sum x^2" />, <InlineMath math="\sum y^2" />: Sum of the squares
  of each <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> value.
- <InlineMath math="\sum xy" />: Sum of the product of each <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> pair.
- <InlineMath math="\bar{x}" />, <InlineMath math="\bar{y}" />: Mean of <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" /> values.

By calculating these three <InlineMath math="SS" /> values and plugging them into the formula for <InlineMath math="r" />, we get the Product Moment Correlation Coefficient.

The formula requires <InlineMath math="SS_{xx}>0" /> and <InlineMath math="SS_{yy}>0" />. If either variable is constant, the denominator is zero and Pearson's <InlineMath math="r" /> is undefined.

## Interpreting the Correlation Coefficient

Once we have the value of <InlineMath math="r" />, we can use the following rough descriptive guidelines. These cutoffs are conventions rather than universal laws. Their practical meaning depends on the field, measurement quality, sample size, and context, so always inspect the scatter plot as well.

| Value of <InlineMath math="r" />      | Correlation Strength  | Description                                        |
| :------------------------------------ | :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="1" />               | Perfect Positive      | All points lie exactly on an upward sloping line.  |
| <InlineMath math="0.7 \le r < 1" />   | Strong Positive       | Clear and strong positive linear relationship.     |
| <InlineMath math="0.3 < r < 0.7" />   | Moderate Positive     | Moderately visible positive linear relationship.   |
| <InlineMath math="0 < r \le 0.3" />   | Weak Positive         | Very low positive linear relationship.             |
| <InlineMath math="0" />               | No Linear Correlation | No linear pattern; a nonlinear relationship may still exist. |
| <InlineMath math="-0.3 \le r < 0" />  | Weak Negative         | Very low negative linear relationship.             |
| <InlineMath math="-0.7 < r < -0.3" /> | Moderate Negative     | Moderately visible negative linear relationship.   |
| <InlineMath math="-1 < r \le -0.7" /> | Strong Negative       | Clear and strong negative linear relationship.     |
| <InlineMath math="-1" />              | Perfect Negative      | All points lie exactly on a downward sloping line. |
