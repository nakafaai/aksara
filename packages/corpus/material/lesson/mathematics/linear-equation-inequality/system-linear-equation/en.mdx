## Values That Satisfy Several Equations at Once

A total price can be produced by many different item combinations. Additional information, such as the total number of items, narrows those possibilities. A linear equation system writes each piece of information as an equation. Its solution <Highlight>must satisfy every equation at once</Highlight>.

### Linear Equation System

A linear equation system is a collection of two or more linear equations that must be **satisfied simultaneously**. Each linear equation has the form:

<BlockMath math="a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \ldots + a_nx_n = b" />

Here <InlineMath math="a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n" /> are coefficients, <InlineMath math="x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n" /> are variables, and <InlineMath math="b" /> is a constant.

### Two Variable Linear Equation Systems

A system with two variables, usually <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />, can contain any number of equations. A system with two equations has the form:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} a_1x + b_1y = c_1 \\ a_2x + b_2y = c_2 \end{cases}" />

**Example**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 21 & \ldots (1) \\ x + y = 10 & \ldots (2) \end{cases}" />

The solution to this system is the pair of values <InlineMath math="(x,y)" /> that satisfies both equations.

### Three Variable Linear Equation Systems

A system may contain any number of equations. To determine unique values for <InlineMath math="x,y,z" />, however, the equations must provide three independent constraints. A common square form is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} a_1x + b_1y + c_1z = d_1 \\ a_2x + b_2y + c_2z = d_2 \\ a_3x + b_3y + c_3z = d_3 \end{cases}" />

**Example**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} x + 2y + 3z = 27 \\ x + y + z = 16 \\ x = 6 \end{cases}" />

## Solving Linear Equation Systems

A linear equation system is solved by **removing one variable** at a time until a single value remains. Substitution and elimination each remove one variable, and both routes work on the same kind of system. Compare at the end when each one is the shorter route.

### Substitution Method

In the substitution method, express **one variable in terms of another**, then insert that expression into the other equation. The following example shows each step.

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 21 & \ldots (1) \\ x + y = 10 & \ldots (2) \end{cases}" />

**Step 1**: Express one variable from the simpler equation.

From equation (2): <InlineMath math="x + y = 10" />, we express <InlineMath math="x" /> in terms of <InlineMath math="y" />:

<BlockMath math="x = 10 - y \ldots (3)" />

**Step 2**: Substitute into the other equation.

Insert equation (3) into equation (1):

<BlockMath math="2(10 - y) + 3y = 21" />

**Step 3**: Solve the resulting equation.

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="20 - 2y + 3y = 21" />
  <BlockMath math="20 + y = 21" />
  <BlockMath math="y = 1 \ldots (4)" />
</MathContainer>

**Step 4**: Substitute the calculated value to find the other variable.

Substitute the value <InlineMath math="y = 1" /> from equation (4) into equation (3):

<BlockMath math="x = 10 - y = 10 - 1 = 9" />

The solution is <InlineMath math="x = 9" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 1" />.

### Elimination Method

In the elimination method, add or subtract two equations to **remove one variable**. The following calculation solves the same example.

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 21 & \ldots (1) \\ x + y = 10 & \ldots (2) \end{cases}" />

**Step 1**: **Match the coefficients** of one variable.

Multiply equation (2) by <InlineMath math="2" /> to match the coefficient of <InlineMath math="x" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 21 & \ldots (1) \\ 2x + 2y = 20 & \ldots (3) \end{cases}" />

**Step 2**: Eliminate the variable by subtracting the equations.

Subtract equation (3) from equation (1):

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="(2x + 3y) - (2x + 2y) = 21 - 20" />
  <BlockMath math="y = 1 \ldots (4)" />
</MathContainer>

**Step 3**: Use the value of <InlineMath math="y" /> to find <InlineMath math="x" />.

Substitute the value <InlineMath math="y = 1" /> from equation (4) into equation (2):

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="x + 1 = 10" />
  <BlockMath math="x = 9" />
</MathContainer>

The solution is <InlineMath math="x = 9" /> and <InlineMath math="y = 1" />.

Verify:

- Equation (1): <InlineMath math="2(9) + 3(1) = 18 + 3 = 21" /> ✓
- Equation (2): <InlineMath math="9 + 1 = 10" /> ✓

## Check Your Understanding

Find the **pair that satisfies both equations**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 3x+2y=16 \\ x-y=2 \end{cases}" />

### Worked Solution

The second equation gives <InlineMath math="x=y+2" />. **Substitute this expression** into the first equation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="3(y+2)+2y=16" />
  <BlockMath math="5y+6=16" />
  <BlockMath math="5y=10" />
  <BlockMath math="y=2" />
</MathContainer>

Back-substitute into <InlineMath math="x=y+2" />:

<BlockMath math="x=2+2=4" />

Both original equations confirm the result:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="3(4)+2(2)=16" />
  <BlockMath math="4-2=2" />
</MathContainer>

The solution is <InlineMath math="(x,y)=(4,2)" />.

## Linear Equation Systems in Context

Applied systems start from a word problem. The description has to be **translated into equations** before any method is applied, and the solution has to be checked against the original question.

### Basketball Scoring

In five-on-five basketball, a successful free throw scores one point. A successful shot from the two-point area scores two points, and one from the three-point area scores three.

Wijaya scores 27 points from **16 successful shots**, including six free throws. Missed shots are not counted in this problem. Let <InlineMath math="a" />, <InlineMath math="b" />, and <InlineMath math="c" /> be the numbers of successful one-, two-, and three-point shots respectively. Each variable is a nonnegative integer.

Translate the **total points**, **successful shots**, and **successful free throws** into three equations:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases}a+2b+3c=27\\a+b+c=16\\a=6\end{cases}" />

Substituting <InlineMath math="a = 6" /> into the second equation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="6 + b + c = 16" />
  <BlockMath math="b + c = 10" />
</MathContainer>

Substitute <InlineMath math="a = 6" /> into the first equation:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="6 + 2b + 3c = 27" />
  <BlockMath math="2b + 3c = 21" />
</MathContainer>

From these two equations:

<BlockMath math="\begin{cases} 2b + 3c = 21 \\ b + c = 10 \end{cases}" />

Multiply the second equation by two, then subtract it from the first to **eliminate the two-point shot count**:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}(2b+3c)-2(b+c)&amp;=21-20\\c&amp;=1\\b&amp;=10-1=9\end{aligned}" />

Wijaya made <InlineMath math="6" /> free throws, <InlineMath math="9" /> <InlineMath math="2" />-point shots, and <InlineMath math="1" /> <InlineMath math="3" />-point shot.

## Interpreting Solutions

For two equations that each represent a line, there are three possibilities. Intersecting lines have **one solution**, distinct parallel lines have no solution, and coincident lines have **infinitely many solutions**.

In three dimensions, a linear equation with at least one nonzero variable coefficient represents a plane. The solution set is the **common intersection of all planes**. An intersection of two planes need not satisfy a third plane. For example, <InlineMath math="x=0" /> and <InlineMath math="y=0" /> intersect along the z-axis, but no point on that axis satisfies <InlineMath math="x+y=1" />.

For three or more planes, the common intersection can be a point, a line, a plane, or empty.
