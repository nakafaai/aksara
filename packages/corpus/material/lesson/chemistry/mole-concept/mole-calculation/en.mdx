## Counting Invisible Particles with One Mole

When you buy eggs at a market, nobody counts them one by one into your basket. You ask for a dozen and get twelve at once. Chemists face a far trickier version of the same problem because atoms and molecules are unimaginably tiny, so they bundle particles into a giant counting unit called the **mole**.

One mole of any substance holds **<InlineMath math="6.02 \times 10^{23}" /> particles**, known as the **Avogadro constant**, <InlineMath math="N_A" />. Those particles can be atoms, molecules, or ions. Because lab balances measure mass and syringes measure gas volume, three compact formulas bridge the invisible world of particle counts to measurements you can actually read on the bench:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{N}{N_A}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{m}{M}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{V}{V_m}" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="n" /> is the number of moles, <InlineMath math="N" /> is the number of particles, <InlineMath math="m" /> is the mass in grams, <InlineMath math="M" /> is the molar mass in <InlineMath math="\text{g mol}^{-1}" />, and <InlineMath math="V_m" /> is the molar volume of a gas.

> One mole always holds the exact same number of particles, even when two different substances weigh completely different amounts on a balance.

Keep that core picture close whenever you convert units. Counting by moles turns a weighing scale into a particle counter.

## Relative Atomic Mass and Relative Molecular Mass

Because a single atom is far too light for any scale, chemists compare atomic masses against one reference standard. The **relative atomic mass**, <InlineMath math="A_r" />, is the average mass of one atom of an element compared with <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{12}" /> of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. Because most elements occur naturally as a mix of isotopes, <InlineMath math="A_r" /> is a weighted average across all of them. Chlorine, for instance, is roughly <InlineMath math="75\%" /> chlorine-35 and <InlineMath math="25\%" /> chlorine-37:

<BlockMath math="A_r(\mathrm{Cl}) = \frac{75 \times 35 + 25 \times 37}{100} = 35.5" />

Once you know the atomic masses, finding the mass of a whole molecule is straightforward addition. The **relative molecular mass**, <InlineMath math="M_r" />, is the sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom in one molecule. For water, adding two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom gives:

<BlockMath math="M_r(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 2(1) + 16 = 18" />

For an ionic compound such as calcium carbonate, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaCO_3}" />, the same sum is called the **relative formula mass**. Suppose you weigh out <InlineMath math="25\ \text{g}" /> of calcium carbonate using <InlineMath math="A_r" /> values of <InlineMath math="40" /> for calcium, <InlineMath math="12" /> for carbon, and <InlineMath math="16" /> for oxygen. Add the formula mass first, then divide to find the moles:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="M_r(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{25}{100} = 0.25\ \text{mol}" />
</MathContainer>

Breaking the formula apart atom by atom prevents missed subscripts like the three oxygen atoms in carbonate.

## Molar Mass and Particle Counts

The **molar mass**, <InlineMath math="M" />, is the mass of one mole of a substance in grams, numerically equal to its <InlineMath math="A_r" /> or <InlineMath math="M_r" />. One mole of carbon weighs <InlineMath math="12\ \text{g}" />, one mole of water weighs <InlineMath math="18\ \text{g}" />, and one mole of carbon dioxide weighs <InlineMath math="44\ \text{g}" />. A classic mistake is assuming equal moles mean equal masses, yet all three samples hold the exact same particle count while carrying very different weights.

### Finding Moles and Atoms from Mass

Suppose you want **the number of moles, molecules, and atoms** in <InlineMath math="9.0\ \text{g}" /> of water. Convert mass to moles first, then multiply by the Avogadro constant for molecules:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{9.0}{18} = 0.50\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="N(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}" />
</MathContainer>

Watch out for the molecules-versus-atoms trap. One water molecule contains three atoms (two hydrogen and one oxygen), so the total number of **atoms** is three times the number of molecules:

<BlockMath math="N(\text{atoms}) = 3 \times 3.01 \times 10^{23} = 9.03 \times 10^{23}" />

Try the same rule on <InlineMath math="0.20\ \text{mol}" /> of ammonia, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" />. Because every ammonia molecule contains four atoms, multiplying by four gives the total atom count directly:

<BlockMath math="N(\text{atoms}) = 0.20 \times 4 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 4.82 \times 10^{23}" />

Always check whether a question asks for molecules or individual atoms before finishing your last line.

### Finding Mass from Particle Counts

Now work in the opposite direction. What is the mass of <InlineMath math="1.204 \times 10^{24}" /> magnesium atoms, given <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{Mg}) = 24" />? Convert particles to moles first, then convert **moles to mass**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{1.204 \times 10^{24}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 2.0\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m = nM = 2.0 \times 24 = 48\ \text{g}" />
</MathContainer>

Sanity check the answer quickly: <InlineMath math="48\ \text{g}" /> is exactly twice the molar mass of magnesium, matching two full moles of atoms.

## Molar Volume of Gases at Standard and Room Conditions

Gas molecules zip around with huge empty gaps between them, so the actual size of each molecule barely affects the total space the gas fills. As a result, at the same temperature and pressure, one mole of **any gas** occupies the exact same volume. This constant is the **molar volume**, <InlineMath math="V_m" />: <InlineMath math="22.4\ \text{dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}" /> at standard temperature and pressure (STP) and <InlineMath math="24.0\ \text{dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}" /> at room temperature and pressure (RTP).

## Empirical and Molecular Formulae

The **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound, while the **molecular formula** states the actual number of atoms inside one molecule. Glucose, for instance, has the molecular formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" /> and the reduced empirical formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2O}" />.

To find an empirical formula from lab data, convert every element's mass into moles and divide by the smallest mole value. In a classic crucible experiment, <InlineMath math="2.4\ \text{g}" /> of magnesium ribbon burns in air to form <InlineMath math="4.0\ \text{g}" /> of magnesium oxide. Subtracting the ribbon mass shows that <InlineMath math="1.6\ \text{g}" /> of oxygen combined with the magnesium:

| Element | Magnesium | Oxygen |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mass (g) | <InlineMath math="2.4" /> | <InlineMath math="1.6" /> |
| Number of moles | <InlineMath math="2.4 \div 24 = 0.10" /> | <InlineMath math="1.6 \div 16 = 0.10" /> |
| Simplest ratio | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |

The one-to-one ratio gives the empirical formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MgO}" />. With a formula mass of <InlineMath math="40" />, multiplying <InlineMath math="0.10\ \text{mol}" /> by <InlineMath math="40" /> returns the exact <InlineMath math="4.0\ \text{g}" /> of oxide weighed at the end.

The same table handles percentage composition data. Suppose an unknown compound contains <InlineMath math="40.0\%" /> carbon, <InlineMath math="6.7\%" /> hydrogen, and <InlineMath math="53.3\%" /> oxygen by mass, with a relative molecular mass of <InlineMath math="180" />. Pretend you have <InlineMath math="100\ \text{g}" /> of sample so each percentage becomes grams directly:

| Element | C | H | O |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Number of moles | <InlineMath math="40.0 \div 12 = 3.33" /> | <InlineMath math="6.7 \div 1 = 6.7" /> | <InlineMath math="53.3 \div 16 = 3.33" /> |
| Simplest ratio | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |

That gives the empirical formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2O}" />, whose unit mass is <InlineMath math="30" />. Dividing the molecular mass by the empirical unit mass finds the multiplier:

<BlockMath math="\frac{180}{30} = 6" />

Multiplying every subscript in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2O}" /> by <InlineMath math="6" /> reveals the true molecular formula, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" />, which is glucose.

## Stoichiometry with Balanced Chemical Equations

A balanced chemical equation works like a kitchen recipe written in moles rather than grams. The big numbers in front of each formula give the **mole ratio** linking reactants and products. The most frequent stoichiometry slip is treating those coefficients as a direct mass ratio. **Always route every calculation through moles**: convert your starting quantity to moles, apply the equation's mole ratio, and convert back to the target unit.

### Product Mass from a Balanced Equation

Find **the mass of carbon dioxide** produced when <InlineMath math="8.0\ \text{g}" /> of methane burns completely in oxygen. Start from the balanced equation:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_4} + 2\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Follow the three-step mole bridge cleanly, writing the unit on every line:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = \frac{8.0}{16} = 0.50\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 0.50\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.50 \times 44 = 22\ \text{g}" />
</MathContainer>

Verify your result with mass conservation. Burning <InlineMath math="0.50\ \text{mol}" /> of methane consumes <InlineMath math="1.0\ \text{mol}" /> of oxygen (<InlineMath math="32\ \text{g}" />) and forms <InlineMath math="1.0\ \text{mol}" /> of water (<InlineMath math="18\ \text{g}" />):

<BlockMath math="8 + 32 = 40\ \text{g} = 22 + 18" />

Reactant mass and product mass match down to the gram, confirming every step.

### Gas Volume from a Balanced Equation

Now connect equation ratios to gas volume at room conditions (<InlineMath math="V_m = 24\ \text{dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}" />). First, suppose <InlineMath math="0.10\ \text{mol}" /> of nitrogen reacts completely with hydrogen to form **ammonia gas**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{N_2} + 3\mathrm{H_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 2 \times 0.10 = 0.20\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 0.20 \times 24 = 4.8\ \text{dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

That matches the reaction model directly: every one nitrogen molecule produces two ammonia molecules.

Next, link a weighed metal to a gas volume. Suppose a <InlineMath math="6.5\ \text{g}" /> strip of zinc (<InlineMath math="A_r = 65" />) dissolves completely in excess hydrochloric acid, and you want the volume of hydrogen released at room temperature and pressure. Write the balanced equation and follow the <InlineMath math="1 : 1" /> mole ratio between zinc and hydrogen:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn} + 2\mathrm{HCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnCl_2} + \mathrm{H_2}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Zn}) = \frac{6.5}{65} = 0.10\ \text{mol} = n(\mathrm{H_2})" />
<BlockMath math="V(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0.10 \times 24 = 2.4\ \text{dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

Because hydrochloric acid is present in excess, **the limiting mass of zinc decides the exact volume of hydrogen collected**.
