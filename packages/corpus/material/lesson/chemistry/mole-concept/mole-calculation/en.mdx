## What One Mole Means

The **mole** is the unit for an amount of substance. One mole of a substance contains **<InlineMath math="6.02 \times 10^{23}" /> particles**, the **Avogadro constant**, <InlineMath math="N_A" />. Those particles can be atoms, molecules or ions. Three formulas link the number of moles to things you can measure:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{N}{N_A}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{m}{M}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{V}{V_m}" />
</MathContainer>

Here <InlineMath math="N" /> is the number of particles, <InlineMath math="m" /> is the mass in grams, <InlineMath math="M" /> is the molar mass in <InlineMath math="\text{g mol}^{-1}" />, and <InlineMath math="V_m" /> is the molar volume of a gas. In the models here, you weigh atoms in a mass spectrometer, rotate molecules to read their mass, and turn moles into mass and volume.

Particles are far too small to count one by one, but mass can be weighed in the lab. So chemists weigh a substance first, then convert the mass into moles.

## Relative Atomic Mass and Relative Molecular Mass

The **relative atomic mass**, <InlineMath math="A_r" />, is the average mass of one atom of an element compared with <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1}{12}" /> of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. It's a weighted average over all the element's isotopes. Chlorine, for example, is about <InlineMath math="75\%" /> chlorine-35 and <InlineMath math="25\%" /> chlorine-37:

<BlockMath math="A_r(\mathrm{Cl}) = \frac{75 \times 35 + 25 \times 37}{100} = 35.5" />

A mass spectrometer measures this value. Heavier ions bend less in the magnetic field, and the height of each peak gives the abundance of an isotope. Pick an element in the model and compare the average with the mass of each isotope.

## Molar Mass and Number of Particles

The **molar mass**, <InlineMath math="M" />, is the mass of one mole of a substance. In grams, it equals <InlineMath math="A_r" /> or <InlineMath math="M_r" />. So one mole of water has a mass of <InlineMath math="18\text{ g}" />, one mole of carbon <InlineMath math="12\text{ g}" /> and one mole of carbon dioxide <InlineMath math="44\text{ g}" />, yet all three contain the same number of particles.

### Example Moles from Mass

Find **the number of moles and molecules** in <InlineMath math="9.0\text{ g}" /> of water.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{9.0}{18} = 0.50\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="N = nN_A = 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}" />
</MathContainer>

Each water molecule has three atoms, so the number of atoms is <InlineMath math="3 \times 3.01 \times 10^{23} = 9.03 \times 10^{23}" />. Check with the model: at <InlineMath math="n = 0.5" />, the mass of water reads <InlineMath math="9\text{ g}" />.

### Example Mass from Number of Particles

What's the mass of <InlineMath math="1.204 \times 10^{24}" /> magnesium atoms? Convert particles to moles first, then **moles to mass**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{1.204 \times 10^{24}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 2.0\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m = nM = 2.0 \times 24 = 48\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: <InlineMath math="48\text{ g}" /> is twice the molar mass of magnesium, matching two moles.

## Empirical and Molecular Formulae

The **empirical formula** shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The **molecular formula** shows the actual number of atoms in one molecule. Glucose has the molecular formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" /> and the empirical formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2O}" />.

You find the empirical formula from the mass of each element. In one experiment, <InlineMath math="2.4\text{ g}" /> of magnesium ribbon burns to give <InlineMath math="4.0\text{ g}" /> of magnesium oxide. The mass of oxygen that combined is <InlineMath math="4.0 - 2.4 = 1.6\text{ g}" />. Set the calculation out in a table:

| Element | Magnesium | Oxygen |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mass (g) | <InlineMath math="2.4" /> | <InlineMath math="1.6" /> |
| Number of moles | <InlineMath math="2.4 \div 24 = 0.10" /> | <InlineMath math="1.6 \div 16 = 0.10" /> |
| Simplest ratio | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |

The ratio is one to one, so the empirical formula is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MgO}" />. Check: the formula mass is <InlineMath math="24 + 16 = 40" />, and <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 40 = 4.0\text{ g}" />, the mass of oxide you weighed.

### Example Mass of a Product

Find **the mass of carbon dioxide** produced when <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ g}" /> of methane burns completely.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_4} + 2\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Follow three steps: mass to moles, mole ratio, then moles to mass.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = \frac{8.0}{16} = 0.50\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 0.50\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.50 \times 44 = 22\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the conservation of mass: the oxygen used is <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 32 = 32\text{ g}" /> and the water produced <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 18 = 18\text{ g}" />. The reactants' mass <InlineMath math="8 + 32 = 40\text{ g}" /> equals the products' mass <InlineMath math="22 + 18 = 40\text{ g}" />.

### Example Gas Volume from an Equation

A <InlineMath math="0.10" /> mol sample of nitrogen reacts completely with hydrogen. Find **the volume of ammonia** at room conditions.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{N_2} + 3\mathrm{H_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 2 \times 0.10 = 0.20\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V = 0.20 \times 24 = 4.8\text{ dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

Check the ratio: one nitrogen molecule gives two ammonia molecules, as the reaction model shows.

## Common Mistakes about the Mole Concept

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| One mole of water and one mole of carbon dioxide have the same mass. | They have the same number of particles, but different molar masses. | Compare <InlineMath math="M" />, not <InlineMath math="n" />. |
| Using the equation's coefficients as a mass ratio. | The coefficients are a mole ratio. | Convert mass to moles before using the ratio. |
| Using <InlineMath math="24\text{ dm}^3" /> for a solid or liquid. | Molar volume applies only to gases. | Check the state of the substance. |
| The number of atoms equals the number of molecules. | One molecule can hold several atoms. | Multiply by the number of atoms in the formula. |

The second mistake is the most common one in stoichiometry questions. **Always go through moles**: mass to moles, mole ratio, then moles to whatever quantity you're asked for.

## Practice on the Mole Concept

These questions start with one conversion and finish with formulae and equations. Use <InlineMath math="A_r" />: H <InlineMath math="1" />, C <InlineMath math="12" />, N <InlineMath math="14" />, O <InlineMath math="16" />, Ca <InlineMath math="40" />, Zn <InlineMath math="65" />. **Write the unit at every step**.

1. Calculate the number of atoms in <InlineMath math="0.20" /> mol of ammonia, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" />.

2. Calculate the number of moles in <InlineMath math="25\text{ g}" /> of calcium carbonate, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaCO_3}" />.

3. A compound contains <InlineMath math="40.0\%" /> carbon, <InlineMath math="6.7\%" /> hydrogen and <InlineMath math="53.3\%" /> oxygen by mass. Its relative molecular mass is <InlineMath math="180" />. Find its empirical and molecular formulae.

4. A <InlineMath math="6.5\text{ g}" /> piece of zinc reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume of hydrogen gas at room conditions.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Each <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" /> molecule contains four atoms.

<BlockMath math="N = 0.20 \times 4 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 4.82 \times 10^{23}" />

**Solution 2**: Work out the relative formula mass first, <InlineMath math="40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100" />.

<BlockMath math="n = \frac{25}{100} = 0.25\text{ mol}" />

**Solution 3**: Assume <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" /> of the compound, so the percentages become grams.

| Element | C | H | O |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Number of moles | <InlineMath math="40.0 \div 12 = 3.33" /> | <InlineMath math="6.7 \div 1 = 6.7" /> | <InlineMath math="53.3 \div 16 = 3.33" /> |
| Simplest ratio | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |

The empirical formula is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2O}" /> with a formula mass of <InlineMath math="30" />. Because <InlineMath math="180 \div 30 = 6" />, the molecular formula is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" />, which is glucose.

**Solution 4**: Write the equation, then follow the <InlineMath math="1 : 1" /> mole ratio between zinc and hydrogen.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn} + 2\mathrm{HCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnCl_2} + \mathrm{H_2}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Zn}) = \frac{6.5}{65} = 0.10\text{ mol} = n(\mathrm{H_2})" />
<BlockMath math="V = 0.10 \times 24 = 2.4\text{ dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

The acid is in excess, so **the zinc decides the amount of gas**.
