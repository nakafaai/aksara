## Reactions Release or Absorb Heat

**Thermochemistry** studies the heat changes in chemical reactions. An **exothermic** reaction releases heat to the surroundings, so the temperature of the mixture rises and the heat change, <InlineMath math="\Delta H" />, is negative. An **endothermic** reaction absorbs heat, so the temperature falls and <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is positive. You measure the heat with <InlineMath math="Q = mc\theta" />, then <InlineMath math="\Delta H = -\dfrac{Q}{n}" /> per mole. In these notes you change the heat change on an energy diagram, heat water in a kettle, and calculate the heats of neutralisation, displacement and combustion.

The heat comes from **chemical bonds**. Breaking bonds in the reactants **absorbs** energy, and forming new bonds in the products **releases** energy. If more energy is released than absorbed, the reaction is exothermic.

Move the <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> slider below. When <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is negative, the products sit lower than the reactants. When <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is positive, the products sit higher.

You already know both kinds. Burning a fuel and neutralising an acid are exothermic. Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water is endothermic, and that's how an instant cold pack for sports injuries works.

## Measuring Heat through a Temperature Change

The heat released by a reaction in solution is absorbed by the water in that solution. You calculate it with:

<BlockMath math="Q = mc\theta" />

Here <InlineMath math="m" /> is the mass of the solution in grams, <InlineMath math="c = 4.2\text{ J g}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" /> is the **specific heat capacity of water**, and <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the temperature change. An aqueous solution is taken to have a density of <InlineMath math="1\text{ g cm}^{-3}" />, so <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> of solution has a mass of <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" />.

The same formula heats water in an electric kettle. Pick a mass of water below: twice the mass needs twice the energy for the same rise in temperature.

## Heat of Neutralisation and Heat of Displacement

The **heat of neutralisation** is the heat change when <InlineMath math="1" /> mole of water forms from an acid and an alkali. For a strong acid and a strong alkali it's nearly the same every time, about <InlineMath math="-57.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />, because the actual reaction is always <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{OH^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}" />.

Try an example. <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid is mixed with <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide. The temperature rises by <InlineMath math="6.8^\circ\text{C}" />. Calculate the heat of neutralisation.

**Work out the heat first, then divide by the moles of water**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 100 \times 4.2 \times 6.8 = 2856\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 1.0 \times \frac{50}{1000} = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{2856}{0.050} = -57\,120\text{ J mol}^{-1} \approx -57.1\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: the answer is close to the standard value of <InlineMath math="-57.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />, and the sign is negative because the temperature rose.

The temperature rise depends on the **concentration**, not the volume. If you double both volumes, the moles of water and the mass of solution both double, so <InlineMath math="\theta" /> stays the same. Change the concentration of the acid and alkali below.

The **heat of displacement** is the heat change when <InlineMath math="1" /> mole of a metal is displaced from a solution of its salt. Excess zinc powder is added to <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> copper(II) sulfate solution, and the temperature rises by <InlineMath math="10.0^\circ\text{C}" />. The copper(II) ion is the limiting reactant, so take the moles from it:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 50 \times 4.2 \times 10.0 = 2100\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}) = 0.20 \times \frac{50}{1000} = 0.010\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{2100}{0.010} = -210\,000\text{ J mol}^{-1} = -210\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the electrochemical series: zinc and copper sit far apart in it, so the reaction is strongly exothermic. <InlineMath math="-210\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" /> is much bigger than the heat of neutralisation.

## Heat of Combustion and Fuel Value

The **heat of combustion** is the heat released when <InlineMath math="1" /> mole of a substance burns completely in excess oxygen. Burning methane breaks <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{-}H}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O{=}O}" /> bonds, then forms stronger <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}O}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O{-}H}" /> bonds. That's why combustion releases so much heat.

In the lab, the heat of combustion of an alcohol is measured by heating water in a copper can. <InlineMath math="0.46\text{ g}" /> of ethanol, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}" />, is burned and raises the temperature of <InlineMath math="200\text{ cm}^3" /> of water by <InlineMath math="12.0^\circ\text{C}" />. The molar mass of ethanol is <InlineMath math="46\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 200 \times 4.2 \times 12.0 = 10\,080\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{0.46}{46} = 0.010\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{10\,080}{0.010} = -1\,008\,000\text{ J mol}^{-1} \approx -1008\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />

Check against the standard value, <InlineMath math="-1376\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />. The lab answer is smaller because **heat is lost** to the air and the can, and some ethanol burns incompletely.

The heat of combustion of alcohols grows with the number of carbon atoms. The standard values for methanol to butan-1-ol are <InlineMath math="726" />, <InlineMath math="1376" />, <InlineMath math="2021" /> and <InlineMath math="2676\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />. Each extra <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2}" /> group adds about <InlineMath math="650\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />, because one more carbon atom and two more hydrogen atoms burn. The **fuel value** is the energy released by <InlineMath math="1\text{ g}" /> of a fuel, in <InlineMath math="\text{kJ g}^{-1}" />. People choose a fuel by this value together with its cost, availability and the effect its combustion has on the environment.

## Common Mistakes about Thermochemistry

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The temperature rose, so <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is positive. | A temperature rise means heat was released, so <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is negative. | An exothermic reaction always has a negative sign. |
| The mass in <InlineMath math="Q = mc\theta" /> is the mass of the reactants. | It's the mass of the solution or water that absorbs the heat. | Use the total volume of solution in <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^3" />. |
| Doubling the volumes of acid and alkali doubles the temperature rise. | The moles of water and the mass of solution both double, so <InlineMath math="\theta" /> stays the same. | Only the concentration changes <InlineMath math="\theta" />. |
| In a displacement, the moles come from the excess zinc. | An excess reactant doesn't react completely, so take the moles from the limiting reactant. | Find the substance that gets used up. |
| Breaking bonds releases energy. | Breaking bonds absorbs energy, and forming bonds releases it. | Picture the energy needed to pull two atoms apart. |

The first mistake turns up most often in objective questions. Keep the chain in mind: **temperature up, heat out, <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> negative**.

## Practice on Thermochemistry

These questions move from heat calculations to comparing fuels and explaining results. **Write the unit at every step** so joules and kilojoules don't get mixed up.

1. <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> silver nitrate solution is mixed with <InlineMath math="25\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium chloride solution. The temperature rises by <InlineMath math="3.5^\circ\text{C}" />. Calculate the heat of precipitation of silver chloride.

2. The heat of combustion of methane is <InlineMath math="-890\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" /> and of butane <InlineMath math="-2877\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />. Calculate the fuel value of each and state which is higher. The molar mass of methane is <InlineMath math="16\text{ g mol}^{-1}" /> and of butane <InlineMath math="58\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />.

3. The heat of neutralisation of ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide is less than <InlineMath math="57.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />. Explain why.

4. When ammonium nitrate dissolves in water, the temperature of the solution falls. State the type of heat change and the sign of <InlineMath math="\Delta H" />, and describe the energy level of the products compared with the reactants.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: The total volume is <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" />, so the mass of solution is <InlineMath math="50\text{ g}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 50 \times 4.2 \times 3.5 = 735\text{ J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{AgCl}) = 0.50 \times \frac{25}{1000} = 0.0125\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{735}{0.0125} = -58\,800\text{ J mol}^{-1} = -58.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check: both solutions hold the same number of moles, so neither is in excess, and the negative sign matches the rising temperature.

**Solution 2**: Divide each heat of combustion by the molar mass:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Methane: } \frac{890}{16} = 55.6\text{ kJ g}^{-1}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Butane: } \frac{2877}{58} = 49.6\text{ kJ g}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

**Methane has the higher fuel value**. Butane releases more heat per mole, but each mole of butane is also much heavier. Check: <InlineMath math="55.6 \times 16 = 890" />, so the ratio is right.

**Solution 3**: Ethanoic acid is a **weak acid** that ionises only partly. During neutralisation, ethanoic acid molecules have to ionise to release <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions, and that ionisation **absorbs some of the heat**. So the net heat released per mole of water is less than <InlineMath math="57.3\text{ kJ}" />.

**Solution 4**: A falling temperature means heat is absorbed from the surroundings, so the dissolving is **endothermic** and <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is **positive**. On an energy level diagram, the products sit higher than the reactants, and the gap is the heat absorbed.
