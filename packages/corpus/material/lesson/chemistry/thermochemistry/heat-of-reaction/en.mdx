## Reactions Release or Absorb Heat

Hold your hands near a campfire and your palms warm up right away. Press an instant cold pack onto a bruised knee and the pouch turns icy in seconds. Both everyday moments happen because **thermochemistry** tracks how heat moves whenever substances react.

An **exothermic** reaction pushes heat out into its surroundings, so the mixture warms up and its enthalpy change, <InlineMath math="\Delta H" />, is negative. An **endothermic** reaction pulls heat in from its surroundings, so the mixture cools down and <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> is positive.

Every reaction transfers heat because of **chemical bonds**. Pulling old bonds apart in the reactants always **absorbs** energy, while snapping new bonds together in the products **releases** energy. Separating two bonded atoms takes effort just like prying apart two strong magnets, so a reaction turns exothermic whenever new bonds return more energy than old bonds cost.

Slide the <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> control below to see how an energy profile reflects this balance. Negative values drop the products below the reactants, while positive values lift the products higher.

Notice that the middle hump never disappears, even for strongly exothermic reactions, because every reaction has to loosen existing bonds first. The vertical gap between start and finish is <InlineMath math="\Delta H" />.

> When the thermometer climbs, the reaction has pushed heat out into the water, so the enthalpy change is always negative.

Try a quick mental check with an instant cold pack. When solid ammonium nitrate dissolves in water, the solution turns chilly because the dissolving process pulls heat from the surrounding water. That cooling tells you the change is **endothermic**, <InlineMath math="\Delta H" /> carries a **positive** sign, and the products rest higher than the reactants on an energy level diagram.

## Measuring Heat through a Temperature Change

To turn a thermometer reading into joules, think of the water in the cup as a sponge soaking up every bit of heat from the reaction. You find the heat transferred, <InlineMath math="Q" />, from the mass of the solution, its specific heat capacity, and its temperature change:

<BlockMath math="Q = mc\theta" />

Once you have <InlineMath math="Q" />, you divide by the number of moles reacted, <InlineMath math="n" />, to report the **molar enthalpy change** per mole:

<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{Q}{n}" />

Here <InlineMath math="m" /> is the **mass of the solution** in grams, <InlineMath math="c = 4.2\ \text{J g}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}" /> is the **specific heat capacity of water**, and <InlineMath math="\theta" /> is the temperature change. Aqueous solutions have a density close to <InlineMath math="1\ \text{g cm}^{-3}" />, so <InlineMath math="100\ \text{cm}^3" /> of solution counts as <InlineMath math="100\ \text{g}" />. Be careful not to plug in the mass of a dry solid reactant for <InlineMath math="m" />, because the water in the solution is what actually warms up or cools down.

Look closely at the sign flip when you switch from the first three reactions to dissolution. Adding more moles creates a larger temperature swing, yet the **molar enthalpy** per mole stays characteristic of each reaction.

One sign trap catches students on nearly every thermochemistry quiz: seeing a rising temperature and writing a positive <InlineMath math="\Delta H" />. Keep the simple sequence **temperature up, heat out, negative enthalpy** firmly linked in your mind, and always write units at each line so joules never masquerade as kilojoules.

Put that routine to work on a **heat of precipitation** problem. Suppose <InlineMath math="25\ \text{cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.50\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}" /> silver nitrate solution mixes with <InlineMath math="25\ \text{cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.50\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium chloride solution, and the thermometer rises by <InlineMath math="3.5^\circ\text{C}" />. The combined solution has a volume of <InlineMath math="50\ \text{cm}^3" />, giving a mass of <InlineMath math="50\ \text{g}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 50 \times 4.2 \times 3.5 = 735\ \text{J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{AgCl}) = 0.50 \times \frac{25}{1000} = 0.0125\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{735}{0.0125} = -58\,800\ \text{J mol}^{-1} = -58.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Both solutions supply the exact same number of moles, so neither reactant is left over, and the negative sign matches the warming mixture.

## Heat Changes in Neutralisation and Displacement

Two of the most frequent calorimeter experiments happen directly in aqueous solution: **neutralising an acid with an alkali** and **displacing a metal from its salt**. Both use the exact same three-step calculation, yet each hides its own subtle trap around concentration, weak acids, and excess reactants.

### Neutralisation Enthalpy and Concentration

The **heat of neutralisation** is the enthalpy change when <InlineMath math="1\ \text{mol}" /> of water forms from an acid and an alkali. Pairing any strong acid with any strong alkali yields almost the same value, about <InlineMath math="-57.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />, because the real chemical change is always the same net ionic reaction:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{OH^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}" />

Work through a lab reading. Mixing <InlineMath math="50\ \text{cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="1.0\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid with <InlineMath math="50\ \text{cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="1.0\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide raises the temperature by <InlineMath math="6.8^\circ\text{C}" />. Find the heat absorbed by the <InlineMath math="100\ \text{g}" /> mixture first, then divide by the moles of water formed:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 100 \times 4.2 \times 6.8 = 2856\ \text{J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 1.0 \times \frac{50}{1000} = 0.050\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{2856}{0.050} = -57\,120\ \text{J mol}^{-1} \approx -57.1\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

That answer sits right beside the standard <InlineMath math="-57.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" /> value, with the negative sign confirming an exothermic reaction.

What happens if you double both volumes while keeping the concentration fixed? Many learners expect the temperature rise to double, yet it stays unchanged because doubling the moles of water also doubles the mass of water that must be heated. Swapping hydrochloric acid for **ethanoic acid**, a **weak acid**, produces a heat of neutralisation with sodium hydroxide smaller than <InlineMath math="57.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" /> because ethanoic acid ionises only partially in water. During neutralisation, intact acid molecules must ionise to release <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions, and that extra ionisation step **absorbs part of the heat**, leaving less net heat released per mole of water.

### Metal Displacement and Limiting Moles

The **heat of displacement** is the enthalpy change when <InlineMath math="1\ \text{mol}" /> of a metal is pushed out of its salt solution by a more reactive metal. Suppose excess zinc powder is stirred into <InlineMath math="50\ \text{cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.20\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}" /> copper(II) sulfate solution, raising the temperature by <InlineMath math="10.0^\circ\text{C}" />.

Here is the trap to dodge: never calculate moles from the **excess zinc**, because leftover zinc sits unreacted at the bottom of the cup. Always compute moles from the **limiting reactant**, which is the copper(II) ion that runs out completely:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 50 \times 4.2 \times 10.0 = 2100\ \text{J}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}) = 0.20 \times \frac{50}{1000} = 0.010\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{2100}{0.010} = -210\,000\ \text{J mol}^{-1} = -210\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Checking the electrochemical series confirms why this figure is so large. Zinc sits far above copper, so the electron transfer releases far more energy per mole than an acid-alkali neutralisation.

## Heat of Combustion and Fuel Value

Burning a fuel in air releases dramatically more heat than aqueous reactions because strong oxygen-rich bonds form in carbon dioxide and water. Measuring **heat of combustion** per mole shows how molecular size drives energy release, while **fuel value** per gram reveals which fuel carries the most punch in a portable tank.

### Bond Energy and Alcohol Combustion

The **heat of combustion** is the heat released when <InlineMath math="1\ \text{mol}" /> of a substance burns completely in excess oxygen. When methane burns, breaking <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{-}H}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O{=}O}" /> bonds takes energy, while forming much stronger <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}O}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O{-}H}" /> bonds pays back far more energy in return.

In the lab, you estimate an alcohol's heat of combustion by burning a spirit lamp under a copper can of water. Suppose burning <InlineMath math="0.46\ \text{g}" /> of ethanol (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}" />, molar mass <InlineMath math="46\ \text{g mol}^{-1}" />) warms <InlineMath math="200\ \text{cm}^3" /> of water by <InlineMath math="12.0^\circ\text{C}" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q = 200 \times 4.2 \times 12.0 = 10\,080\ \text{J}" />
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{0.46}{46} = 0.010\ \text{mol}" />
<BlockMath math="\Delta H = -\frac{10\,080}{0.010} = -1\,008\,000\ \text{J mol}^{-1} \approx -1008\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Compare that reading with the standard value of <InlineMath math="-1376\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />. The lab figure always comes out smaller because **heat escapes** into the air and the copper can, and some ethanol burns incompletely into soot.

Across straight-chain alcohols from methanol to butan-1-ol, standard combustion heats climb steadily through <InlineMath math="726" />, <InlineMath math="1376" />, <InlineMath math="2021" />, and <InlineMath math="2676\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />. Every added <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2}" /> link supplies one extra carbon atom and two extra hydrogen atoms to burn, adding roughly <InlineMath math="650\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />. ### Fuel Value and Practical Fuel Selection

Molar enthalpy compares fuels mole by mole, yet rockets, camping stoves, and car tanks care about weight. The **fuel value** measures the heat released when <InlineMath math="1\ \text{g}" /> of fuel burns completely, expressed in <InlineMath math="\text{kJ g}^{-1}" />.

Compare methane (<InlineMath math="\Delta H = -890\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />, molar mass <InlineMath math="16\ \text{g mol}^{-1}" />) against butane (<InlineMath math="\Delta H = -2877\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}" />, molar mass <InlineMath math="58\ \text{g mol}^{-1}" />). Divide each molar combustion heat by its molar mass:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\text{Methane: } \frac{890}{16} = 55.6\ \text{kJ g}^{-1}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{Butane: } \frac{2877}{58} = 49.6\ \text{kJ g}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Even though butane releases more than triple the heat per mole, **methane has the higher fuel value** because each mole of butane weighs nearly four times as much. Multiplying <InlineMath math="55.6" /> back by <InlineMath math="16" /> returns <InlineMath math="890" />, confirming the division. In real engineering choices, people weigh **fuel value** alongside cost, safe storage, steady supply, and cleaner exhaust.
