## Everyday Chemistry in Industry and Home

**Consumer and industrial chemistry** links molecular structure directly to everyday materials such as cooking oil, soap, packaged food, medicines, industrial fertilisers, and nanomaterials. Oils and fats are **esters** formed from glycerol and fatty acids, whereas soap is produced by alkaline hydrolysis of those esters in **saponification**. At the nanoscale (<InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="100\text{ nm}" />), shrinking a particle dramatically expands its surface area relative to its volume and changes how fast it reacts.

> Molecular shape, solubility, and particle size govern how everyday products clean, preserve, and react.

Across each household or industrial process, track which bonds break or form and how intermolecular forces respond to temperature, pressure, or dissolved ions.

## Oils Fats and Hydrogenation

Animal **fats** consist mainly of **saturated** fatty acids with only <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{-}C}" /> single bonds, so their straight chains pack closely and stay solid at room temperature. Vegetable **oils** contain mainly **unsaturated** fatty acids carrying <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> double bonds. Do not confuse the two states by calling vegetable oil more saturated than animal fat. Each double bond introduces a kink in the carbon chain that prevents tight packing and lowers the melting point, keeping vegetable oil liquid.

Food makers convert liquid vegetable oil into spreadable **margarine** through **hydrogenation**. Hydrogen gas bubbles through hot oil at roughly <InlineMath math="200^\circ\text{C}" /> over a finely divided nickel catalyst, adding hydrogen across the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> double bonds. Removing double bonds straightens the chains, raises the melting point, and turns the oil into a soft solid.

## Soap Manufacture and Cleaning Action

Turning fats into cleaning agents involves two connected ideas: **breaking ester bonds** with alkali to manufacture soap salts, and using the resulting amphiphilic ions to suspend grease in water.

### Saponification and Soap Yield

**Saponification** is the alkaline hydrolysis of an oil or fat using hot sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide solution. Be careful not to mix up saponification with esterification. Esterification builds an ester from an alcohol and a carboxylic acid, whereas **saponification breaks an ester** with alkali to produce soap and glycerol:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_{57}H_{110}O_6} + 3\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{C_{17}H_{35}COONa} + \mathrm{C_3H_5(OH)_3}" />

### Micelle Cleaning and Hard Water

Each soap anion carries a negatively charged **hydrophilic head** attracted to water and a long hydrocarbon **hydrophobic tail** attracted to grease. During washing, tails burrow into oily dirt while heads face the surrounding water, lifting grease into suspended droplets called **micelles**. Because every micelle carries a negative outer charge, droplets repel one another and rinse cleanly away.

## Food Additives Medicines and Cosmetics

**Food additives** extend shelf life or improve flavour, colour, and texture, and each class performs a distinct chemical role:

| Type | Job | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Preservative | Slows the growth of microorganisms | Sodium benzoate |
| Antioxidant | Prevents fats from oxidising and turning rancid | Ascorbic acid |
| Flavouring | Adds or enhances taste | Monosodium glutamate |
| Stabiliser and thickener | Maintains smooth texture and viscosity | Gelatin, starch |
| Colouring | Adds or restores colour | Tartrazine |

Modern **medicines** target specific biological mechanisms. **Analgesics** such as paracetamol relieve pain. **Antibiotics** such as penicillin kill bacteria, not viruses, and you must **finish the entire prescribed course** rather than stopping early once symptoms fade so resistant bacteria do not survive and multiply. **Cosmetics** span skincare, make-up, and fragrance products and must be applied strictly according to label directions.

## Industrial Ammonia Synthesis and Catalysts

## Nanotechnology and Green Technology

<BlockMath math="\frac{\text{surface area}}{\text{volume}} = \frac{6L^2}{L^3} = \frac{6}{L}" />

## Applying Consumer and Industrial Chemistry

Solve these four problems by **writing the balanced equation or geometric formula first**, then compare your working with the solutions directly beneath.

1. Explain why liquid vegetable oil is hydrogenated to manufacture margarine.

2. Explain why synthetic detergent cleans more effectively than soap in hard water.

3. Calculate the mass of soap produced from <InlineMath math="445\text{ g}" /> of glyceryl tristearate.

4. Compare the surface-area-to-volume ratio of a cube with edge <InlineMath math="20\text{ nm}" /> to a cube with edge <InlineMath math="2\ \mu\text{m}" />.

**Answer 1**: Vegetable oil stays liquid because its hydrocarbon chains are **unsaturated** with bent <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> double bonds. Hydrogenation over a nickel catalyst adds hydrogen across those double bonds, making the chains **more saturated** and straighter so molecules pack closely, raising the melting point into soft solid margarine.

**Answer 2**: In hard water, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}" /> ions react with soap anions to form insoluble **scum**, wasting soap. The calcium and magnesium salts of synthetic detergents **dissolve in water**, so no scum precipitates and detergent anions keep lifting dirt.

**Answer 3**: Find the moles of fat first, then apply the <InlineMath math="1 : 3" /> ratio:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\text{fat}) = \frac{445}{890} = 0.50\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m(\text{soap}) = 3 \times 0.50 \times 306 = 459\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

**Answer 4**: Convert both lengths to nanometres first (<InlineMath math="2\ \mu\text{m} = 2000\text{ nm}" />):

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{6}{20} = 0.3\text{ nm}^{-1}" />
<BlockMath math="\frac{6}{2000} = 0.003\text{ nm}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

The nanocube's surface-area-to-volume ratio is **one hundred times larger**, matching its hundredfold smaller edge length.
