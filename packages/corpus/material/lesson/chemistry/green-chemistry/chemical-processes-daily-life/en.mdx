## Identifying Everyday Chemical Changes

Before judging an activity as green or not green, identify <Highlight>what chemical process is happening</Highlight>. Burning waste, washing clothes, making compost, cooking, photosynthesis in plants, and rusting iron all involve **substances changing or interacting**.

[Green chemistry](https://www.epa.gov/greenchemistry/basics-green-chemistry) evaluates how a chemical product or process is designed from the start. Sound design reduces hazards, waste, excess energy demand, single-use materials, and accident risks while cutting the use or generation of hazardous substances.

> Every green assessment starts with the chemistry itself because you cannot cut waste or hazards until you know what enters and leaves the process.

Checking any everyday process means answering four questions, one for each stage of the change.

| Part of the process | Question |
| :------------------ | :------- |
| Starting substances | What substances enter the process? |
| Change | Are the substances only mixed, or do they become new substances? |
| Products | What substances form after the process runs? |
| Impact | Is there waste, excess heat, hazardous gas, or material that is hard to degrade? |

### Tracing Fuel Combustion at Home

A biogas stove burns methane, and a spirit lamp burns ethanol. Both are chemical changes where **the carbon and hydrogen atoms of the fuel move into carbon dioxide and water**. Their balanced equations show how much carbon dioxide each mole of fuel releases:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + 2\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_5OH(l)} + 3\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2O(l)}" />
</MathContainer>

Because one mole of carbon dioxide weighs <InlineMath math="44\text{ g}" />, burning <InlineMath math="n" /> moles of methane produces <InlineMath math="44n\text{ g}" /> of carbon dioxide, whereas burning <InlineMath math="n" /> moles of ethanol produces <InlineMath math="88n\text{ g}" />. ### Sorting Atoms Elements and Molecules

The periodic table identifies each element symbol, yet a symbol alone does not state the particle type. Reading reactions accurately requires distinguishing **an atom, an element, an elemental molecule, and a compound molecule**.

An [atom](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00493) is the smallest particle that still characterizes a chemical element. A [chemical element](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01022) is a species of atoms with the same number of protons. A [molecule](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/M04002) is an electrically neutral entity made of more than one bonded atom.

## Reading and Balancing Chemical Equations

A chemical reaction equation records reactants, products, and their relative amounts. Substances on the left are **reactants**, substances on the right are **products**, the arrow means yields, and numbers placed before formulas are called **coefficients**.

<BlockMath math="\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}" />

State symbols appear right after each chemical formula to show its physical state during the process.

| Symbol | Meaning |
| :----- | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(s)}" /> | solid |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(l)}" /> | liquid |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(g)}" /> | gas |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(aq)}" /> | dissolved in water |

Change only the coefficients when balancing a reaction. Never alter the subscripts inside a formula, because subscripts define the identity of the substance: <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> is water, whereas <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" /> is hydrogen peroxide.

### Balancing the Photosynthesis Summary Equation

Photosynthesis is written as a summary reaction covering many biochemical steps. Plants combine **carbon dioxide and water** using light energy to make sugar and oxygen. An unbalanced draft starts like this:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}" />

Counting atoms reveals that the two sides do not match yet.

| Element | Left | Right |
| :------ | :--- | :---- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> |

Start with the six carbon atoms in one sugar molecule by placing <InlineMath math="6\mathrm{CO_2}" /> on the left, then match the twelve hydrogen atoms with <InlineMath math="6\mathrm{H_2O}" />. The left side now carries <InlineMath math="18" /> oxygen atoms. Six belong in the sugar, leaving <InlineMath math="12" /> oxygen atoms to form <InlineMath math="6\mathrm{O_2}" />:

<BlockMath math="6\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 6\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{O_2(g)}" />

Because this formal summary condenses many intermediate steps, the carbohydrate formula appears without a state symbol. Now both sides hold equal atom counts for every element.

| Element | Left | Right |
| :------ | :--- | :---- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="18" /> | <InlineMath math="18" /> |

To balance any equation, keep every chemical formula intact, <Highlight>adjust only the coefficients</Highlight>, and check that every element matches across both sides. Slide <InlineMath math="n" /> below to scale the number of glucose molecules formed and read the balanced atom counts.

### Conserving Mass When Dry Leaves Burn

Burning dry leaves does not make matter vanish. One repeating cellulose unit reacts completely with oxygen in the air to form **carbon dioxide and water vapor**:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{10}O_5} + 6\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 6\mathrm{CO_2} + 5\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Each <InlineMath math="162\text{ g}" /> of cellulose combines with <InlineMath math="192\text{ g}" /> of oxygen from the air, releasing <InlineMath math="264\text{ g}" /> of carbon dioxide and <InlineMath math="90\text{ g}" /> of water vapor. The total exhaust mass of <InlineMath math="354\text{ g}" /> is more than twice the dry leaf mass alone because atmospheric oxygen joined the products.

## Evaluating Daily Processes with Green Criteria

Once you can read a reaction, judge whether the process fits green chemistry criteria. A process can be useful yet still not green if it **forms pollutants**, relies on unnecessary hazardous substances, or demands excessive energy.

| Everyday process | Chemical change | Green chemistry assessment |
| :--------------- | :-------------- | :------------------------- |
| Burning plastic waste | Organic polymers react with oxygen, releasing heat, smoke, and gaseous products. | Disposal creates additional pollutants and fails to prevent waste at the source. |
| Using cleaner in the right amount | Cleaning agents lift dirt without excessive dosing. | The right dose avoids excess consumption and residue while label hazard rules still apply. |
| Composting food scraps | Microorganisms break organic materials down step by step. | Proper composting cuts organic waste when moisture and airflow limit odor, leachate, and pests. |
| Randomly mixing household cleaners | Substances safe on their own react to form hazardous gas. | Mixing raises accident risk. Combine cleaners only when the label explicitly instructs it. |

Never mix [household bleach or disinfectants](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/cleaning-and-disinfecting-with-bleach.html) with other cleaners, because the mixture can release toxic vapors. Follow the product label and ventilation instructions carefully.

A material's origin alone never proves safety. Examine composition, dose, and use together. Comparing three ways to handle dry yard leaves shows the difference between waste management and green chemistry design.

| Option | What happens | Judgment |
| :----- | :----------- | :------- |
| Burn them | Leaves react with oxygen and produce smoke. | Incompatible with green chemistry because **pollution forms**. |
| Put them in mixed trash | Leaves add to landfill waste that must be handled. | Better than burning, although waste is still generated. |
| Compost them properly | Organic material breaks down slowly into compost. | Avoids burning and returns nutrients to soil, yet composting alone treats existing waste rather than redesigning a chemical process. |

Composting is responsible biological waste treatment, whereas green chemistry redesigns a chemical product or process to prevent harm at the source. To evaluate any everyday process, identify the inputs, the chemical change, the outputs, and the hazards prevented before waste ever forms.
