## Identify the Process First

Before judging an activity as "green" or "not green", we need to know what chemical process is happening. Burning waste, washing clothes, making compost, cooking, photosynthesis in plants, and rusting iron all involve substances changing or interacting.

Green chemistry does not ask whether a process looks natural. It asks whether the process is designed to reduce hazards, waste, excessive energy use, single-use materials, and accident risks from the start. [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/greenchemistry/basics-green-chemistry) summarizes this idea as designing chemical products and processes that reduce the use or generation of hazardous substances.

Analyze the process in this order.

| Part of the process | Question |
| :----------- | :------- |
| Starting substances | What substances enter the process? |
| Change | Are the substances only mixed, or do they become new substances? |
| Products | What substances form after the process runs? |
| Impact | Is there waste, excess heat, hazardous gas, or material that is hard to degrade? |

## Atoms Elements and Molecules

The periodic table identifies each element symbol, but a symbol alone does not state the particle type. We must still distinguish an atom, an element, an elemental molecule, and a compound molecule.

The [IUPAC Gold Book](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00493) defines an atom as the smallest particle that still characterizes a chemical element. In the [IUPAC Gold Book](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01022), a chemical element is a species of atoms with the same number of protons. The [IUPAC Gold Book](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/M04002) defines a molecule as an electrically neutral entity made of more than one atom.

Use the following definitions in this lesson.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :------------- | :------ |
| Atom | One particle of an element. | a <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> atom or an <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> atom |
| Element | A substance whose atomic identity is fixed by proton number. | iron, carbon, oxygen |
| Elemental molecule | A molecule made from atoms of the same element. | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P_4}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{S_8}" /> |
| Compound molecule | A molecule made from atoms of different elements. | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}" /> |

Not every compound exists as separate molecules. Table salt, for example, forms a repeating ionic arrangement. The examples on this page use molecules because we are practicing simple reaction equations.

## Parts of a Reaction Equation

A chemical reaction equation records reactants, products, and their relative amounts. Substances on the left are **reactants**, substances on the right are **products**, the arrow means "yields", and numbers placed before formulas are called **coefficients**.

[OpenStax Chemistry 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations) explains that reactants are written on the left, products on the right, plus signs separate substances, and coefficients show relative amounts.

The basic pattern is:

<BlockMath math="\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}" />

State symbols are written after the formula.

| Symbol | Meaning |
| :----- | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(s)}" /> | solid |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(l)}" /> | liquid |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(g)}" /> | gas |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(aq)}" /> | dissolved in water |

Coefficients may be changed to balance a reaction. Subscripts inside formulas must not be changed casually, because subscripts define the substance. Writing <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> is different from writing <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" />.

## Balance the Photosynthesis Equation

Photosynthesis is often written as a summary reaction. The real process has many steps, but the summary equation records the atoms before and after the reaction. [OpenStax Concepts of Biology](https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/5-1-overview-of-photosynthesis) explains that photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and water to produce sugar and oxygen.

An unbalanced equation can be written as:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}" />

If we count atoms, the two sides do not match.

| Element | Left | Right |
| :------ | :--- | :---- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> |

The right coefficients make the atom count match:

<BlockMath math="6\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 6\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{O_2(g)}" />

This formal summary covers the many steps of photosynthesis. The carbohydrate formula is therefore shown without a phase symbol.

Now the count is balanced.

| Element | Left | Right |
| :------ | :--- | :---- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="18" /> | <InlineMath math="18" /> |

To balance the equation, keep each chemical formula unchanged, adjust only the coefficients, and confirm that both sides contain the same number of atoms of every element.

## Evaluate the Process with Green Chemistry

After we can read a reaction, we can judge whether the process fits green chemistry principles. A process can be useful but still not green if it forms pollutants, uses unnecessary hazardous substances, or needs too much energy.

| Everyday process | Chemical change | Green chemistry assessment |
| :--------------- | :--------------- | :------------------- |
| Burning plastic waste | Organic substances in plastic react with oxygen and heat. | Not aligned because hazardous smoke can form and waste is not prevented at the source. |
| Using cleaner in the right amount | Cleaning agents help remove dirt without excessive use. | Closer to waste prevention, but hazard labels still matter. |
| Composting food scraps | Microorganisms break organic materials into simpler materials. | Can reduce organic waste if managed without odor, leachate, or pests. |
| Randomly mixing household cleaners | Substances that are safe separately can react to form hazardous gas. | Not aligned because it increases accident risk. Do not mix cleaning products without official instructions. |

[CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/cleaning-and-disinfecting-with-bleach.html) explicitly warns against mixing household bleach or any disinfectant with other cleaners because the mixture can release vapors that are dangerous to breathe in. Follow the product label and ventilation instructions.

The examples separate a material's origin from its safety. Composting can help, but poor management can still create odor, leachate, or pests. A manufactured cleaner can be safer when its composition is known, the dose is correct, and less waste is produced.

## Judging Processes from Substances and Impact

Imagine three ways to handle dry leaves in a yard.

| Option | What happens | Judgment |
| :----- | :----------- | :------- |
| Burn them | Leaves react with oxygen and produce smoke. | Not suitable for green chemistry because pollution forms. |
| Put them in mixed trash | The leaves add to the waste that must be handled. | Better than burning, although waste is still produced. |
| Compost them properly | Organic material slowly breaks down into compost. | Best for this waste-management choice because it avoids burning and returns organic matter to use, but composting alone is not the design of a chemical product or process. |

The best answer among these three waste-management choices is **proper composting**. Composting avoids burning, reduces discarded material, and returns existing organic matter to use. That makes it responsible waste management. Calling it green chemistry requires a chemical product or process designed to reduce harm. Decomposition alone does not meet that condition.

To analyze an everyday chemical process, identify the substances that enter, the changes that occur, the substances that leave, and the hazards that can be prevented at the source.
