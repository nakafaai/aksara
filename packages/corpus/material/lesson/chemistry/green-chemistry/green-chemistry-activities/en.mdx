## A Green Chemistry Activity Redesigns a Product or Process

A **green chemistry activity** changes the design or use of a chemical product or process so hazards and waste are prevented at the source. A helpful environmental action is not automatically green chemistry. We still need to identify what enters the process, what changes chemically, what leaves it, and which hazard is reduced.

The United Nations uses the term **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** for <InlineMath math="17" /> global goals in the <InlineMath math="2030" /> Agenda. Green chemistry does not replace the SDGs and an SDG label does not prove that an activity is green chemistry. It can support several goals when chemical design makes materials, energy, water, air, or waste measurably safer.

The [United Nations](https://sdgs.un.org/goals) provides the official SDG list.

[ACS](https://www.acs.org/green-chemistry-sustainability/education/chemistry-sustainable-development-goals.html) also explains how chemistry connects to SDG challenges, including health, clean energy, and more responsible production.

The table links each SDG to a chemical design question and a result that can be checked.

| Goal | Chemical design question | Result to check |
| :--- | :------------------- | :------------- |
| SDG <InlineMath math="3" /> | Exposure to hazardous substances | Materials and doses are made safer. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="6" /> | Residues entering water | Liquid waste is reduced at the source. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="7" /> | Energy used by a process | Heat or electricity demand is reduced. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="12" /> | Single-use materials | The activity reduces unnecessary material use. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="13" /> | Emissions from burning | Open burning and fossil energy use are reduced. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="14" /> and SDG <InlineMath math="15" /> | Pollutants in water, soil, and living things | Pollutants are prevented before they spread. |

A chemical redesign contributes to an SDG only when the change and its supporting evidence are stated clearly.

## Follow the Material Pathway

To judge a green chemistry claim, trace the material pathway and compare it with a defined alternative. Record the substances used, the chemical or physical changes, the product, the residue, the hazard, and the function that must still work.

| Part of the process | Question to answer |
| :------------ | :----------------- |
| Input materials | Which substances are used, and are any of them hazardous? |
| Process | Are substances mixed, reused, decomposed, or reacted? |
| Product or residue | What leaves the process after it happens? |
| Evidence | What can be observed or measured under the same conditions? |
| Risk | Which intrinsic hazard or exposure is reduced compared with the old method? |

A green claim is testable only when it includes evidence. Words such as "natural", "efficient", and "eco-friendly" do not identify which hazard or waste has decreased. A fair comparison must also keep the required function constant. Using less cleaner is not an improvement if the surface remains dirty, and a replacement material must still perform the same task.

## Use Enough Soap without Adding Excess Residue

Lots of foam can be mistaken for better cleaning. With dish soap, too much product can lengthen rinsing and add more cleaning residue to wastewater. Compare the dose that removes the dirt with a larger dose, then check the residue and water needed for rinsing. This is an example of careful product use, not a redesign of the cleaner itself.

A **surfactant** is a substance in a cleaner that helps water lift oil and dirt. Surfactants are useful, but they still need to be used in a sufficient amount.

| What is compared | Chemical effect | Observed result |
| :--------------- | :---------------- | :--------------- |
| Very little soap | The surfactant may not be enough to lift oil. | The stain still sticks. |
| Enough soap | The surfactant works without much excess residue. | The stain is gone and rinsing is not excessive. |
| Too much soap | Extra surfactant enters wastewater. | Foam is hard to remove while rinsing. |

The responsible action is to use the effective dose on the label and avoid extra product that only increases residue and rinsing. Green chemistry applies more directly when the cleaner itself is redesigned to work with lower hazard, less material, or less water.

## Composting Is Waste Treatment unless the Product or Process Changes

Dry leaves, fruit peels, and food scraps contain organic matter. Burning causes rapid oxidation and produces smoke. During proper composting, microorganisms break the matter down gradually. Composting is useful waste treatment. It counts as green chemistry only when a chemical product or process is also redesigned to prevent a hazard or waste at the source.

| Way to handle organic waste | What happens | Careful classification |
| :-------------------------- | :----------- | :---------------------- |
| Burn it | Organic matter reacts with oxygen and forms smoke. | Creates pollution and does not prevent it at the source. |
| Put it in mixed trash | The material remains a waste burden that must be handled. | This changes disposal while the product and process stay the same. |
| Compost it properly | Microorganisms gradually decompose the organic matter. | Useful biological waste treatment, but green chemistry requires an additional chemical design or source-prevention claim. |

Composting still needs controlled moisture, oxygen flow, pile structure, and nutrient balance. A poorly managed pile can become anaerobic, smell, or release leachate. [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/approaches-composting) explains how these conditions control microbial activity and temperature. Name the activity from what it actually changes: waste treatment, product redesign, process redesign, or source prevention.

## Natural Materials Still Need Evidence

A biobased plastic made from banana or cassava residue may sound promising because it uses renewable or discarded feedstock. Yet "biobased" does not automatically mean safe, energy efficient, biodegradable, or compostable under local conditions. [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/trash-free-waters/frequently-asked-questions-about-plastic-recycling-and-composting) distinguishes biobased, biodegradable, and compostable claims. The claim must be followed from feedstock and additives through manufacture, useful performance, and end of use.

| Claim | What to check |
| :---- | :------------ |
| The material comes from organic waste. | Which additives are used with that waste? |
| The product is called degradable. | Under which temperature, moisture, oxygen, and time conditions does it actually degrade, and into what? |
| The process looks simple. | How much heat, water, or electricity is used? |
| The product replaces ordinary plastic. | Is it strong enough for the same function? |
| The product is called eco-friendly. | Which hazard is truly reduced compared with the old option? |

[EPA](https://www.epa.gov/greenchemistry/basics-green-chemistry) defines green chemistry around the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances across their life cycle. It emphasizes prevention at the source, which reduces the pollution that later needs treatment.

An activity belongs under green chemistry when it changes a chemical product or process, prevents hazard or waste at the source, preserves the required function, and supports the claim with a fair comparison. General environmental actions may reduce other impacts, but they should be named accurately. Calling a material "natural" or an activity "green" is never a substitute for evidence.
