## Linking Green Chemistry Activities to Global Goals

A **green chemistry activity** changes the design or use of a chemical product or process so hazards and waste are prevented at the source. A helpful environmental action does not automatically qualify as green chemistry. You still need to trace what enters the process, what changes chemically, what leaves it, and which hazard actually declines.

The [United Nations](https://sdgs.un.org/goals) defines <InlineMath math="17" /> **Sustainable Development Goals**, or SDGs, in its <InlineMath math="2030" /> Agenda. Green chemistry supports several [SDG challenges](https://www.acs.org/green-chemistry-sustainability/education/chemistry-sustainable-development-goals.html) when a chemical redesign measurably cuts hazardous materials, energy demand, water or air pollution, or waste.

> A green claim becomes credible only when measurements prove that hazard or waste dropped while the product still performs its intended job.

The table connects key SDGs to concrete chemical design questions and verifiable outcomes.

| Goal | Chemical issue to examine | Result to check |
| :--- | :------------------------ | :-------------- |
| SDG <InlineMath math="3" /> | Exposure to hazardous substances | Materials and doses are made safer. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="6" /> | Residues entering water | Liquid waste is reduced at the source. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="7" /> | Energy used by a process | Heat or electricity demand declines. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="12" /> | Single-use materials | Unnecessary material consumption drops. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="13" /> | Emissions from burning | Open burning and fossil energy use fall. |
| SDG <InlineMath math="14" /> and SDG <InlineMath math="15" /> | Pollutants in water, soil, and living things | Pollutants are prevented before they spread. |

### Cutting Process Waste with Recyclable Catalysts

A catalytic converter in a car exhaust illustrates how **catalysis** transforms hazardous substances before they enter the air. Over a solid metal catalyst bed, toxic carbon monoxide reacts with oxygen to form far less toxic carbon dioxide without consuming the catalyst:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{CO(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \xrightarrow{\text{catalyst}} 2\mathrm{CO_2(g)}" />

### Tracking Material Flow from Inputs to Residue

Begin any process audit by listing every material used, the changes it undergoes, the products, the residues, and the hazards. Whenever you compare two processes, keep the **required task constant** so the comparison stays fair.

| Part of the process | Question to answer |
| :------------------ | :----------------- |
| Input materials | Which substances enter, and are any of them hazardous? |
| Process | Are substances mixed, reused, decomposed, or reacted? |
| Product or residue | What leaves the process after it finishes? |
| Evidence | What can be observed or measured under identical conditions? |
| Risk | Which intrinsic hazard or exposure declines relative to the old method? |

A green claim is <Highlight>testable only when it includes evidence</Highlight>. Labels like natural or eco-friendly never specify which hazard dropped. Every kilogram entering a process leaves either as useful product or as residue:

<BlockMath math="m_{\text{in}} = m_{\text{product}} + m_{\text{residue}}" />

## Comparing Household Dosing and Organic Waste Treatment

Everyday choices around kitchen cleaners and organic scraps show the contrast between **source reduction** and **waste treatment**. Both matter in daily life, yet measuring exact doses and tracking where carbon goes reveal what each choice actually achieves.

### Measuring Effective Soap Doses Without Extra Residue

Thick foam is often mistaken for stronger cleaning. With dish soap, overdosing lengthens rinsing and sends extra **surfactant** into wastewater. A surfactant lifts oil and grease into water, yet extra soap beyond the effective dose adds residue without cleaning better.

| What is compared | Chemical effect | Observed result |
| :--------------- | :-------------- | :-------------- |
| Very little soap | Too little surfactant to emulsify grease. | The stain remains on the plate. |
| Enough soap | Surfactant lifts oil without heavy surplus. | The stain clears and rinsing stays quick. |
| Too much soap | Excess surfactant enters household wastewater. | Persistent foam demands extra rinse water. |

In a **hypothetical comparison** on the same stain and surface area, Formulation A needs <InlineMath math="10\text{ mL}" /> per wash whereas redesigned Formulation B achieves the same cleanliness with <InlineMath math="8\text{ mL}" />. The volume cut per wash is:

<BlockMath math="\frac{10 - 8}{10} \times 100\% = 20\%" />

### Composting Organic Matter and Controlling Heap Gases

Dry leaves, fruit peels, and food scraps contain organic carbon. Open burning oxidizes that carbon within minutes into smoke and gas, whereas proper [composting](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/approaches-composting) is **biological waste treatment** where microbes decompose matter gradually when moisture, oxygen flow, and nutrients stay balanced.

| Way to handle organic waste | What happens | Careful classification |
| :-------------------------- | :----------- | :--------------------- |
| Burn it | Organic matter oxidizes rapidly into smoke. | Generates smoke and airborne pollutants. |
| Put it in mixed trash | Material stays a waste burden for disposal. | Disposal route changes while original product stays unchanged. |
| Compost it properly | Microorganisms break matter down gradually. | Biological waste treatment handles existing organic waste. |

## Checking Evidence Behind Natural Material Claims

A biobased plastic from banana or cassava waste sounds promising because it uses renewable feedstock. Yet **biobased never automatically means safe**, energy efficient, biodegradable, or compostable under local conditions. [Biobased, biodegradable, and compostable](https://www.epa.gov/trash-free-waters/frequently-asked-questions-about-plastic-recycling-and-composting) describe distinct properties that must be tested separately across the full life cycle.

| Claim | What to check |
| :---- | :------------ |
| The material comes from organic waste. | Which additives and plasticizers are blended with that waste? |
| The product is called degradable. | Under which temperature, moisture, oxygen, and time does it break down, and into what? |
| The process looks simple. | How much heat, water, or electricity does processing consume? |
| The product replaces ordinary plastic. | Is it durable enough to perform the exact same task? |
| The product is called eco-friendly. | Which specific hazard declined compared with the conventional option? |

An activity belongs under [green chemistry](https://www.epa.gov/greenchemistry/basics-green-chemistry) when it redesigns a chemical product or process, prevents hazard or waste at the source, preserves the required function, and backs the improvement with fair comparison data. Words like natural or green never replace evidence.
