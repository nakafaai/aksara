## Addition Polymerisation Along Carbon Chains

Look around a room and almost every flexible cable jacket, water pipe, and food container is built from giant molecular chains. A **polymer** is a large molecule formed when thousands of small reactive molecules, called **monomers**, link end to end during **polymerisation**. Some polymers grow inside living organisms, whereas synthetic polymers are engineered from petroleum or plant feedstocks to match specific strength and heat demands.

| Polymer | Monomer | Type |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Starch | Glucose | Natural |
| Protein | Amino acids | Natural |
| Natural rubber | Isoprene | Natural |
| Polyethene | Ethene | Synthetic |
| Polyvinyl chloride, PVC | Chloroethene | Synthetic |
| Polypropene | Propene | Synthetic |
| Nylon | Hexanedioic acid and hexane-1,6-diamine | Synthetic |

### Opening Double Bonds to Build Chains

For **addition polymerisation** to occur, every monomer must carry a carbon-carbon double bond, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" />. Saturated molecules like ethane possess only single bonds, so they cannot open up to join a growing chain. During polymerisation, one bond of each double bond breaks open and links neighbouring monomers **without losing any atoms**:

<BlockMath math="n\,\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{-[CH_2{-}CH_2]_n-}" />

A frequent slip is leaving a double bond inside the repeating unit, writing <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-CH_2{=}CH_2-}" /> instead of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-CH_2{-}CH_2-}" />. Always count four covalent bonds around each backbone carbon to confirm the double bond has opened. For propene, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHCH_3}" />, the methyl group stays attached as a side branch off the single-bonded carbon backbone:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{-[CH_2{-}CH(CH_3)]_n-}" />

### Relative Molecular Mass of Addition Chains

Because addition polymerisation discards no atoms, the **relative molecular mass** of the finished polymer equals <InlineMath math="n" /> times the mass of one repeating unit, giving an **atom economy** of <InlineMath math="100\%" />. Heavier side atoms such as chlorine in PVC make the mass line climb far more steeply than polyethene for the same degree of polymerisation. ## Condensation Polymerisation and Eliminated Water

Not every polymer grows by opening a double bond. In **condensation polymerisation**, monomers carry two reactive functional groups at opposite ends, and every bond formed between them ejects a small byproduct molecule such as water, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. This step-growth pathway produces strong synthetic fibres used in ropes, sails, and crease-resistant clothing.

### Amide and Ester Linkages in Nylon and Terylene

**Nylon-6,6** is a polyamide built from hexanedioic acid and hexane-1,6-diamine. Each carboxyl group (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COOH}" />) reacts with an amine group (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{-NH_2}" />) to forge an **amide linkage** (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{-CONH-}" />) while releasing water:

<BlockMath math="n\,\mathrm{HOOC(CH_2)_4COOH} + n\,\mathrm{H_2N(CH_2)_6NH_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{-[OC(CH_2)_4CONH(CH_2)_6NH]_n-} + 2n\,\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Replacing the diamine with a diol produces **terylene**, a polyester held together by **ester linkages** (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COO-}" />), again eliminating water at every junction. Inspect both alternating chains below and spot where each water molecule breaks away.

### Mass Yield and Atom Economy Comparison

Assuming a condensation polymer weighs the same as its starting monomers is a classic accounting error. One mole of hexanedioic acid weighs <InlineMath math="146\text{ g}" /> and one mole of hexane-1,6-diamine weighs <InlineMath math="116\text{ g}" />, totalling <InlineMath math="262\text{ g}" />. Because two moles of water (<InlineMath math="36\text{ g}" />) escape per repeating unit, only <InlineMath math="226\text{ g}" /> remains in the nylon chain. ## Natural Rubber Coagulation and Sulfur Vulcanisation

Tapped straight from the bark of *Hevea brasiliensis*, **natural rubber** arrives as milky **latex**, a polymer of isoprene (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_5H_8}" />). Raw rubber turns soft and sticky in tropical heat, yet a controlled sequence of acid coagulation and sulfur heating converts it into tough car tyres.

### Controlling Latex Coagulation with Acid and Ammonia

Fresh latex stays liquid because every microscopic rubber particle is wrapped in a **negatively charged protein membrane**. Like charges repel, keeping the rubber particles suspended apart in water. Adding an acid such as ethanoic acid introduces <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions that neutralise those negative membranes, letting particles collide, rupture, and clump together as **coagulated rubber**. Left standing in warm air, bacteria naturally digest sugars in latex into lactic acid, causing slow spontaneous coagulation.

To keep freshly tapped latex liquid during transport, farmers stir in **ammonia solution**. Many learners accidentally swap the roles of acid and ammonia, so anchor the chemistry through ions: ammonia is an alkali whose <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions neutralise bacterial acid before it can touch the protein membranes.

> Acid neutralises the negative protein membranes so rubber particles collide and coagulate, whereas ammonia neutralises acid to keep latex liquid.

### Sulfur Crosslinks and Elastic Recovery

Even after coagulation, unvulcanised rubber remains weak because its long polyisoprene chains easily slide past one another under tension and fail to snap back. Heating natural rubber with sulfur triggers **vulcanisation**: sulfur atoms react across carbon double bonds to build strong **sulfur cross-links** between adjacent chains.

| Property | Unvulcanised rubber | Vulcanised rubber |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Elasticity | Less elastic, stays stretched | More elastic, returns to its shape |
| Strength | Weak and soft | Stronger and harder |
| Heat resistance | Turns sticky when hot | Resists heat |
| Resistance to oxidation | Oxidises easily | More resistant |

Watch how the sulfur bridges act like microscopic springs: rather than softening the rubber, vulcanisation locks chains against permanent slipping while giving tyres the strength and heat resistance needed on hot roads. **Synthetic rubbers** such as neoprene push durability further by resisting oil in fuel hoses and wetsuits. Because most synthetic polymers are **non-biodegradable** and burning PVC releases toxic hydrogen chloride gas, reducing, reusing, and recycling plastics remains essential alongside smart material design.
