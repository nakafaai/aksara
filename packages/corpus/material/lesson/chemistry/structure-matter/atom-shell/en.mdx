## Shells as Energy Levels

In the simple Bohr model, electrons are shown in **atomic shells**, which represent specific energy levels around the nucleus. A shell closer to the nucleus has lower energy. A shell farther from the nucleus has higher energy.

The Bohr model uses <Highlight>orbits with specific energies</Highlight> to explain the line spectrum of hydrogen.

In a shell diagram, each circle marks an energy level. The circles are not solid paths followed by electrons. They only show the arrangement of energy levels in the simple model.

> Think of atomic shells as energy rungs on a ladder around the nucleus rather than circular tracks carved in space.

Shells are named with letters:

| Shell | Shell number |
| :---- | :----------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 4" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 6" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Q}" /> | <InlineMath math="n = 7" /> |

The letters <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> through <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Q}" /> are shell names. The value <InlineMath math="n" /> is the **shell number**. As <InlineMath math="n" /> increases, the electron shell is generally farther from the nucleus and has higher energy.

### Light from a Falling Electron

Shells farther from the nucleus have higher energy. When hydrogen's electron drops from shell <InlineMath math="n = 3" /> to shell <InlineMath math="n = 2" />, **the energy difference leaves as one photon**. That photon is red, with a wavelength of about <InlineMath math="656\ \mathrm{nm}" />. Choose another jump and watch the line move toward blue and violet as the energy gap grows.

### Maximum Electron Capacity

Shells are named <InlineMath math="\mathrm K" /> through <InlineMath math="\mathrm Q" />. For shell number <InlineMath math="n" />, the maximum capacity is <InlineMath math="2n^2" />. Substitute the shell number to obtain each limit:

| Shell | Shell number | Maximum electrons from <InlineMath math="2n^2" /> |
| :---- | :----------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2(1)^2 = 2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="2(2)^2 = 8" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="2(3)^2 = 18" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="2(4)^2 = 32" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="2(5)^2 = 50" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P}" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="2(6)^2 = 72" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Q}" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> | <InlineMath math="2(7)^2 = 98" /> |

The word **maximum** matters. Shell <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> can hold up to <InlineMath math="18" /> electrons, but that does not mean every atom fills <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> to <InlineMath math="18" /> before using the next shell. For the examples up to calcium, we use the simpler pattern in the interactive model.

The capacity rule gives each shell its limit: shell <InlineMath math="n" /> holds at most <InlineMath math="2n^2" /> electrons. Rotate the bars: the limit grows fast, from <InlineMath math="2" /> in the K shell to <InlineMath math="32" /> in the N shell.

### One Outer Electron Farther from the Nucleus

Lithium, sodium, and potassium each carry one outer electron, but **each sits one shell farther from the nucleus** (<InlineMath math="n = 2" />, <InlineMath math="3" />, and <InlineMath math="4" />). Because the attraction weakens with distance and inner shielding, less energy is needed to pull that outer electron away, making the reaction in water more vigorous down the group.

## Calcium Electron Configuration

Calcium has atomic number <InlineMath math="20" />. The symbol <InlineMath math="Z" /> means atomic number. Since the atom is neutral, it also has <InlineMath math="20" /> electrons. Fill the shells in order from the **lowest energy**, and record the electron count of each shell as you go.

### Calcium Shell Filling Steps

In the **simple shell model**, calcium's shells fill in this order:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Ca}: Z &= 20 \\
e^- &= 20 \\
\mathrm{K} &: 2 \quad \text{left } 18 \\
\mathrm{L} &: 8 \quad \text{left } 10 \\
\mathrm{M} &: 8 \quad \text{left } 2 \\
\mathrm{N} &: 2 \quad \text{left } 0
\end{aligned}" />

### Filling Shells M and N

A common question is: why not <InlineMath math="2, 8, 10" />?

In calcium's ground state, electrons occupy the <InlineMath math="4s" /> subshell before <InlineMath math="3d" />. The number before the letter identifies the main shell: <InlineMath math="4s" /> belongs to shell <InlineMath math="\mathrm N" />, while <InlineMath math="3d" /> belongs to shell <InlineMath math="\mathrm M" />. Shell N therefore begins filling before shell M reaches capacity.

After shells K and L hold <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="8" /> electrons, <InlineMath math="10" /> electrons remain. Eight occupy the parts of shell M already filled in argon, and the next two enter <InlineMath math="4s" />. This gives the **ground-state configuration** <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 2" />.

Here is calcium in the simple shell model: <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 2" /> electrons from the K shell outward. Count each ring, and notice the two fuchsia electrons in shell N, even though shell M could hold more.

## Where the Simple Shell Model Stops

Do not mix up these two ideas.

| Statement | Accurate meaning |
| :-------- | :--------------- |
| Atomic shells are drawn as circles. | The drawing represents energy levels. Electrons do not follow a rigid circular path. |
| The formula <InlineMath math="2n^2" /> gives maximum capacity. | Maximum capacity differs from the order in which electrons fill the shells. Heavier elements need the subshell and orbital model. |

In the modern orbital model, electrons after argon fill <InlineMath math="4s" /> before <InlineMath math="3d" />. The interactive model above uses a simple shell pattern to show the number of electrons in each shell. A more detailed configuration uses the modern orbital-filling order.

The outermost shell also leads into the idea of valence electrons. Electrons in the outermost shell are called **valence electrons**, and they strongly affect whether an atom tends to form ions or chemical bonds.
