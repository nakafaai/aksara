## Flame Colors Appear as Electrons Return to Lower Energy Levels

When salts such as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaCl}" /> or <InlineMath math="\mathrm{KCl}" /> are heated in a flame test, the visible color is not random. Heat energy can temporarily place electrons at a higher energy state. When the electrons return to a lower state, the atom or ion emits light.

The Bohr model uses discrete energy levels to explain the line spectrum of hydrogen.

The word **discrete** means not every energy value is allowed. Picture a staircase. You can stand on the first, second, or third step, but not in the empty space between two steps. In an atom, electrons are only allowed at certain energy levels.

**Excitation** means an electron absorbs energy and temporarily moves to a higher energy level. When the electron drops back down, energy is released as light. In a flame test, the experiment must follow safety procedures and use protective equipment because it involves heating chemicals.

A flame color is light from electrons falling to a lower level. In hydrogen the levels sit at <InlineMath math="E_n=-\tfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \mathrm{eV}" />, so a fall from <InlineMath math="n=3" /> to <InlineMath math="n=2" /> releases <InlineMath math="1.89\ \mathrm{eV}" />, and <InlineMath math="\lambda=\tfrac{1240}{1.89}\approx656\ \mathrm{nm}" /> is red light. ## Electron Arrangement in the Shell Model

**Electron configuration** is a way to write how electrons are distributed around the atomic nucleus. Here, we use a **simple shell model**, where electrons are grouped into shells <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" />.

For a neutral atom:

<BlockMath math="e^- = Z" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="e^-" /> is the number of electrons, while <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the atomic number or the number of protons. So a neutral sodium atom with <InlineMath math="Z = 11" /> has <InlineMath math="11" /> electrons.

This simple shell model is limited to light elements up to calcium, which means <InlineMath math="Z \le 20" />. Heavier elements require subshells and orbitals.

## Reading the Filling Order

In the simple shell model for the first elements **up to calcium**, electrons are filled from the shell closer to the nucleus first. The order is:

| Shell | Occupation in the atoms shown here | How to read it |
| :---- | :------------------------------- | :------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | First shell, closest to the nucleus. |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> | Filled after the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> shell is full. |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> for elements through calcium | Filled after the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" /> shell is full. |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> or <InlineMath math="2" /> for potassium and calcium | Used after the <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8" /> pattern. |

The simple shell model can be used for the elements from hydrogen through calcium. The arrangement <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 2" /> is calcium's configuration. Each earlier element has the number of electrons given by its atomic number. Heavier elements require subshell filling because the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> shell can hold more than <InlineMath math="8" /> electrons.

## Connecting Configuration to Spectra

The configurations in the shell model show the arrangement of electrons in the ground state, meaning <Highlight>the lowest-energy state</Highlight>. A line spectrum appears when an electron receives energy and then returns to a lower energy level.

The energy of the emitted light can be written as:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E_{\text{photon}} &amp;= E_{\text{high}} - E_{\text{low}} \\ &amp;= h\nu \\ &amp;= \frac{hc}{\lambda} \end{aligned}" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="h" /> is Planck's constant, <InlineMath math="\nu" /> is the frequency of light, <InlineMath math="c" /> is the speed of light, and <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is wavelength. The [exact values](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/Constants/Table/allascii.txt) are <InlineMath math="h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}" /> and <InlineMath math="c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}" />.

For example, calculate the energy of one photon with a vacuum wavelength of <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{nm}" />. Use the rounded constants <InlineMath math="h\approx6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}" /> and <InlineMath math="c\approx3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\lambda&=600\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m=6.00\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm m\\
E&=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\\
&\approx\frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)}{6.00\times10^{-7}}\ \mathrm J\\
&\approx3.31\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J
\end{aligned}" />

Electrons occupy specific energy levels. A transition to a lower energy level can release light with a specific energy, while electron configuration describes how the electrons are arranged in the ground state.

## Electron Count from Atomic Number

When asked to write a simple electron configuration, use this order:

- Make sure whether the particle is a **neutral atom or an ion**.
- If it is neutral, the electron count equals the atomic number.
- Fill shells from inside to outside in the model being used.
- Write the electron count in each shell with commas.

For neutral magnesium:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Z &= 12 \\
e^- &= 12 \\
\text{configuration} &= 2, 8, 2
\end{aligned}" />

The electron configuration of neutral magnesium is <InlineMath math="2, 8, 2" />. Starting with the atomic number prevents an incorrect electron count for a neutral atom.
