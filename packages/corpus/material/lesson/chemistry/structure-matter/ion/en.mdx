## When an Atom Is No Longer Neutral

An **ion** forms when an atom or group of atoms carries an overall electric charge. Here, we focus first on single-atom ions whose <Highlight>electron count differs from their proton count</Highlight>.

Protons stay inside the nucleus during ordinary chemical reactions. Only electrons leave or join the outer shells. That is why the proton count still defines the element, while the balance between protons and electrons sets the charge.

> Protons lock in an element's identity while gaining or losing electrons shifts its electric charge.

Each proton contributes <InlineMath math="+1" /> and each electron contributes <InlineMath math="-1" />. Matching counts leave an atom electrically neutral. Fewer electrons leave a positive ion, and extra electrons create a negative ion.

An ion forms when an electron moves. Sodium has <InlineMath math="1" /> electron in its outer shell, while chlorine needs <InlineMath math="1" /> more to fill its outer shell with <InlineMath math="8" />. **Sodium shrinks into a smaller <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na^+}" />, and chlorine expands into a larger <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl^-}" />**, because the electron cloud changes while the nuclear pull stays constant.

## Cations and Anions

A positively charged ion is called a **cation**. A cation forms when an atom loses electrons.

A negatively charged ion is called an **anion**. An anion forms when an atom gains electrons.

An [ion](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03158) is an atomic or molecular particle with a net electric charge.

## Ion Charge from the Difference between Proton and Electron Counts

You can find any ion's charge number by subtracting its electron count from its proton count.

<BlockMath math="q = p^+ - e^-" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="q" /> is the ion charge number in **elementary-charge units**, not coulombs, <InlineMath math="p^+" /> is the number of protons, and <InlineMath math="e^-" /> is the number of electrons.

To find the electron count of an ion, rearrange the same formula.

<BlockMath math="e^- = p^+ - q" />

Applying this rearrangement to a sodium cation removes one electron.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}^{+} &: p^+ = 11,\ q = +1 \\
e^- &= 11 - (+1) \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}" />

Doing the same calculation for a fluoride anion adds one electron.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{19}_{9}\mathrm{F}^{-} &: p^+ = 9,\ q = -1 \\
e^- &= 9 - (-1) \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}" />

The negative sign in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{F}^{-}" /> means there are more electrons than protons, so subtracting a negative charge raises the electron count to <InlineMath math="10" />.

Compare a lithium atom with a lithium ion below, with protons in orange at the back and electrons in violet at the front. Losing one electron leaves <InlineMath math="3" /> protons against <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons, giving a charge of <InlineMath math="+1" />.

## Composition of an Ion

When an atom becomes an ion, its **proton and neutron counts stay the same**. Its electron count changes according to the ion's charge. The table shows what happens to each part.

| Part to read | When an ion forms | Effect |
| :----------- | :---------------- | :----- |
| Protons | Stay the same | The element does not change. |
| Neutrons | Stay the same for the same isotope | The mass number does not change. |
| Electrons | Change | The ion charge changes. |

So <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}^{+}" /> are both sodium because the proton count stays <InlineMath math="11" />. A neutral sodium atom has <InlineMath math="11" /> electrons. After losing one electron, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}^{+}" /> has <InlineMath math="10" />.

Check one row, such as <InlineMath math="{}^{7}_{3}\mathrm{Li}^{+}" />. Its atomic number is <InlineMath math="3" />, so it has <InlineMath math="3" /> protons. With a charge of <InlineMath math="+1" />, its electron count follows directly.

<BlockMath math="e^- = 3 - (+1) = 2" />

Its neutron count comes from subtracting the atomic number from the mass number.

<BlockMath math="n^0 = 7 - 3 = 4" />

If you feel unsure, start from the charge calculation. Cations always have **fewer electrons than protons**, while anions have more electrons than protons.
