## When an Atom Is No Longer Neutral

A neutral atom has the same number of protons and electrons. An **ion** forms when an atom or group of atoms has an overall charge. Here, we focus first on ions made from one atom.

An ion forms when the number of <Highlight>electrons differs from the number of protons</Highlight>.

Protons are in the nucleus and usually do not move during simple chemical processes. The electron count changes when an ion forms. The number of protons determines the element, while the difference between proton and electron counts determines the ion charge.

Each proton contributes <InlineMath math="+1" /> and each electron contributes <InlineMath math="-1" />. The ion charge is therefore the proton count minus the electron count. Equal counts give a neutral atom. Fewer electrons give a positive ion, while more electrons give a negative ion.

## Cations and Anions

A positively charged ion is called a **cation**. A cation forms when an atom loses electrons.

A negatively charged ion is called an **anion**. An anion forms when an atom gains electrons.

An [ion](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03158) is an atomic or molecular particle with a net electric charge.

## Ion Charge from the Difference between Proton and Electron Counts

<BlockMath math="q = p^+ - e^-" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="q" /> is the ion charge number in **elementary-charge units**, not coulombs, <InlineMath math="p^+" /> is the number of protons, and <InlineMath math="e^-" /> is the number of electrons.

To find the electron count of an ion, rearrange the same formula:

<BlockMath math="e^- = p^+ - q" />

Sodium example:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}^{+} &: p^+ = 11,\ q = +1 \\
e^- &= 11 - (+1) \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}" />

Fluoride example:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{19}_{9}\mathrm{F}^{-} &: p^+ = 9,\ q = -1 \\
e^- &= 9 - (-1) \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}" />

The negative sign in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{F}^{-}" /> means there are more electrons than protons. That is why <InlineMath math="9 - (-1)" /> becomes <InlineMath math="10" />.

Compare a lithium atom with a lithium ion, protons in orange at the back and electrons in violet at the front. Losing one electron leaves <InlineMath math="3" /> protons against <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons, so <InlineMath math="q=3-2=+1" />.

## Composition of an Ion

When an atom becomes an ion, its **proton and neutron counts stay the same**. Its electron count changes according to the ion's charge. The table shows what happens to each part.

| Part to read | When an ion forms | Effect |
| :----------- | :---------------- | :----- |
| Protons | Stay the same | The element does not change. |
| Neutrons | Stay the same for the same isotope | The mass number does not change. |
| Electrons | Change | The ion charge changes. |

So <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}^{+}" /> are both sodium because the proton count stays <InlineMath math="11" />. A neutral sodium atom has <InlineMath math="11" /> electrons. After losing one electron, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}^{+}" /> has <InlineMath math="10" />.

### Checking the Lithium Row

Check one row, such as <InlineMath math="{}^{7}_{3}\mathrm{Li}^{+}" />. Its atomic number is <InlineMath math="3" />, so it has <InlineMath math="3" /> protons. Its charge is <InlineMath math="+1" />, so the electron count is:

<BlockMath math="e^- = 3 - (+1) = 2" />

Its neutron count comes from the mass number:

<BlockMath math="n^0 = 7 - 3 = 4" />

If you feel unsure, start from the charge calculation. Cations always have **fewer electrons than protons**, while anions have more electrons than protons.
