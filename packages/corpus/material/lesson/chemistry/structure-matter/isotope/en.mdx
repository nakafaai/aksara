## Isotopes of the Same Element Have Different Masses

Hydrogen in the Sun, carbon in our bodies, and oxygen in water do not always appear as only one mass version. One element can have several atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Those atoms are called **isotopes**.

[Isotopes](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03331) are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

In short, isotopes share the same <InlineMath math="Z" />, while their neutron counts <InlineMath math="n^0" /> and mass numbers <InlineMath math="A" /> differ.

The symbol <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the atomic number, which equals the number of protons. The symbol <InlineMath math="A" /> is the mass number, which equals protons plus neutrons. If <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the same, the element is the same. If <InlineMath math="n^0" /> is different, the mass number also changes.

> Isotopes belong to the same element because their proton count stays fixed even when extra neutrons make the nucleus heavier.

## Hydrogen and Carbon Isotopes

Hydrogen and carbon provide the clearest examples of how **extra neutrons change mass without altering an element**. Both families appear across everyday matter and laboratory standards.

### Three Isotopes of Hydrogen

All hydrogen isotopes have <InlineMath math="1" /> proton. Because their proton count is <InlineMath math="1" />, they are still hydrogen. The difference is their **neutron count**.

| Hydrogen isotope | Symbol | Protons | Electrons in a neutral atom | Neutrons |
| :--------------- | :----- | :------ | :---------------- | :------- |
| Hydrogen-1, or protium | <InlineMath math="{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Deuterium | <InlineMath math="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Tritium | <InlineMath math="{}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

Deuterium is sometimes written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{D}" />, and tritium is sometimes written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{T}" />. Those shortcuts do not mean new elements. Deuterium and tritium are still hydrogen because their proton count is still <InlineMath math="1" />.

Only the neutron column grows, from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="2" />, and with it the mass number.

### Carbon Isotopes Differ in Neutron Count

Carbon always has <InlineMath math="6" /> protons. In <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, and <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, the neutron count rises step by step.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 12 - 6 = 6 \\
{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 13 - 6 = 7 \\
{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 14 - 6 = 8
\end{aligned}" />

<InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> are stable. <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is radioactive, so its amount in nature is extremely small. Because <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> **decays over time**, scientists can use it to estimate the age of objects that once came from living things, such as old wood or bone.

## Differences Between Isotopes and Ions

An ion forms when an atom gains or loses electrons. Isotopes of the same element **differ in their number of neutrons**.

| Concept | Particle count that differs | Particle that determines the element |
| :------ | :-------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Ion | Electrons | Protons |
| Isotope | Neutrons | Protons |

For example, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}^{+}" /> is a carbon ion that has lost one electron. With <InlineMath math="6" /> protons and charge <InlineMath math="+1" />, its electron count is <InlineMath math="5" />. In contrast, <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is a carbon isotope because its neutron count is different from <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />.

If the atom is neutral, electrons still equal protons.

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow e^- = p^+" />

So neutral <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> has <InlineMath math="6" /> protons, <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons, and <InlineMath math="8" /> neutrons.

Place every nucleus by its neutrons across and its protons up. Isotopes of one element share <InlineMath math="Z" />, so the carbon isotopes line up on one amber row. An ion does not move on this map at all, because only its electrons changed. Compare <InlineMath math="^{14}\mathrm{C}" /> with <InlineMath math="^{14}\mathrm{N}" />, which share a mass number yet belong to different elements.

## Nuclear Stability and Isotope Uses

An isotope's neutron-to-proton balance shapes **how stable its nucleus remains over time**. That balance decides whether an isotope serves as a stable reference or a radioactive clock.

### Practical Uses of Isotopes

Neutron count affects an isotope's **mass and nuclear stability**. Those properties determine why a particular isotope can serve as a tracer, a possible energy source, or a clock for estimating the age of a material.

[Deuterium and tritium](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainsdeuterium-tritium-fusion-fuel) are studied as a fuel pair in fusion energy research.

<InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is used in radiocarbon dating to estimate the age of organic materials, meaning materials that once came from living things. This method can determine ages up to about <InlineMath math="60\,000" /> years.

Any use of an isotope must account for its nuclear stability, meaning whether its nucleus tends to decay. Radioactive isotopes such as tritium and <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> require controlled procedures and radiation-safety rules.

### Carbon Fourteen Decays Slowly

Carbon dating uses the isotope <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, which decays into <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}" />. Nobody can predict when one nucleus will decay, but **the number left always halves every <InlineMath math="5730" /> years**. ## From Isotopes to Average Atomic Mass

On the periodic table, relative atomic mass is often a decimal. That number is not the mass number of one isotope. Relative atomic mass is calculated from the mass of each isotope and its abundance in a reference sample.

The tabulated percentages are a [**representative isotopic composition**](https://www.physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Compositions/notes.html) for reference materials. The composition can vary with a material's origin and treatment. The percentages below are therefore reference values, and a particular natural sample may differ.

| Carbon isotope | Mass number | Representative isotopic composition |
| :------------- | :---------- | :---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | About <InlineMath math="98.93\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="13" /> | About <InlineMath math="1.07\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="14" /> | Not included in the NIST stable-isotope composition |

Because <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is the most abundant carbon isotope, carbon's relative atomic mass on the periodic table is close to <InlineMath math="12" />, but not exactly <InlineMath math="12" />.

Isotope percentages are measured with a mass spectrometer. Each peak's position shows the isotope mass and **its height reflects natural abundance**. Look at chlorine's two peaks below and notice why the weighted average sits at <InlineMath math="35.45" />.

Use these three questions whenever you read an isotope symbol.

- How many protons are there?
- What is the mass number?
- How many neutrons come from <InlineMath math="A - Z" />?

Applying those steps to <InlineMath math="{}^{92}_{40}\mathrm{Zr}" /> gives its neutron count directly.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n^0 &= A - Z \\
&= 92 - 40 \\
&= 52
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="{}^{92}_{40}\mathrm{Zr}" /> has <InlineMath math="52" /> neutrons. The relation <InlineMath math="n = A - Z" /> works for any isotope with a known atomic number and mass number, so no neutron table is needed.

The **weighted average** combines isotope masses with their abundances. Approximating the isotope masses of carbon-12 and carbon-13 as <InlineMath math="12\ \mathrm{u}" /> and <InlineMath math="13\ \mathrm{u}" /> while neglecting the tiny trace of carbon-14 gives the standard periodic table value.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
A_r(\mathrm C)&\approx 12(0.9893)+13(0.0107)\\
&=11.8716+0.1391\\
&=12.0107\approx12.01
\end{aligned}" />

A more precise calculation uses measured isotope masses instead of mass numbers. The average also depends on the sample's isotope composition.
