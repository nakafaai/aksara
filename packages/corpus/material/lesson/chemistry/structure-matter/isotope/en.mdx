## Same Element Different Mass

Hydrogen in the Sun, carbon in our bodies, and oxygen in water do not always appear as only one mass version. One element can have several atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Those atoms are called **isotopes**.

[OpenStax Chemistry Atoms First 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-atoms-first-2e/pages/2-3-atomic-structure-and-symbolism) explains that isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

The [IUPAC Gold Book](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03331) emphasizes the same idea. Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Read the pattern like this:

<BlockMath math="\text{isotopes} \Rightarrow Z\ \text{same},\ n^0\ \text{different},\ A\ \text{different}" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the atomic number, which equals the number of protons. The symbol <InlineMath math="A" /> is the mass number, which equals protons plus neutrons. If <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the same, the element is the same. If <InlineMath math="n^0" /> is different, the mass number also changes.

Isotopes belong to the same element because they have the same number of protons. Different numbers of neutrons give them different mass numbers.

## Three Isotopes of Hydrogen

All hydrogen isotopes have <InlineMath math="1" /> proton. Because their proton count is <InlineMath math="1" />, they are still hydrogen. The difference is their neutron count.

| Hydrogen isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutral electrons | Neutrons |
| :--------------- | :----- | :------ | :---------------- | :------- |
| Light hydrogen | <InlineMath math="{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Deuterium | <InlineMath math="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| Tritium | <InlineMath math="{}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

Deuterium is sometimes written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{D}" />, and tritium is sometimes written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{T}" />. Those shortcuts do not mean new elements. Deuterium and tritium are still hydrogen because their proton count is still <InlineMath math="1" />.

## Carbon Shows the Role of Neutrons

Carbon always has <InlineMath math="6" /> protons. In <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, and <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />, the neutron count increases.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 12 - 6 = 6 \\
{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 13 - 6 = 7 \\
{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} &: n^0 = 14 - 6 = 8
\end{aligned}" />

<InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> are stable. <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is radioactive, so its amount in nature is extremely small. Because <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> decays over time, scientists can use it to estimate the age of objects that once came from living things, such as old wood or bone.

## Do Not Mix It Up with Ions

Ions and isotopes both change numbers in an atom, but the changing part is different.

| Concept | What changes | What locks the element |
| :------ | :----------- | :--------------------- |
| Ion | Electrons | Protons |
| Isotope | Neutrons | Protons |

For example, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}^{2-}" /> is a carbon ion because its electrons changed. In contrast, <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is a carbon isotope because its neutron count is different from <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />.

If the atom is neutral, electrons still equal protons:

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow e^- = p^+" />

So neutral <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> has <InlineMath math="6" /> protons, <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons, and <InlineMath math="8" /> neutrons.

## Uses of Isotopes

Neutron count affects an isotope's mass and nuclear stability. Those properties determine why a particular isotope can serve as a tracer, a possible energy source, or a clock for estimating the age of a material.

Deuterium and tritium, two hydrogen isotopes, are widely discussed in nuclear fusion research. [DOE](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainsdeuterium-tritium-fusion-fuel) describes deuterium-tritium fuel as an important isotope pair in fusion energy development.

<InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is used in radiocarbon dating to estimate the age of organic materials, meaning materials that once came from living things. The [University of Chicago](https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/what-is-carbon-14-dating) explains that <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> dating can determine the age of organic materials up to about <InlineMath math="60\,000" /> years.

Any use of an isotope must account for its nuclear stability, meaning whether its nucleus tends to decay. Radioactive isotopes such as tritium and <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> require controlled procedures and radiation-safety rules.

## From Isotopes to Average Atomic Mass

On the periodic table, relative atomic mass is often a decimal. That number is not the mass number of one isotope. It is derived from isotope masses and the isotopic composition of the material being represented.

[NIST](https://www.physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Compositions/notes.html) calls its tabulated percentages a **representative isotopic composition** for materials commonly encountered in laboratories. It also warns that composition varies with a material's origin and treatment. The percentages below are reference values, and a particular natural sample may differ.

Carbon is an easy example:

| Carbon isotope | Mass number | Representative isotopic composition |
| :------------- | :---------- | :---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="12" /> | About <InlineMath math="98.93\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="13" /> | About <InlineMath math="1.07\%" /> |
| <InlineMath math="{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="14" /> | Extremely small |

Because <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> is the most abundant carbon isotope, carbon's relative atomic mass on the periodic table is close to <InlineMath math="12" />, but not exactly <InlineMath math="12" />.

## Distinguishing Isotopes by Atomic and Mass Numbers

Use these three questions whenever you read an isotope:

- How many protons are there?
- What is the mass number?
- How many neutrons come from <InlineMath math="A - Z" />?

For <InlineMath math="{}^{92}_{40}\mathrm{Zr}" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
n^0 &= A - Z \\
&= 92 - 40 \\
&= 52
\end{aligned}" />

So <InlineMath math="{}^{92}_{40}\mathrm{Zr}" /> has <InlineMath math="52" /> neutrons. The relation <InlineMath math="n=A-Z" /> works for any isotope with a known atomic number and mass number, so no neutron table is needed.
