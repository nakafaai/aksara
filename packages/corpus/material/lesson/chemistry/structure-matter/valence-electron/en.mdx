## Valence Electrons in Chemical Reactions

Start with calcium. In the simple shell model, a neutral calcium atom is written with the electron configuration <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 2" />. That means calcium's electrons are spread across the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{L}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> shells.

The outermost occupied shell is the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> shell. It contains <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons. So, a calcium atom has <InlineMath math="2" /> **valence electrons**.

For main-group elements, valence electrons are <Highlight>the electrons in the outermost occupied shell</Highlight> of an atom.

When atoms form bonds or ions, they most often share, lose, or gain these outer electrons. The number and arrangement of valence electrons therefore affect how an element reacts.

## Identifying Valence Electrons from the Outermost Shell

The following steps determine the number of electrons in the **outermost occupied shell**.

- Write the shell configuration for the neutral atom.
- Find the last shell that still contains electrons.
- The number of electrons in that last shell is the number of valence electrons.

Examples:

| Atom | Shell configuration | Outermost shell | Valence electrons |
| :--- | :------------------ | :-------------- | :---------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Mg}" /> | <InlineMath math="2, 8, 2" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl}" /> | <InlineMath math="2, 8, 7" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ar}" /> | <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{M}" /> | <InlineMath math="8" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ca}" /> | <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 2" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

Do not add all electrons when looking for valence electrons. Calcium has <InlineMath math="20" /> total electrons, but only <InlineMath math="2" /> valence electrons because only the outermost occupied shell is counted.

The number of occupied shells increases down the group, while the outermost shell still contains <InlineMath math="1" /> electron. This is why lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, cesium, and francium are placed in the same group.

Hydrogen is an exception. It has <InlineMath math="1" /> valence electron, but it does not behave like an alkali metal. Valence-electron count is one clue to chemical behavior, and the rest of the atomic structure also matters.

## Connection to Chemical Properties

Chemical reactions often involve valence electrons, the electrons in the outer shell. Atoms can **lose, gain, or share these electrons**.

In short:

- In many main-group metals, atoms with <InlineMath math="1" /> or <InlineMath math="2" /> valence electrons tend to lose electrons when forming ions.
- Elements with <InlineMath math="7" /> valence electrons often gain or share <InlineMath math="1" /> electron.
- Noble gases such as helium and argon have stable valence-electron configurations, so they are very unreactive.

For main-group elements, the quick pattern is:

- Group <InlineMath math="1" /> has <InlineMath math="1" /> valence electron.
- Group <InlineMath math="2" /> has <InlineMath math="2" /> valence electrons.
- Groups <InlineMath math="13" /> to <InlineMath math="18" /> have <InlineMath math="3" /> to <InlineMath math="8" /> valence electrons.

Helium is an important exception: helium is in group <InlineMath math="18" />, but it has only <InlineMath math="2" /> valence electrons because its first shell is full with <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons.

For transition elements, electrons in the outermost <InlineMath math="s" /> subshell and nearby <InlineMath math="d" /> subshells can participate in bonding. The simple group-number shortcut therefore does not always give one valence-electron count. The examples below use main-group elements and the simple shell model.

### Bonds Follow Valence Electrons

Valence electrons decide how many bonds an atom forms. Carbon has <InlineMath math="4" /> valence electrons and forms <InlineMath math="4" /> bonds in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4}" />. Nitrogen forms <InlineMath math="3" /> bonds in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" />, oxygen <InlineMath math="2" /> in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, and chlorine <InlineMath math="1" /> in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{HCl}" />. **Each atom shares electrons until its outer shell is full**.

## Checking Valence Electrons from the Outermost Shell

Read the shell configuration and take the outermost occupied shell count as the valence-electron count. For groups 1 and 2, this equals the group number. For groups 13 through 18, subtract 10 from the group number. Remember the **helium exception**: 2 valence electrons, but group 18.

### Valence Electron Patterns within a Group

Elements <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Al}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ga}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{In}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Tl}" /> are in group <InlineMath math="13" />. The quick main-group pattern is <InlineMath math="3" /> valence electrons.

<InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K}" /> sit in group <InlineMath math="1" />, so the same pattern gives each <InlineMath math="1" /> valence electron that is easily lost, and both tend to form <InlineMath math="+1" /> ions. Argon is less reactive than chlorine because it has a stable **valence octet**. An octet means eight valence electrons, not that shell M has reached its full capacity of 18. Chlorine often gains one electron when forming an ion or shares electrons in covalent bonds.

Write the shell configuration, find the outermost occupied shell and count its electrons. Do that for atomic numbers 1 to 20 and the count climbs to eight, then falls back to one whenever a new shell starts. Zoom in on the drop after neon, and on chlorine, one electron short of eight.
