## Evidence That Atoms Have Inner Parts

Dalton's model explained fixed ratios in chemical reactions, but it still pictured atoms as solid spheres. Electrical experiments later showed that atoms contain smaller charged particles.

In the late 1800s, scientists began using low-pressure glass tubes and high voltage. A beam called a **cathode ray** appeared inside the tube. The beam could be bent by electric and magnetic fields. From this evidence, atoms started to look less like solid balls and more like structures with smaller particles inside.

[OpenStax Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-atoms-first-2e/pages/2-2-evolution-of-atomic-theory) explains the evidence sequence from Thomson's cathode ray experiment, Rutherford's gold foil experiment, and Chadwick's discovery of the neutron.

[Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/J-J-Thomson) records that J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in <InlineMath math="1897" /> and showed that this particle was far lighter than an atom.

## Cathode Rays Identified Electrons

Thomson used a cathode ray tube. Inside the tube, most air was removed, then a high voltage was applied. The beam that appeared was always attracted toward positive charge and pushed away from negative charge.

The electrical rule is simple: opposite charges attract, and like charges repel. Because the cathode ray was attracted to positive charge, its particles had negative charge. We now call those particles **electrons**.

<BlockMath math="\text{cathode ray is attracted to positive charge }(+) \Rightarrow \text{particles carry negative charge }(-)" />

Thomson also found that cathode ray particles were far lighter than atoms and had the same properties even when the electrode metal changed. The unchanged properties showed that electrons were not fragments of one particular metal. They were common components of atoms.

To picture Thomson's model, imagine plum pudding. The pudding represents spread-out positive charge, while the plums represent embedded negative electrons. This picture explains the parts of Thomson's model. It does not show an atom's real shape. Rutherford later improved the model.

## A Tiny Dense Nucleus

Rutherford's Manchester team tested Thomson's model. At Rutherford's direction, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired positively charged alpha particles at very thin metal foils, including gold. If positive charge were spread out evenly as Thomson's model suggested, almost all alpha particles should pass through with only small bends. [American Institute of Physics](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html) records their experimental roles and Rutherford's later interpretation.

A small fraction of the observations contradicted Thomson's prediction:

- most alpha particles passed through the gold foil with little change in direction
- a small fraction bent
- a very tiny fraction bounced backward

OpenStax explains that this pattern led Rutherford to two conclusions: atoms are mostly empty space, and positive charge is concentrated in a tiny, relatively heavy center. That small center is called the **atomic nucleus**.

To picture the size, imagine the nucleus as a small berry in the middle of a stadium. The atom would be about the size of the stadium. [OpenStax Chemistry Atoms First 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-atoms-first-2e/pages/2-3-atomic-structure-and-symbolism) states that an atom's diameter is about <InlineMath math="10^{-10}\ \text{m}" />, while the nucleus is about <InlineMath math="10^{-15}\ \text{m}" /> across, roughly <InlineMath math="10^5" /> times smaller.

## Hydrogen Nuclei Identified the Proton

The gold-foil evidence located the atom's positive charge, but it did not yet identify the particles inside the nucleus. In <InlineMath math="1919" />, Rutherford bombarded nitrogen with alpha particles and detected fast hydrogen nuclei coming out. A hydrogen nucleus carries one positive elementary charge and is now called a **proton**.

[Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1990/9592-the-nobel-prize-in-physics-1990/) distinguishes these steps. Rutherford interpreted the nuclear atom in <InlineMath math="1911" /> and demonstrated free protons in <InlineMath math="1919" />. Discovering the nucleus and identifying the proton were related, but they were not the same event.

## Neutrons Explain the Remaining Mass

Once protons explained the nucleus's positive charge, there was still a gap: atomic masses were often larger than the mass of the protons alone. What added mass to the nucleus without adding charge?

In <InlineMath math="1932" />, James Chadwick showed evidence for a neutral particle with almost the same mass as a proton. That particle is called the **neutron**. Neutral means its charge is <InlineMath math="0" />, so neutrons can add mass to a nucleus without changing the atom's electric charge.

[Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1935/chadwick/facts/) records that James Chadwick proved radiation from beryllium hit by alpha particles consisted of neutral particles with about the same mass as protons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. The element stays the same because the proton number stays fixed, while the mass changes with the neutron number.

## Comparing the Three Subatomic Particles

This table compares each particle's charge, main location, and contribution to atomic mass.

| Particle | Main location | Relative charge | Relative mass | Role in the atom |
| :------- | :------------ | :-------------- | :------------ | :--------------- |
| Electron | Space around the nucleus | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | About <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{1800}" /> of a proton's mass | A negative charge marker in atomic space |
| Proton | Nucleus | <InlineMath math="+1" /> | About <InlineMath math="1\ \text{u}" /> | The particle that determines element identity |
| Neutron | Nucleus | <InlineMath math="0" /> | About <InlineMath math="1\ \text{u}" /> | Mass inside the nucleus without extra charge |

A neutral atom has the same number of protons and electrons. For example, if an atom has <InlineMath math="6" /> protons and is still neutral, it also has <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons.

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow \text{number of } p^+ = \text{number of } e^-" />

Remember these three relationships: **protons determine element identity**, **neutrons help determine mass**, and **electrons determine many chemical behaviors**. Use them together when comparing atomic charge, mass, and identity.
