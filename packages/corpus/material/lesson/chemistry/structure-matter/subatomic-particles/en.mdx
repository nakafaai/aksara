## Evidence for Subatomic Particles

Dalton's model explained fixed ratios in chemical reactions, but it still pictured atoms as solid spheres. Electrical experiments later showed that atoms contain smaller charged particles.

In the late 1800s, scientists began using low-pressure glass tubes and high voltage. A beam called a **cathode ray** appeared inside the tube. The beam could be bent by electric and magnetic fields. From this evidence, atoms started to look less like solid balls and more like structures with smaller particles inside.

> Every subatomic particle was uncovered by firing a beam or electric field at matter and tracking how it bent, bounced, or passed straight through.

The evidence sequence runs from Thomson's cathode ray experiment through Rutherford's gold foil experiment to Chadwick's discovery of the neutron. J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in <InlineMath math="1897" /> and showed that this particle was far lighter than an atom.

Each experiment answered one question and opened the next. Dalton pictured solid spheres in 1803, Thomson found the electron in 1897, Rutherford's team found a tiny dense nucleus in 1911, hydrogen nuclei were identified as protons in 1919, and Chadwick found the neutron in 1932. Zoom along the line and notice how fast the last three came.

## Cathode Rays Identified Electrons

Thomson used a cathode ray tube. Inside the tube, most air was removed, then a high voltage was applied. The beam that appeared was always attracted toward positive charge and pushed away from negative charge.

Opposite charges attract, while like charges repel. Because the cathode ray was attracted to positive charge <InlineMath math="+" />, its particles had negative charge <InlineMath math="-" />. We now call those particles **electrons**.

Thomson also found that cathode ray particles were far lighter than atoms and had the same properties even when the electrode metal changed. The unchanged properties showed that electrons were not fragments of one particular metal. They were <Highlight>common components of atoms</Highlight>.

In Thomson's model, positive charge is spread throughout the atom and electrons are embedded in that distribution. The model accounts for electrons, but it does not contain a small, dense nucleus. Rutherford's experiment later revealed that limitation.

### Bending the Electron Beam

Thomson placed two charged plates inside a cathode ray tube. Reversing the plate voltage reverses the bend, and **doubling the plate voltage doubles the spot shift toward the positive plate**. Measuring that proportional shift let Thomson determine the electron's charge-to-mass ratio.

## A Tiny Dense Nucleus and the Proton

Rutherford's Manchester team tested Thomson's model. At Rutherford's direction, [Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html) fired positively charged alpha particles at very thin metal foils, including gold. If positive charge were spread out evenly as Thomson's model suggested, almost all alpha particles should pass through with only small bends.

A small fraction of the observations contradicted Thomson's prediction:

- most alpha particles passed through the gold foil with little change in direction
- a small fraction bent
- a very tiny fraction bounced backward

Now connect those observations to the location of positive charge inside the atom. Because almost all alpha particles passed straight through, most of the atom must be empty space. The few particles that bent sharply or bounced back showed that the positive charge and most of the atom's mass are packed into a very small region. That region is called the **atomic nucleus**.

To picture the scale, imagine enlarging an atom until it is as wide as a <InlineMath math="100\ \text{m}" /> stadium. Its nucleus would be only about <InlineMath math="1\ \text{mm}" /> across, roughly the size of a grain of sand at the center. A real atom is about <InlineMath math="10^{-10}\ \text{m}" /> across, while its nucleus is about <InlineMath math="10^{-15}\ \text{m}" /> across. The nuclear diameter is therefore about <InlineMath math="10^5" /> times smaller than the atomic diameter.

### Alpha Particles Bounce Back

For a head-on alpha particle aimed straight at a gold nucleus, electric repulsion brings it to a momentary stop before pushing it backward. **A faster alpha particle gets closer to the nucleus** before turning around, giving an upper bound on how small the gold nucleus must be.

### Hydrogen Nuclei Identified the Proton

The gold-foil evidence located the atom's positive charge, but it did not yet identify the particles inside the nucleus. In <InlineMath math="1919" />, Rutherford bombarded nitrogen with alpha particles and detected fast hydrogen nuclei coming out. A hydrogen nucleus carries one positive elementary charge and is now called a **proton**.

Rutherford interpreted the nuclear atom in <InlineMath math="1911" /> and demonstrated [free protons](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1990/9592-the-nobel-prize-in-physics-1990/) in <InlineMath math="1919" />. The two steps are related but distinct events.

## Neutron Mass and Charge

Protons explained the nucleus's positive charge, but the mass of the protons alone could not explain the full atomic mass. Scientists still needed to find another particle that added mass without adding charge.

In <InlineMath math="1932" />, James Chadwick showed evidence for a neutral particle with almost the same mass as a proton. That particle is called the **neutron**. Neutral means its charge is <InlineMath math="0" />, so neutrons can add mass to a nucleus without changing the atom's electric charge.

[Chadwick](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1935/chadwick/facts/) showed that radiation from beryllium bombarded with alpha particles consisted of neutral particles with about the same mass as protons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. The element stays the same because the proton number stays fixed, while the mass changes with the neutron number.

## Comparing the Three Subatomic Particles

This table compares each particle's charge, main location, and contribution to atomic mass.

| Particle | Main location | Relative charge | Approximate mass | Role in the atom |
| :------- | :------------ | :-------------- | :------------ | :--------------- |
| Electron | Space around the nucleus | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | About <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{1800}" /> of a proton's mass | Carries negative charge and participates in chemical reactions |
| Proton | Nucleus | <InlineMath math="+1" /> | About <InlineMath math="1\ \text{u}" /> | The particle that determines element identity |
| Neutron | Nucleus | <InlineMath math="0" /> | About <InlineMath math="1\ \text{u}" /> | Mass inside the nucleus without extra charge |

Whenever an atom carries zero net charge, its proton count matches its electron count exactly. A neutral carbon atom with <InlineMath math="6" /> protons, for instance, also holds <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \text{neutral atom} &amp;\Rightarrow \text{number of } p^+ \\ &amp;= \text{number of } e^- \end{aligned}" />

Remember these three relationships: **protons determine element identity**, **neutrons help determine mass**, and **electrons determine many chemical behaviors**. Use them together when comparing atomic charge, mass, and identity.
