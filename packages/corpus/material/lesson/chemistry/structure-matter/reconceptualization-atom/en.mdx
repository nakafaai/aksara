## Atoms as Counting Units in Chemistry

Before John Dalton, atoms had already been imagined as the smallest pieces of matter. The idea was still philosophical and had not produced a model that could calculate experimental results.

Dalton could not see atoms directly, so he studied the masses of substances before and after reactions. From those measurements, he developed a model that explained <Highlight>why compounds have fixed compositions</Highlight> and why their masses often form simple ratios.

> Dalton changed how scientists understood atoms. He treated them as counting units that explain the mass and composition of substances in a chemical reaction.

Dalton developed a scientific atomic theory for chemistry in the early 1800s.

## Five Claims in the Dalton Model

Dalton's model is often called the solid sphere model. Atoms were imagined as tiny solid balls that could not be divided, differed by element, and joined with other atoms in definite numbers.

| Dalton's claim | Meaning |
| :------------- | :-------------------------------- |
| Matter is made of atoms. | Matter can be described as a collection of extremely small particles. |
| Atoms of the same element are treated as identical. | In this model, two carbon atoms are assumed to have the same mass and properties. |
| Atoms do not turn into atoms of another element in ordinary chemical reactions. | A chemical reaction rearranges the atoms that are already present. |
| Compounds form from atoms in definite ratios. | Water is written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> because the atom ratio is <InlineMath math="H:O = 2:1" />. |
| Two elements can form more than one compound. | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> both contain carbon and oxygen, but their ratios differ. |

Dalton treated **atoms as discrete units** that combine in whole-number ratios. When the same two elements form different compounds and the mass of one element is fixed, the masses of the other element can therefore have a simple whole-number ratio.

## Carbon and Oxygen in the Law of Multiple Proportions

Take carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. Both contain carbon and oxygen, but the number of oxygen atoms is different.

| Compound | Formula | Atom ratio | Relative O mass when C is fixed |
| :------- | :------ | :--------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Carbon monoxide | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> | <InlineMath math="C:O = 1:1" /> | <InlineMath math="16" /> parts |
| Carbon dioxide | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="C:O = 1:2" /> | <InlineMath math="32" /> parts |

Here, **parts** means comparison units. The ratio stays meaningful with any consistent unit.

If the carbon mass is kept the same, the oxygen masses in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> form this ratio:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\frac{m_O \text{ in } \mathrm{CO}}{m_O \text{ in } \mathrm{CO_2}}
&= \frac{16}{32} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}" />

Because <InlineMath math="1:2" /> is a clean integer ratio, Dalton read it as proof that carbon combines with whole oxygen atoms rather than arbitrary fractions of oxygen mass.

With carbon fixed at <InlineMath math="12\ \mathrm{g}" />, carbon monoxide holds <InlineMath math="16\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen and carbon dioxide <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" />, one grid step for <InlineMath math="8\ \mathrm{g}" />. The second bar is exactly twice the first, which Dalton read as one oxygen atom against two.

## Limits of the Dalton Model

Dalton's model explained the mass patterns in chemical reactions, but it could not explain every observation. If atoms were solid spheres with no inner parts, why could metals conduct electricity? Why could electric and magnetic fields bend cathode rays?

J. J. Thomson's cathode ray experiments revealed a **negatively charged particle** much smaller than an atom. We now call that particle the electron. This means atoms are not completely indivisible as Dalton assumed.

Dalton assumed that all atoms of the same element had the same mass. That assumption could not explain isotopes, which have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. The discovery of subatomic particles explains why atoms of one element can have different masses.

Dalton's model explains conservation of mass, fixed composition, and multiple proportions. It cannot explain electric charge, isotopes, or the internal structure of atoms. Those later observations require a model that includes subatomic particles.

Dalton's solid spheres cannot explain alpha particles that bounce back from gold foil. When all positive charge sits in a tiny nucleus, an alpha particle passing at impact parameter <InlineMath math="b" /> deflects by the angle <InlineMath math="\theta" /> given below, where <InlineMath math="b_0" /> depends on energy and charge.

<BlockMath math="\theta = 2 \arctan\left(\frac{b_0}{b}\right)" />

### Thomson Finds the Electron

The first crack in Dalton's solid atom came from a glowing tube. **The beam bends toward the positive plate**, so it carries negative charge, and Thomson called its particles electrons.

### Rutherford Finds a Nucleus

Dalton's model treated the atom as a solid ball. Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold. Most particles passed almost straight through, but a few turned sharply and some even bounced back. Rutherford concluded that **the atom's mass and positive charge sit in a very small nucleus**, about twenty thousand times smaller than the atom.

### From Orbits to Probability Clouds
