## Information in an Atomic Symbol

An atomic symbol records three pieces of information in a compact form, the **element symbol**, **atomic number**, and **mass number**. Together, they identify the element and state the numbers of protons and nucleons in the atom.

The atomic number is the number of protons. The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons.

The notation commonly used in basic chemistry is:

<BlockMath math="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}" />

Read it like this:

- <InlineMath math="\mathrm{X}" /> is the element symbol
- <InlineMath math="Z" /> is the atomic number
- <InlineMath math="A" /> is the mass number

## Counting Protons Electrons and Neutrons

The atomic number is written as <InlineMath math="Z" />. This number equals the number of protons in the nucleus.

<BlockMath math="Z = p^+" />

For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. Neutral means the total positive and negative charges balance.

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow e^- = p^+ = Z" />

The mass number is written as <InlineMath math="A" />. This number counts protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

<BlockMath math="A = p^+ + n^0" />

Once the mass number and atomic number are known, the neutron count is just their difference:

<BlockMath math="n^0 = A - Z" />

## Carbon Example

For a carbon atom with mass number <InlineMath math="12" />, the symbol is written:

<BlockMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" />

From that symbol, we read:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
Z &= 6 \Rightarrow p^+ = 6 \\
A &= 12 \Rightarrow p^+ + n^0 = 12 \\
n^0 &= 12 - 6 = 6
\end{aligned}" />

If that <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> atom is neutral, it also has <InlineMath math="6" /> electrons.

## Mass Number and Relative Atomic Mass

On the periodic table, you often see decimal values such as carbon's relative atomic mass of about <InlineMath math="12.01" />. That decimal is not the mass number of one specific carbon atom.

An element's relative atomic mass depends on isotope masses and isotopic composition. That composition can vary with a material's origin and treatment. [NIST](https://www.physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Compositions/notes.html)

So keep these two ideas separate:

| What you read | Common form | Meaning |
| :------------ | :---------- | :------ |
| Mass number | A whole number, such as <InlineMath math="12" /> in <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> | The number of protons plus neutrons in one specific atom. |
| Relative atomic mass | Often decimal, such as about <InlineMath math="12.01" /> for carbon | An isotope-composition-weighted mass for the material being represented. |

When reading an atomic symbol, track three facts: atomic symbols give proton and neutron counts, ions change the electron count, and isotopes change the neutron count. The proton count still determines the element's identity.

## Capitalization of Element Symbols

Element symbols usually have one or two letters. The first letter is always uppercase, and the second letter, if present, is lowercase.

| Writing | How to read it |
| :------ | :------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | Carbon |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl}" /> | Chlorine |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Co}" /> | Cobalt |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> | Formula for carbon monoxide, a compound made of carbon and oxygen |

Look closely at the last two rows. In <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Co}" />, the lowercase <InlineMath math="\mathrm{o}" /> joins the uppercase <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> to form one element symbol: cobalt. If the second letter becomes uppercase, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> is read as two element symbols, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" />, in the formula for carbon monoxide. Uppercase and lowercase letters therefore tell you which substance the formula names.

When you want to check the latest element symbols and relative atomic masses, open the [IUPAC Periodic Table of Elements](https://iupac.org/what-we-do/periodic-table-of-elements/).
