## Atomic Number Sets the Order

Each box in the modern periodic table represents one element. The elements are arranged by increasing **atomic number**, written as <InlineMath math="Z" />. The atomic number is the number of protons in an atom's nucleus.

Increasing atomic number <InlineMath math="Z" /> produces repeating patterns in chemical properties.

> The periodic table is a map of proton counts where rows track shells and columns track outer electrons.

## Mass Is Not the Order

One element can have atoms with different mass numbers because its isotopes carry different numbers of neutrons. That is why **the table is ordered by proton number, not by mass**: every isotope of boron, carbon, or neon stays in the same box because its proton count never changes.

## Periods and Groups

A **period** is a horizontal row. Periods are read from left to right and numbered <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="7" />. In the simple shell model, elements in the same period have the same number of main occupied shells.

A **group** is a vertical column. Groups are read from top to bottom and numbered <InlineMath math="1" /> to <InlineMath math="18" />. In the main groups, the number of valence electrons is often the same, so chemical properties tend to be similar.

Elements in one group are not identical. In the main groups, similar numbers and arrangements of valence electrons often produce similar reaction patterns.

Count the elements in each period of the modern table and a pattern appears: <InlineMath math="2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32" />. Each column below is one period, one grid step for <InlineMath math="4" /> elements. The jumps to <InlineMath math="18" /> and <InlineMath math="32" /> are where the transition metals and then the lanthanides and actinides join.

A metal from group <InlineMath math="1" /> and a halogen from group <InlineMath math="17" /> always meet one to one, because **each group forms ions of opposite unit charge**. Moving down those groups keeps the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MX}" /> formula identical while steadily widening the crystal unit cell from <InlineMath math="\mathrm{LiF}" /> to <InlineMath math="\mathrm{KCl}" />.

### One Group Reacts Alike

## Colors Mark Element Regions

The periodic table also divides elements into regions.

| Region | Typical location and behavior |
| :----- | :---------------------------- |
| Metals | Many are on the left and in the middle of the table. Many metals conduct electricity and tend to **lose electrons when forming ions**. |
| Nonmetals | Many are in the upper-right part of the table. Many nonmetals tend to gain or share electrons when bonding. |
| Metalloids | They sit at the boundary between metals and nonmetals. Their properties are in between, for example silicon can be used as a semiconductor. |
| Noble gases | They are in group <InlineMath math="18" />. These elements are very unreactive because their outer electron arrangement is stable. |

Lists of elements classified as metalloids can differ among references because some elements have overlapping metallic and nonmetallic properties. Metalloids occupy the transition area between metals and nonmetals.

## Why Two Rows Sit Below

If the lanthanides and actinides were drawn in the middle of the main table, the periodic table would become very wide. They are placed below **to keep the table readable**. They still occupy positions within the periodic system.

Lanthanides are still part of period <InlineMath math="6" />, while actinides are still part of period <InlineMath math="7" />.
