## Position and Nuclear Attraction

An element's position in the periodic table gives clues about atomic size, how easily an electron is removed, and how strongly the atom pulls electrons in a bond.

These trends depend on **nuclear attraction toward valence electrons**. Valence electrons are electrons in the outer shell. When the number of protons increases, the nuclear pull usually becomes stronger. When the number of shells increases, valence electrons are farther from the nucleus and feel a weaker pull.

## Four Periodic Properties

Each property describes a different aspect of an atom. The trend arrows describe changes in atomic size, the energy needed to remove an electron, the energy change when an electron is added, and the attraction of shared electrons in a bond.

- **Atomic radius** measures atomic size. Atoms do not have hard surfaces, so the radius is inferred from distances between nuclei using a stated measurement model.
- **First ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove the first electron from an isolated neutral atom in the gas phase.
- **Electron affinity** is the energy change when an electron is added to an isolated atom in the gas phase.
- **Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a bond.

The difference between electron affinity and electronegativity lies in the electron's situation. Electron affinity describes the energy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom. Electronegativity describes the attraction of shared electrons within a bond.

## Trends Across Periods and Groups

Across a main-group series such as sodium through chlorine, proton number increases while <Highlight>valence electrons enter the same main shell</Highlight>. Shielding changes less than nuclear charge, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons generally increases. Atomic radius therefore tends to decrease, while first ionization energy and electronegativity tend to increase.

Down one group, the number of occupied shells increases. Valence electrons are farther from the nucleus, and inner electrons reduce the attraction they feel from the nucleus. This reduction is called **electron shielding**.

Two sign conventions are used for electron affinity. The [IUPAC Gold Book](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E01977) uses the energy released when an electron attaches, so favorable attachment has <InlineMath math="\mathrm{EA}>0" />. When the same process is written as a reaction-energy change, <InlineMath math="\Delta E=-\mathrm{EA}<0" />.

For chlorine, attaching an electron releases energy. We write <InlineMath math="\mathrm{EA}>0" /> for its positive electron affinity and <InlineMath math="\Delta E=-\mathrm{EA}<0" /> for the negative energy change of the reaction:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Cl(g)}+e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}" />

Noble gases such as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ar}" /> have stable valence-electron configurations, so they do not easily accept extra electrons. Electron affinity has more exceptions to a simple left-to-right trend than atomic radius or first ionization energy.

Here are both trends in real data. Across a period the nucleus gains protons while the outer shell stays the same, so it gets harder to pull an electron off and the first ionization energy climbs. At the start of the next period a new shell adds distance and shielding, and the energy drops sharply. Zoom in on the pink drop from neon to sodium.

### Reactivity across a Period and down a Group

A metal's reactivity follows its ionization energy. From sodium to magnesium across a period, **the ionization energy rises from <InlineMath math="496" /> to <InlineMath math="738\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}" />**, so magnesium barely reacts with cold water. Going down from magnesium to calcium in a group, the ionization energy falls and the reaction speeds up. Compare the three metals in the trough.

## Comparing Two Pairs of Elements

Compare elements **in the same period or group** so the reason is clear:

| Pair | What changes? | Predicted trend |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Na and Cl in period 3 | Both have outer electrons in shell M, but Cl has more protons and stronger effective nuclear attraction. | Cl has a smaller radius. Its first electron is harder to remove. |
| Li and Na in group 1 | Li's valence electron is in shell L, while Na's is in shell M. Na has an extra inner shell that contributes shielding. | Na has a larger radius. Its first electron is easier to remove. |

These comparisons explain general trends. For electron affinity or pairs such as Mg and Al, check the data and subshell arrangement because the general direction alone is insufficient.

### Halide Ions Grow down the Group

Radius grows down a group because more electron shells are added. Compare the crystals of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaF}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaCl}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaBr}" />. The cation is the same, but **the halide anion grows from <InlineMath math="\mathrm{F^-}" /> to <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Br^-}" />**, so the edge of the unit cell grows longer too.
