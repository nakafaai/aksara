## Three Properties That Distinguish Subatomic Particles

Atoms usually contain **protons**, **neutrons**, and **electrons**. Hydrogen-1 has no neutron. Their charge, mass, and location determine how each particle affects an atom.

Compare these three properties:

- **charge**, the electric sign of the particle
- **mass**, how much the particle contributes to atomic mass
- **location**, the region of the atom where the particle is found in the atomic model

> Charge decides which way a particle bends, mass decides how tightly it turns, and location decides whether it sets element identity or drives reactions.

Together, charge, mass, and location distinguish the three subatomic particles. The [2022 CODATA review](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/pdf/wall_2022.pdf) provides more precise values for these constants.

## Charge Comes in Whole Units

Millikan balanced tiny oil drops between two charged plates. When a drop hangs still, the electric force exactly equals its weight. Set the voltage until one drop stops, then read its charge. **The charge is always a whole-number multiple of <InlineMath math="e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}" />**, the charge of one electron.

## Charge Determines the Direction of Bending

Electric charge determines how a particle responds to an electric field. A proton is positive, an electron is negative, and a neutron has no total charge.

The proton and electron have the **same charge magnitude, but opposite signs**:

<BlockMath math="e = 1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}" />

That means:

- a proton has charge <InlineMath math="+e" />
- an electron has charge <InlineMath math="-e" />
- a neutron has charge <InlineMath math="0" />

In an electric field, a charged particle experiences a force described by:

<BlockMath math="\vec F = q\vec E" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="q" /> is the particle charge in coulombs, and <InlineMath math="E" /> is the electric field. If <InlineMath math="q" /> is positive, the force points with the field. If <InlineMath math="q" /> is negative, the force points the opposite way. If <InlineMath math="q = 0" />, the particle is not bent by a simple electric field.

An electric field exerts a force on charged particles. A proton is pushed along the field, an electron in the opposite direction, and a neutron is not deflected in this simple model.

Send three beams with the same kinetic energy <InlineMath math="K" /> between charged plates. After a distance <InlineMath math="x" />, a particle with charge <InlineMath math="q" /> in the field <InlineMath math="E" /> has moved sideways by <InlineMath math="y=\tfrac{qEx^2}{4K}" />. The electron and the proton bend by the same amount in opposite directions, and the neutron goes straight. Zoom in at the end of the plates.

## Mass and Acceleration

Subatomic particle masses are tiny, so we often use the **unified atomic mass unit**, written <InlineMath math="\text{u}" />. One <InlineMath math="\mathrm u" /> is <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{12}" /> of the mass of a neutral <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> atom in its ground state.

| Particle | Relative charge | Mass in u | Mass in g | Main location |
| :------- | :-------------- | :------------ | :------------ | :------------ |
| Proton | <InlineMath math="+1" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0073\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="1.67262 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}" /> | Nucleus |
| Neutron | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0087\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="1.67493 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}" /> | Nucleus |
| Electron | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | <InlineMath math="0.00055\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="9.10938 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{g}" /> | Space around the nucleus |

Protons and neutrons have nearly the same mass, while an electron is much lighter than either of them.

A simple comparison is:

<BlockMath math="\frac{m_p}{m_e} \approx \frac{1.67262\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm g}{9.10938\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm g}\approx1836" />

So one proton has about the same mass as <InlineMath math="1836" /> electrons. Protons and neutrons in the nucleus therefore provide almost all of an atom's mass.

Suppose the same force acts on a tennis ball and a bowling ball for the same amount of time. The tennis ball has less mass, so it undergoes a greater acceleration and a greater change in velocity. The balls represent only the difference in mass. Their shapes do not represent particle shapes. An electron responds more strongly than a proton to the same electric force because its mass is much smaller.

Here are the three masses in u at the same scale, one grid step for <InlineMath math="0.5\ \text{u}" />. Proton and neutron stand almost equally tall. The electron's column is there too, but at about <InlineMath math="1/1836" /> of a proton you can hardly see it, which is why the mass of an atom sits in its nucleus.

## Particle Location and Chemical Role

Protons and neutrons remain in the nucleus, while electrons occupy the surrounding region. That difference determines which particle controls **element identity, isotope mass, and chemical reactions**.

| Particle | Location | Common role in chemistry |
| :------- | :------- | :----------------------- |
| Proton | Nucleus | Determines element identity. |
| Neutron | Nucleus | Adds mass and distinguishes isotopes. |
| Electron | Space around the nucleus | Strongly affects reactions and ion formation. |

The number of protons determines the element. For example, an atom with <InlineMath math="6" /> protons is carbon. If the neutron number changes, the element is still carbon, but the isotope is different. If the electron number changes, the atom becomes an ion.

For a neutral atom, the number of protons and electrons is the same:

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow p^+ = e^-" />

This equation states that the total positive and negative charges balance. Proton and electron masses remain different.

## Using Particle Properties in Atomic Symbols

In an [atomic symbol](/en/subjects/chemistry/structure-matter/atom-symbol), atomic number, mass number, and ion charge connect particle properties with these particle counts:

- the number of protons gives the **atomic number**
- the number of protons plus neutrons gives the **mass number**
- the difference between protons and electrons gives the **ion charge**

Use the three relationships together. <Highlight>Proton count determines element identity</Highlight>, proton plus neutron count determines mass number, and the difference between proton and electron counts determines the atom or ion charge.
