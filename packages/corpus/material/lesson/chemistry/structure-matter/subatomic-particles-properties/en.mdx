## Charge Mass and Location

Atoms contain **protons**, **neutrons**, and **electrons**. Their charge, mass, and location determine how each particle affects an atom.

Compare these three properties:

- **charge**, the electric sign of the particle
- **mass**, how much the particle contributes to atomic mass
- **location**, the region of the atom where the particle is found in the atomic model

[OpenStax Chemistry: Atoms First 2e](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-atoms-first-2e/pages/2-3-atomic-structure-and-symbolism) provides a table of subatomic particle charge, mass, and location.

For more precise constants, [NIST](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/pdf/wall_2022.pdf) publishes the CODATA recommended values.

## Charge Determines the Direction of Bending

Electric charge determines how a particle responds to an electric field. A proton is positive, an electron is negative, and a neutron has no total charge.

The proton and electron have the same charge magnitude, but opposite signs:

<BlockMath math="e = 1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}" />

That means:

- a proton has charge <InlineMath math="+e" />
- an electron has charge <InlineMath math="-e" />
- a neutron has charge <InlineMath math="0" />

In an electric field, a charged particle experiences a force described by:

<BlockMath math="F = qE" />

The symbol <InlineMath math="q" /> is the particle charge, and <InlineMath math="E" /> is the electric field. If <InlineMath math="q" /> is positive, the force points with the field. If <InlineMath math="q" /> is negative, the force points the opposite way. If <InlineMath math="q = 0" />, the particle is not bent by a simple electric field.

An electric field can be pictured like wind that only charged objects feel. Protons and electrons both feel the push, but in opposite directions. A neutron is neutral, so in this simple model it goes straight.

## Mass and Acceleration

Subatomic particle masses are tiny, so we often use the **unified atomic mass unit**, written <InlineMath math="\text{u}" />. It is defined from the mass of a <InlineMath math="{}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}" /> atom.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Absolute mass | Main location |
| :------- | :-------------- | :------------ | :------------ | :------------ |
| Proton | <InlineMath math="+1" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0073\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="1.67262 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}" /> | Nucleus |
| Neutron | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0087\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="1.67493 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}" /> | Nucleus |
| Electron | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | <InlineMath math="0.00055\ \text{u}" /> | <InlineMath math="9.10939 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{g}" /> | Space around the nucleus |

Protons and neutrons have nearly the same mass, while an electron is much lighter than either of them.

A simple comparison is:

<BlockMath math="\frac{m_p}{m_e} \approx 1836" />

So one proton has about the same mass as <InlineMath math="1836" /> electrons. Protons and neutrons in the nucleus therefore provide almost all of an atom's mass.

Suppose the same force acts on a tennis ball and a bowling ball for the same amount of time. The tennis ball has less mass, so it undergoes a greater acceleration and a greater change in velocity. The balls represent only the difference in mass. Their shapes do not represent particle shapes. An electron responds more strongly than a proton to the same electric force because its mass is much smaller.

## Particle Location and Chemical Role

Protons and neutrons remain in the nucleus, while electrons occupy the surrounding region. That difference determines which particle controls element identity, isotope mass, and chemical reactions.

| Particle | Location | Common role in chemistry |
| :------- | :------- | :----------------------- |
| Proton | Nucleus | Determines element identity. |
| Neutron | Nucleus | Adds mass and distinguishes isotopes. |
| Electron | Space around the nucleus | Strongly affects reactions and ion formation. |

The number of protons determines the element. For example, an atom with <InlineMath math="6" /> protons is carbon. If the neutron number changes, the element is still carbon, but the isotope is different. If the electron number changes, the atom becomes an ion.

For a neutral atom, the number of protons and electrons is the same:

<BlockMath math="\text{neutral atom} \Rightarrow p^+ = e^-" />

This equation states that the total positive and negative charges balance. Proton and electron masses remain different.

## Using Particle Properties in Atomic Symbols

Atomic symbols place three pieces of information around the element symbol. On [Atomic Symbols](/en/subjects/chemistry/structure-matter/atom-symbol), each number has a specific meaning:

- the number of protons gives the **atomic number**
- the number of protons plus neutrons gives the **mass number**
- the difference between protons and electrons gives the **ion charge**

Use the three relationships together. Proton count determines element identity, proton plus neutron count determines mass number, and the difference between proton and electron counts determines the atom or ion charge.
