## Why Atoms Form Bonds

A **chemical bond** is the attraction holding atoms or ions together in a substance. Atoms bond to reach a stable **octet** of eight valence electrons or a **duplet** of two, matching a noble gas. A metal **transfers** electrons to a non-metal in an ionic bond, non-metals **share** electron pairs in a covalent bond, and metals form a **sea of electrons** in a metallic bond.

> Every bond type starts with which elements meet and how many valence electrons each atom carries.

Whenever you meet a compound, check the elements involved first. A metal paired with a non-metal forms an ionic bond, two non-metals form a covalent bond, and a metal alone forms a metallic bond. Checking the number of [valence electrons](/en/subjects/chemistry/structure-matter/valence-electron) in each atom tells you how many electrons move or get shared. Noble gases like neon (<InlineMath math="2.8" />) and argon (<InlineMath math="2.8.8" />) already have full outer shells, so they rarely react.

## Ionic Bonds from Electron Transfer

An **ionic bond** forms when a metal atom gives away its valence electrons and a non-metal atom accepts them. The metal atom turns into a positive **cation**, the non-metal atom turns into a negative **anion**, and strong **electrostatic attraction** pulls the opposite charges together.

Sodium and chlorine show the whole sequence clearly. Sodium (<InlineMath math="2.8.1" />) loses one electron to become <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Na^+}" /> with the arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8" />. Chlorine (<InlineMath math="2.8.7" />) gains that electron to become <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl^-}" /> with the arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8.8" />. Play the model and watch the metal's outer electron cross into the non-metal's outer shell while both particles change size.

Notice that the sodium ion becomes **smaller** than the sodium atom because an entire electron shell disappears, whereas the chloride ion becomes **larger** because the extra electron increases repulsion. Do not mistake sodium chloride for a shared-electron molecule. Because sodium is a metal and chlorine is a non-metal, electrons transfer completely rather than stay shared. Millions of these oppositely charged ions then pack into a rigid **crystal lattice** with no isolated molecules.

Because every ion is **locked at a fixed lattice site** in the solid state, solid sodium chloride cannot conduct electricity. Only after melting or dissolving in water can the ions move freely and carry charge.

### Formula of Magnesium Chloride

Build the **formula of the ionic compound** formed by magnesium (proton number <InlineMath math="12" />) and chlorine (proton number <InlineMath math="17" />).

Start from the electron arrangements. Magnesium is <InlineMath math="2.8.2" />, so it loses <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons. Chlorine is <InlineMath math="2.8.7" />, so each chlorine atom needs to gain just <InlineMath math="1" /> electron:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Mg} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mg^{2+}} + 2e^-" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Cl} + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl^-}" />
</MathContainer>

One magnesium atom releases two electrons, so two chlorine atoms are needed to accept both. That gives the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MgCl_2}" />. Check the total charge because every ionic compound must be electrically neutral:

<BlockMath math="(+2) + 2 \times (-1) = 0" />

The charges balance to zero, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}" /> reaches <InlineMath math="2.8" />, and each <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl^-}" /> reaches <InlineMath math="2.8.8" />.

### Formula of Magnesium Oxide

Magnesium (<InlineMath math="2.8.2" />) reacts with oxygen (<InlineMath math="2.6" />). Magnesium loses <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons and oxygen gains <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons, so **the ion ratio is one to one** and the formula is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MgO}" />.

<BlockMath math="(+2) + (-2) = 0" />

The charges in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MgO}" /> are <InlineMath math="+2" /> and <InlineMath math="-2" />, twice the magnitude of <InlineMath math="+1" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" /> in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaCl}" />. Stronger electrostatic attraction pulls the lattice tighter and raises the melting point dramatically, which shows up in the shorter unit cell edge of magnesium oxide in the lattice view above.

## Covalent Bonds from Shared Electrons

A **covalent bond** forms when two non-metal atoms **share a pair of electrons**. Both atoms attract electrons strongly, so neither gives electrons away completely. Each shared pair counts as one covalent bond, and the number of shared pairs matches how many electrons each atom still needs for a stable shell:

| Molecule | Electrons each atom needs | Type of bond |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> (for a duplet) | Single bond, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H{-}H}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | Double bond, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O{=}O}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | Triple bond, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N{\equiv}N}" /> |

## Hydrogen Bonds and Dative Bonds

Both hydrogen bonds and dative bonds grow out of **electron pairs and charge attraction**, yet they act at different scales. A hydrogen bond operates **between neighbouring molecules**, whereas a dative bond is a covalent bond inside a particle where both shared electrons originate from one atom.

### Hydrogen Bonds between Molecules

A **hydrogen bond** is the intermolecular attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to **nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine** and a lone pair on nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine in another molecule. Be careful not to call a bond between two hydrogen atoms a hydrogen bond. The molecule <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> contains an ordinary covalent bond, whereas a hydrogen bond requires a strongly electronegative atom pulling bonding electrons away from hydrogen so the hydrogen end turns slightly positive.

Hydrogen bonds lift boiling points sharply. In each pair below, the left molecule forms hydrogen bonds while the right molecule from the same group does not.

Hydrogen sulfide molecules are heavier than water molecules, yet hydrogen sulfide boils <InlineMath math="160^\circ\text{C}" /> lower. Sulfur is not electronegative enough to form hydrogen bonds, so <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2S}" /> molecules are held only by weak van der Waals forces. Water molecules cling together through **hydrogen bonds**, demanding far more heat energy to separate.

### Dative Bonds in the Ammonium Ion

A **dative bond** is a covalent bond in which **both electrons of the shared pair come from one atom**. The donor atom supplies a lone pair, and the receiving atom or ion offers an empty orbital with space for two electrons.

Ammonia carries one lone pair on its nitrogen atom, while a hydrogen ion <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> has no electrons at all. Nitrogen donates its lone pair to form the ammonium ion:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{NH_3} + \mathrm{H^+} \rightarrow \mathrm{NH_4^+}" />

Water forms the hydronium ion <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_3O^+}" /> the same way when oxygen donates one lone pair to <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" />. Once <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_4^+}" /> exists, all four <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N{-}H}" /> bonds have the same length and strength. The word dative records only how the pair arrived.

## Metallic Bonds in a Sea of Electrons

In a metal, every atom releases its valence electrons into a mobile pool. Those electrons are **delocalised**, meaning they move freely throughout the entire lattice as a sea of electrons. A **metallic bond** is the electrostatic attraction between that sea of delocalised electrons and the positively charged metal cations.

That mobile electron sea explains malleability and heat conduction as well. Layers of cations can slide past one another under a hammer without shattering the lattice, because the sea of electrons keeps binding the shifted layers together. Free electrons also transfer kinetic energy rapidly from a hot end to a cold end.

## Comparing Ionic and Covalent Compounds

Bond type controls physical properties because it decides **which attractions must be overcome** during melting or boiling. Melting an ionic solid requires overcoming strong ionic bonds throughout the lattice. Melting a simple covalent substance such as naphthalene does not break the strong covalent bonds inside each molecule. It only overcomes the weak **intermolecular forces** between molecules.

Compare the two classes side by side whenever you need to predict conductivity, solubility, or state at room temperature:

| Property | Ionic compound | Simple covalent compound |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Melting and boiling points | High | Low |
| Electrical conductivity | Conducts when molten or aqueous | Does not conduct in any state |
| Solubility | Usually soluble in water | Usually soluble in organic solvents |

Always ask **which particles move or separate**. Solid ionic compounds fail to conduct because their ions cannot move, whereas molten or dissolved ionic compounds conduct readily. Meanwhile, some covalent substances form **giant covalent structures** such as diamond and silicon dioxide, where continuous networks of covalent bonds push melting points far higher than those of simple molecules.

## Checking Formulas and Bond Explanations

Work through these four problems by **writing each atom's electron arrangement first**, then compare your steps against the worked answers right beneath them.

1. Aluminium (proton number <InlineMath math="13" />) reacts with oxygen (proton number <InlineMath math="8" />). Find the formula of the compound formed.

2. Explain how the bond in a nitrogen molecule, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2}" />, forms and state how many electron pairs are shared.

3. Element <InlineMath math="\mathrm{X}" /> has proton number <InlineMath math="19" /> and element <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Y}" /> has proton number <InlineMath math="17" />. State the bond type, the formula, and whether its aqueous solution conducts electricity.

4. Water has relative molecular mass <InlineMath math="18" /> and hydrogen sulfide has <InlineMath math="34" />. Explain why water boils at <InlineMath math="100^\circ\text{C}" /> while hydrogen sulfide boils at <InlineMath math="-60^\circ\text{C}" />.

**Answer 1**: Aluminium has arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8.3" />, so it loses <InlineMath math="3" /> electrons to form <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Al^{3+}}" />. Oxygen has arrangement <InlineMath math="2.6" />, so it gains <InlineMath math="2" /> electrons to form <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O^{2-}}" />. Balancing six transferred electrons requires two aluminium ions and three oxide ions, giving <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Al_2O_3}" />:

<BlockMath math="2 \times (+3) + 3 \times (-2) = 0" />

**Answer 2**: Nitrogen has arrangement <InlineMath math="2.5" />, so each atom needs <InlineMath math="3" /> electrons to reach an octet. Two nitrogen atoms share **three pairs of electrons** to form a triple covalent bond, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N{\equiv}N}" />, leaving each atom with six shared electrons plus one lone pair:

<BlockMath math="3 \times 2 + 2 = 8" />

**Answer 3**: Element <InlineMath math="\mathrm{X}" /> has arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8.8.1" />, so it is a metal that loses <InlineMath math="1" /> electron to become <InlineMath math="\mathrm{X^+}" />. Element <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Y}" /> has arrangement <InlineMath math="2.8.7" />, so it is a non-metal that gains <InlineMath math="1" /> electron to become <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Y^-}" />. They form an **ionic bond** with formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{XY}" /> because <InlineMath math="(+1) + (-1) = 0" />. In aqueous solution, the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{X^+}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Y^-}" /> ions move freely, so the solution **conducts electricity**.

**Answer 4**: Greater molecular mass usually strengthens van der Waals forces, yet water boils higher because oxygen is highly electronegative and forms **hydrogen bonds** between water molecules. Sulfur cannot form hydrogen bonds, so <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2S}" /> molecules are held only by weak van der Waals forces. Breaking hydrogen bonds requires extra heat energy, creating a boiling point gap of:

<BlockMath math="100^\circ\text{C} - (-60^\circ\text{C}) = 160^\circ\text{C}" />
