## Carbon Compounds and Homologous Series

A **carbon compound** is a compound that contains carbon. Organic compounds come from living things, and a **hydrocarbon** is an organic compound made of carbon and hydrogen only. Carbon compounds are arranged in **homologous series**: families with the same general formula, the same functional group, and a <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2}" /> difference between neighbouring members. Alkanes have the general formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}" />. In these notes you rotate molecular models, compare alkanes with alkenes, pick out isomers, and work out the molar mass of each series.

| Homologous series | General formula | Functional group | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Alkane | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}" /> | Single bond <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{-}C}" /> | Ethane, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_6}" /> |
| Alkene | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n}}" /> | Double bond <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> | Ethene, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_4}" /> |
| Alkyne | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n-2}}" /> | Triple bond | Ethyne, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_2}" /> |
| Alcohol | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}" /> | Hydroxyl, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-OH}" /> | Ethanol, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}" /> |
| Carboxylic acid | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}" /> | Carboxyl, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COOH}" /> | Ethanoic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH}" /> |
| Ester | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}COOC_mH_{2m+1}}" /> | Carboxylate, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COO-}" /> | Ethyl ethanoate |

## Alkanes and Alkenes React Differently

Both alkanes and alkenes burn in excess oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water. Alkenes burn with a **sootier flame**, because their percentage of carbon by mass is higher.

Try an example. Compare the percentage of carbon by mass in ethene and ethane. The relative atomic mass of C is <InlineMath math="12" /> and of H is <InlineMath math="1" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_4}: \frac{2 \times 12}{28} \times 100\% = 85.7\%" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_6}: \frac{2 \times 12}{30} \times 100\% = 80.0\%" />
</MathContainer>

Check: ethene has two fewer hydrogen atoms for the same number of carbons, so its carbon percentage is indeed higher, and more carbon is left unburnt as soot.

Alkanes only undergo **substitution reactions** with halogens under ultraviolet light, because all their bonds are single. Alkenes are **unsaturated** hydrocarbons, and the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> double bond allows **addition reactions**:

| Addition reaction | Reagent and conditions | Product from ethene |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hydrogenation | Hydrogen, nickel catalyst, <InlineMath math="180^\circ\text{C}" /> | Ethane |
| Halogenation | Bromine water | 1,2-dibromoethane, brown colour disappears |
| Hydration | Steam, phosphoric acid, <InlineMath math="300^\circ\text{C}" />, <InlineMath math="60\text{ atm}" /> | Ethanol |
| Oxidation | Acidified potassium manganate(VII) | Ethane-1,2-diol, purple colour disappears |

## Alcohols and Carboxylic Acids Form Esters

**Alcohols** have a hydroxyl group, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-OH}" />. Ethanol is made by **fermentation** of glucose by yeast or by hydration of ethene. Alcohols burn with a clean blue flame, are oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate(VI) to carboxylic acids, and are dehydrated by hot porcelain chips to alkenes.

**Carboxylic acids**, such as the ethanoic acid in vinegar, are weak acids. They react with metals, bases and carbonates like other acids. Carboxylic acids also react with alcohols, with concentrated sulfuric acid as a catalyst, to make sweet-smelling **esters**. This process is called **esterification**:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH} + \mathrm{C_2H_5OH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5} + \mathrm{H_2O}" />

An ester's name comes from the alcohol first, then the acid. Ethanol and ethanoic acid give **ethyl ethanoate**.

## Isomers and How to Name Them

**Isomers** are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. Hexane, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{14}}" />, has five isomers. A more branched chain makes the molecule more compact, so the forces between molecules are weaker and the boiling point is lower.

To name an isomer, **find the longest carbon chain** first, then number it from the end that gives the branches the smallest numbers. For 2-methylpentane, the longest chain has five carbon atoms, so the name ends in pentane, and the methyl group sits on the second carbon.

Try a calculation. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when <InlineMath math="8.6\text{ g}" /> of hexane burns completely. The molar mass of hexane is <InlineMath math="86\text{ g mol}^{-1}" /> and of carbon dioxide <InlineMath math="44\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{C_6H_{14}} + 19\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 12\mathrm{CO_2} + 14\mathrm{H_2O}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{C_6H_{14}}) = \frac{8.6}{86} = 0.10\text{ mol} \Rightarrow n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 6 \times 0.10 = 0.60\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.60 \times 44 = 26.4\text{ g}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the carbon atoms: each hexane molecule has six carbon atoms, and each carbon atom becomes one carbon dioxide molecule, so the mole ratio must be <InlineMath math="1 : 6" />.

## Common Mistakes about Carbon Compounds

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Alkanes react with bromine water. | Alkanes are saturated and don't undergo addition, so the brown colour stays. | Only a <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> bond decolourises bromine water. |
| Isomers have the same physical properties. | A different structural formula changes the forces between molecules, so boiling points differ. | Compare hexane with 2,2-dimethylbutane. |
| An ester's name starts with the acid. | The first part of the name comes from the alcohol, for example ethyl from ethanol. | Ethanol and ethanoic acid give ethyl ethanoate. |
| The longest chain is the one drawn straight. | The longest chain can bend through a branch. | Count the carbon atoms along every path from end to end. |
| Ethanol is oxidised to ethene. | Oxidation gives ethanoic acid. Ethene comes from dehydration. | Ask whether oxygen is added or water is removed. |

The first mistake comes up most often. Remember this test: **brown bromine water loses its colour only with an alkene**.

## Practice on Carbon Compounds

These questions move from general formulae to reactions and isomers. **Draw the structural formula** whenever a question involves isomers or functional groups.

1. Write the molecular formula of the alkane with eight carbon atoms and calculate its molar mass.

2. Describe a chemical test to tell hexane from hexene.

3. Name the ester formed from propanol and ethanoic acid, and write the equation.

4. Draw and name all the isomers of butane, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}" />.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Use the general formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}" /> with <InlineMath math="n = 8" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_8H_{18}}" />
<BlockMath math="M = 8 \times 12 + 18 \times 1 = 114\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

Check with <InlineMath math="14n + 2" />: <InlineMath math="14 \times 8 + 2 = 114" />. The same.

**Solution 2**: Add a few drops of **bromine water** to each liquid and shake. **Hexene decolourises** the brown bromine water because its double bond undergoes addition. Hexane **doesn't change** the brown colour, because all its bonds are single.

**Solution 3**: The alcohol part, propyl, comes first, and the acid part, ethanoate, second. The ester is **propyl ethanoate**:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH} + \mathrm{C_3H_7OH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COOC_3H_7} + \mathrm{H_2O}" />

Check the atoms: left <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_5H_{12}O_3}" />, right <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} = \mathrm{C_5H_{12}O_3}" />. Balanced.

**Solution 4**: Butane has **two isomers**. The first is a straight chain of four carbon atoms, **butane**. The second is a chain of three carbon atoms with a methyl group on the second carbon, **2-methylpropane**. Check: both are <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}" />, and there's no other arrangement, because a methyl group on the first carbon just lengthens the chain again.
