## Carbon Families and Homologous Trends

From cooking gas in a kitchen burner to the sweet scent of ripe pears, organic molecules owe their diversity to carbon's ability to form four strong covalent bonds and build long chains. A **carbon compound** is any compound containing carbon, and a **hydrocarbon** consists exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms.

Chemists organise millions of organic molecules into **homologous series**, families that share one general formula, carry the same **functional group**, and differ by exactly one <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2}" /> unit between consecutive members.

| Homologous series | General formula | Functional group | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Alkane | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}" /> | Single bond <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{-}C}" /> | Ethane, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_6}" /> |
| Alkene | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n}}" /> | Double bond <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> | Ethene, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_4}" /> |
| Alkyne | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n-2}}" /> | Triple bond <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{\equiv}C}" /> | Ethyne, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_2}" /> |
| Alcohol | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}" /> | Hydroxyl, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-OH}" /> | Ethanol, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}" /> |
| Carboxylic acid | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}" /> | Carboxyl, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COOH}" /> | Ethanoic acid, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH}" /> |
| Ester | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}COOC_mH_{2m+1}}" /> | Carboxylate, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COO-}" /> | Ethyl ethanoate |

### Molar Mass Growth Across Hydrocarbon Series

Every step up a homologous series inserts one <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_2}" /> group, raising the **molar mass** by exactly <InlineMath math="14\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />. Because every hydrocarbon series climbs at the same rate per carbon atom, their molar mass lines run strictly parallel, separated only by the two hydrogen atoms lost when moving from an alkane (<InlineMath math="14n + 2" />) to an alkene (<InlineMath math="14n" />) or an alkyne (<InlineMath math="14n - 2" />). ### Comparing Oxygenated and Hydrocarbon Molar Masses

Replacing one hydrogen atom on an alkane with a **hydroxyl group**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-OH}" />, keeps the hydrogen count unchanged while inserting one oxygen atom of mass <InlineMath math="16\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />. As a result, alcohols follow <InlineMath math="14n + 18" />, sitting a constant <InlineMath math="16\text{ g mol}^{-1}" /> above alkanes and <InlineMath math="18\text{ g mol}^{-1}" /> above alkenes at every carbon count. ## Boiling Points and Crude Oil Separation

Physical properties shift smoothly along any homologous series even though chemical reactivity stays governed by the functional group. As molecules grow longer and heavier, the **intermolecular forces** between neighbouring chains strengthen, demanding more thermal energy to pull molecules apart into a vapour.

### Alkane Boiling Point Progression

At room temperature, the smallest alkanes bounce freely as gases because their tiny electron clouds attract one another weakly. Once chains reach five carbon atoms, stronger attractions hold the molecules together as **volatile liquids**, so the **boiling point rises** steadily across the first six straight-chain alkanes below.

### Separating Crude Oil by Fractional Distillation

Raw **crude oil** is a dark mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons spanning from one-carbon gases to thick bitumen chains with dozens of carbons. Inside a **fractionating column**, a furnace vapourises the crude oil at the hot base, and the rising vapour cools steadily as it climbs through bubble-cap trays. ## Hydrocarbon Reactions Isomers and Ester Synthesis

Once molecules are separated by size, their **functional groups** take over and govern how they burn, react with halogens, oxidise, or condense into fragrances. Comparing saturated and unsaturated four-carbon molecules reveals how a single double bond transforms chemical behaviour.

### Saturated Alkanes Unsaturated Alkenes and Structural Isomers

Both alkanes and alkenes burn in excess oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water, yet alkenes burn with a **sootier flame** because their carbon percentage by mass is higher. Compare ethene (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_4}" />) with ethane (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_6}" />) using relative atomic masses <InlineMath math="12" /> for C and <InlineMath math="1" /> for H:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_4}: \frac{2 \times 12}{28} \times 100\% = 85.7\%" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_2H_6}: \frac{2 \times 12}{30} \times 100\% = 80.0\%" />
</MathContainer>

Because alkanes are **saturated**, they only undergo slow **substitution reactions** with halogens under ultraviolet light. Alkenes are **unsaturated**, so their <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C{=}C}" /> double bond opens readily for **addition reactions**:

| Addition reaction | Reagent and conditions | Product from ethene |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hydrogenation | Hydrogen, nickel catalyst, <InlineMath math="180^\circ\text{C}" /> | Ethane |
| Halogenation | Bromine water | 1,2-dibromoethane, brown colour disappears |
| Hydration | Steam, phosphoric acid, <InlineMath math="300^\circ\text{C}" />, <InlineMath math="60\text{ atm}" /> | Ethanol |
| Oxidation | Acidified potassium manganate(VII) | Ethane-1,2-diol, purple colour disappears |

The same four carbon atoms can also be wired into different **structural isomers**, compounds sharing one molecular formula but different carbon skeletons or double-bond positions. Butane (<InlineMath math="\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}" />) forms two isomers: straight-chain **butane** and branched **2-methylpropane** (putting a methyl group on carbon one merely extends the straight chain).

Branching packs a molecule more compactly, weakening intermolecular forces and lowering its boiling point, so isomers never share identical physical properties. When naming any isomer, always **trace the longest continuous carbon chain**, even if it bends through a branch, and number from the end giving substituents or the double bond the lowest locant.

### Alcohol Oxidation and Dehydration Pathways

**Alcohols** carry the hydroxyl functional group, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-OH}" />, which makes small members such as methanol, ethanol, and propanol completely miscible with water. Industry produces ethanol either by **fermentation** of glucose with yeast or by catalytic hydration of ethene with steam. Alcohols burn cleanly with a pale blue flame, making ethanol a practical biofuel.

Two contrasting laboratory reactions convert ethanol into completely different homologous series. Heating ethanol with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) performs **oxidation**, adding oxygen to produce **ethanoic acid** while turning the orange solution green. Passing ethanol vapour over hot porcelain chips performs **dehydration**, removing a water molecule to yield **ethene**. Learners often mix up these two pathways, so check what changes in the formula: oxidation adds oxygen to form a carboxylic acid, whereas dehydration removes water to form an alkene.

### Forming Sweet Esters Under Reflux

**Carboxylic acids**, such as ethanoic acid in household vinegar, are weak acids that react with metals, alkalis, and metal carbonates. When heated with an alcohol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst, a carboxylic acid undergoes reversible **esterification** to form water and a fragrant **ester** carrying the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{-COO-}" /> linkage:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH} + \mathrm{C_2H_5OH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5} + \mathrm{H_2O}" />

Watch the naming order carefully because reversing it is a frequent error: the **first word comes from the alcohol** (ethyl from ethanol, propyl from propanol) and the **second word comes from the carboxylic acid** (ethanoate from ethanoic acid).
