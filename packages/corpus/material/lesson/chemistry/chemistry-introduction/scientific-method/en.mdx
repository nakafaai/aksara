## Chemistry and the Scientific Method

**Chemistry** is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure and properties of matter, and the changes matter goes through. Chemists answer questions about matter with the **scientific method**: making observations, making inferences, identifying the problem, forming a hypothesis, controlling variables, carrying out an experiment, interpreting data and drawing a conclusion. In these notes, you test a hypothesis with a hydrogen gas model, read a real solubility graph, and see parallax error for yourself.

Those steps aren't a list to memorise. Each step answers a question raised by the one before it. An observation raises a question, a hypothesis offers a provisional answer, and a fair experiment tests that answer.

## Fields of Chemistry around You

Chemistry shows up in many fields. **Analytical chemistry** finds out what a substance contains, such as the concentration of a pollutant in river water. **Organic chemistry** studies carbon compounds such as medicines and plastics. **Biochemistry** studies reactions inside living cells. **Materials chemistry** creates new materials such as light alloys and ceramics.

Careers that need chemistry include pharmacist, chemical engineer, toxicologist, forensic scientist and food scientist. They all use the same scientific method, whether they're testing a new medicine or finding the cause of a fire.

## Telling Observations from Inferences

An **observation** is information collected with your senses or a measuring instrument, such as bubbles of gas coming off. An **inference** is an early interpretation or explanation of that observation, such as carbon dioxide being produced. Anyone can confirm an observation. An inference has to be tested.

## Variables and Hypotheses

A fair experiment changes **one** thing only. The **manipulated variable** is the factor you change on purpose. The **responding variable** is what you measure, because its value changes with the manipulated variable. The **fixed variables** are all the other factors, which have to stay the same. A **hypothesis** is a testable statement that links the manipulated variable to the responding variable.

Take one investigation. Magnesium ribbon is put into excess hydrochloric acid, and the hydrogen gas is collected:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Mg(s)} + 2\mathrm{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{MgCl_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}" />

The hypothesis: the greater the mass of magnesium, the greater the volume of hydrogen gas. From the equation, <InlineMath math="24\text{ mg}" /> of magnesium (<InlineMath math="0.001" /> mol) gives <InlineMath math="0.001" /> mol of hydrogen, which is <InlineMath math="24\text{ cm}^3" /> at room conditions. So the volume in <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^3" /> equals the mass in milligrams. Move the mass slider and check the hypothesis.

### Example Identifying the Variables

For the magnesium investigation, state all three kinds of variable and **one testable hypothesis**.

- Manipulated variable: the mass of magnesium ribbon.
- Responding variable: the volume of hydrogen gas collected.
- Fixed variables: the volume and concentration of hydrochloric acid, and the temperature.
- Hypothesis: the greater the mass of magnesium, the greater the volume of hydrogen gas given off.

Check the hypothesis with the model: when the mass doubles from <InlineMath math="24\text{ mg}" /> to <InlineMath math="48\text{ mg}" />, the volume also doubles from <InlineMath math="24\text{ cm}^3" /> to <InlineMath math="48\text{ cm}^3" />. The hypothesis is accepted.

### Example Writing a Conclusion

A conclusion states the relationship **the data show**, not the one you hoped for. The graph is a straight line through the origin, so the conclusion is: the volume of hydrogen gas is **directly proportional** to the mass of magnesium when the acid is in excess. The condition "acid in excess" matters. If the acid runs out first, the volume of gas stops increasing even if you keep adding magnesium.

## Safety and Handling of Chemicals

Chemical bottles carry hazard symbols. Know each symbol so you know how to handle the substance:

| Hazard symbol | Meaning | Example substances |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Flammable | Catches fire easily near a flame or heat | Ethanol, petrol |
| Toxic | Can cause harm or death if swallowed, inhaled or absorbed | Mercury, chlorine |
| Corrosive | Destroys skin and metals | Concentrated sulfuric acid, sodium hydroxide |
| Irritant | Makes skin or eyes itchy and sore | Dilute aqueous ammonia |
| Oxidising | Helps other substances burn more strongly | Potassium manganate(VII) |

Wear a **lab coat, safety goggles and gloves** when you handle chemicals. Handle volatile or strong-smelling substances in a **fume chamber**. If acid spills, cover the spill with sodium hydrogen carbonate before cleaning it up. If mercury spills, sprinkle sulfur powder over it. Chemical waste doesn't go down the sink but into a labelled waste container.

## Common Mistakes in Scientific Investigations

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Carbon dioxide being given off is an observation. | You can't see the type of gas. It's an inference until you test it with limewater. | Ask whether your senses or an instrument detect it directly. |
| Changing the mass of magnesium and the acid concentration together. | Two variables change, so you can't tell what caused the change in volume. | Make sure only one variable is manipulated. |
| Hypothesis: magnesium produces a gas. | That statement doesn't link two variables. | A hypothesis names the manipulated and responding variables. |
| Repeating readings removes parallax error. | Parallax error repeats in the same direction every time. | Fix your eye position, not the number of readings. |

The first mistake is the most common one in science process skill questions. **An observation is what you detect**, while **an inference is what you think caused it**.

## Practice on Introduction to Chemistry

These questions test science process skills, from observation to a calculation from a graph. **Name each variable precisely** in every answer.

1. When zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution, the blue colour of the solution fades and a brown solid forms. State one observation and one inference.

2. A student studies the effect of temperature on the time sugar takes to dissolve in water. State the manipulated variable, the responding variable and two fixed variables.

3. Using the magnesium model, what volume of hydrogen gas forms from <InlineMath math="72\text{ mg}" /> of magnesium with excess acid at room conditions?

4. A saturated potassium nitrate solution in <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" /> of water is cooled from <InlineMath math="80^\circ\text{C}" /> to <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />. Use the solubility chart to find the mass of crystals that form.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: An observation is something you detect directly, such as **the blue colour of the solution fading** or **a brown solid forming**. An inference is the interpretation, such as zinc displacing copper(II) ions to form brown copper metal. You can test that inference by filtering off the solid and checking its properties.

**Solution 2**: The water temperature is the manipulated variable because the student changes it. The time for the sugar to dissolve is the responding variable because it's measured. Fixed variables include the mass of sugar, the volume of water and the size of the sugar crystals. If one of them changes, the effect of temperature can't be separated out.

**Solution 3**: One mole of magnesium gives one mole of hydrogen.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Mg}) = \frac{0.072}{24} = 0.003\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0.003 \times 24{,}000 = 72\text{ cm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

Check with the model: move the slider to <InlineMath math="m = 72" /> and read <InlineMath math="72\text{ cm}^3" />.

**Solution 4**: From the chart, <InlineMath math="100\text{ g}" /> of water dissolves <InlineMath math="169\text{ g}" /> of potassium nitrate at <InlineMath math="80^\circ\text{C}" /> and <InlineMath math="63.9\text{ g}" /> at <InlineMath math="40^\circ\text{C}" />.

<BlockMath math="169 - 63.9 = 105.1\text{ g}" />

So <InlineMath math="105.1\text{ g}" /> of crystals form. Check: <InlineMath math="105.1 + 63.9 = 169" />.
