## Electrons Moving in Every Redox Reaction

Picture passing a coin from your hand into a friend's hand. Your hand loses a coin only because your friend's hand catches it at the exact same instant. Chemistry runs the same rule whenever electrons jump between particles.

A **redox reaction** is a reaction where **oxidation and reduction happen simultaneously**. **Oxidation** is the loss of electrons, and **reduction** is the gain of electrons, so neither change ever unfolds alone.

### Splitting a Reaction into Half Equations

Drop a clean strip of zinc into blue copper(II) sulfate solution. The zinc strip slowly eats away while reddish-brown solid copper coats the metal:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} + \mathrm{Cu(s)}" />

Break that overall change into two **half-equations** so the electron trail stands out clearly. Each zinc atom gives away two electrons and is **oxidised**, while each copper(II) ion captures those two electrons and is **reduced**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + 2e^-" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}" />
</MathContainer>

The particle that pulls electrons away from another substance is the **oxidising agent**, and the particle that donates electrons is the **reducing agent**. Here copper(II) ions act as the oxidising agent while zinc acts as the reducing agent. Learners often flip these two names because "oxidising" sounds like "being oxidised", so anchor the opposite rule early.

> Every oxidising agent takes electrons and becomes reduced itself, whereas the reducing agent surrenders electrons and becomes oxidised.

### Tracking Electrons with Oxidation Numbers

When covalent molecules react, electrons are shared rather than transferred cleanly, so chemists track them using **oxidation numbers**, the charge an atom would carry if every bond were purely ionic. A free element has oxidation number <InlineMath math="0" />, oxygen in most compounds is <InlineMath math="-2" />, hydrogen is <InlineMath math="+1" />, and all oxidation numbers in a neutral compound sum to <InlineMath math="0" />.

A rising oxidation number signals **oxidation**, while a falling oxidation number signals **reduction**. Follow this rule inside the blast furnace, where iron(III) oxide reacts with carbon monoxide:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Fe_2O_3} + 3\mathrm{CO} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe} + 3\mathrm{CO_2}" />

Call each unknown oxidation number <InlineMath math="x" /> and solve for iron and carbon on both sides:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}: 2x + 3(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = +3" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CO}: x + (-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = +2" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}: x + 2(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = +4" />
</MathContainer>

Iron drops from <InlineMath math="+3" /> to <InlineMath math="0" />, meaning iron(III) oxide is **reduced**. Carbon climbs from <InlineMath math="+2" /> to <InlineMath math="+4" />, meaning carbon monoxide is **oxidised** and serves as the reducing agent. Electron accounting matches too: two iron atoms gain six electrons while three carbon atoms lose six electrons.

Apply the same sum check to potassium manganate(VII), <InlineMath math="\mathrm{KMnO_4}" />, where potassium is <InlineMath math="+1" /> and each oxygen is <InlineMath math="-2" />:

<BlockMath math="(+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = +7" />

The Roman numeral in potassium manganate(VII) confirms that manganese carries an oxidation state of <InlineMath math="+7" />.

## Standard Electrode Potentials and Metal Ranking

The **standard electrode potential**, <InlineMath math="E^\circ" />, measures how strongly a species pulls electrons compared with the standard hydrogen electrode fixed at <InlineMath math="0.00\text{ V}" />. A **more negative** <InlineMath math="E^\circ" /> marks a metal that gives up electrons eagerly, making it a stronger reducing agent. Ordering metals from most negative to most positive produces the **electrochemical series**.

## Chemical Cells and Ion Flow Across the Salt Bridge

A **chemical cell**, or voltaic cell, turns a spontaneous redox reaction into steady electrical energy by placing the two half-reactions in separate beakers. Electrons must travel through an external wire, while a **salt bridge** lets ions migrate so neither beaker builds up an unbalanced electric charge.

### Reading Voltage from Two Metal Half Cells

In any metal pair, the metal with the **more negative** <InlineMath math="E^\circ" /> releases electrons and becomes the **negative terminal** (anode), where oxidation happens. Never assume the metal that gains mass is the negative terminal: copper deposits at the **positive terminal** (cathode) because its <InlineMath math="E^\circ" /> is more positive than zinc's. Watch electrons flow out of the shrinking zinc strip through the meter into the growing copper strip. The **standard cell voltage** is always the **difference** between the two electrode potentials, never their sum:

Test the formula on an iron and copper cell. Iron has <InlineMath math="E^\circ = -0.44\text{ V}" />, more negative than copper at <InlineMath math="+0.34\text{ V}" />, so **iron is the negative terminal** and oxidises while copper reduces:

<BlockMath math="E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (-0.44) = 0.78\text{ V}" />

Because iron sits between zinc and copper in the electrochemical series, its voltage of <InlineMath math="0.78\text{ V}" /> lands cleanly below the <InlineMath math="1.10\text{ V}" /> of a zinc and copper cell.

### Balancing Beaker Charges with a Salt Bridge

As zinc dissolves into <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}" /> ions, the anode beaker gains positive charge, while the cathode beaker loses positive charge as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}" /> ions plate out. Without a **salt bridge**, that charge imbalance halts electron flow within a fraction of a second. ## Forcing Redox Reactions Through Electrolysis

**Electrolysis** runs the chemical cell in reverse by using an external battery to force a non-spontaneous redox change. The battery pushes electrons onto the **cathode**, where cations are reduced, and pulls electrons out of the **anode**, where anions are oxidised. In aqueous electrolytes, water supplies competing <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions, and three factors decide the winner: **position in the electrochemical series**, **ion concentration**, and **electrode material**.

### Ion Concentration and Electrode Choice in Aqueous Cells

Compare dilute and concentrated sodium chloride solutions electrolysed with inert carbon electrodes. At both cathodes, **hydrogen ions** are reduced to hydrogen gas rather than sodium metal because sodium ions sit far too high in the electrochemical series to accept electrons in water. At the anode, however, high chloride concentration overrides the series position and switches the gas from oxygen to chlorine.

### Predicting Deposited Metal Mass from Electron Moles

When two electrolytic cells are wired **in series**, the exact same **moles of electrons** pass through both cells, yet they do **not** deposit equal moles of metal unless their ion charges match. Suppose <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol}" /> of electrons flows through a copper(II) sulfate cell and a silver nitrate cell in series, with relative atomic masses <InlineMath math="64" /> for copper and <InlineMath math="108" /> for silver.

Write the cathode half-equations first to read each electron-to-metal ratio:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}: \quad n(\mathrm{Cu}) = \frac{0.10}{2} = 0.050\text{ mol}, \quad m = 0.050 \times 64 = 3.2\text{ g}" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Ag^+} + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Ag}: \quad n(\mathrm{Ag}) = 0.10\text{ mol}, \quad m = 0.10 \times 108 = 10.8\text{ g}" />

## Metal Extraction and Sacrificial Rust Protection

The electrochemical series governs real-world metallurgy and corrosion control too. Metals less electropositive than carbon are extracted by **reduction with carbon**, as seen when carbon monoxide reduces iron(III) oxide inside a blast furnace. Metals more electropositive than carbon, such as aluminium, cling too tightly to oxygen for carbon to strip away, so they are extracted by **electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide**.

**Rusting** is a miniature redox cell driven by oxygen and water on an iron surface. Beneath the centre of a water droplet, iron oxidises to <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}" />, while at the air-rich rim, dissolved oxygen reduces to hydroxide ions:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Fe} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe^{2+}} + 2e^-" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{O_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\mathrm{OH^-}" />
</MathContainer>

Those ions combine into iron(II) hydroxide, which oxidises further into flaky brown rust, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O}" />. Engineers stop ships and buried steel pipes from rusting through **sacrificial protection**: bolting a block of a **more electropositive metal** such as zinc (<InlineMath math="-0.76\text{ V}" />) onto an iron hull (<InlineMath math="-0.44\text{ V}" />).

> Even when paint scratches down to bare steel, a bolted zinc block oxidises first and sends protective electrons into the iron so the hull cannot rust.

As water and oxygen touch the scratched metal, zinc surrenders electrons preferentially:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + 2e^-" />

Those electrons travel into the iron and reduce oxygen on its surface, preventing iron atoms from losing electrons or forming <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}" /> ions. Over time the zinc block dissolves away and is simply replaced while the steel hull stays intact.
