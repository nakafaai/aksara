## Pure Compounds Have a Fixed Mass Ratio

The law of constant composition states that every pure compound contains its constituent elements in a <Highlight>fixed mass ratio</Highlight>. It is also called the law of definite proportions or Proust's law.

The law of constant composition applies to **pure compounds**. The composition of a mixture such as sugar water changes when the amount of sugar or water changes. Pure water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in the same mass ratio. A [chemical substance](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01039/plain) has a constant composition and characteristic properties.

## Water Keeps the Same Ratio

The formula for water is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. That means each molecule of water contains <InlineMath math="2" /> hydrogen atoms and <InlineMath math="1" /> oxygen atom. Using the simple relative atomic masses <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(H)} = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)} = 16" />, the hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio in water is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{H}} : m_{\mathrm{O}}
  &= (2 \times 1) : (1 \times 16) \\
  &= 2 : 16 \\
  &= 1 : 8
\end{aligned}" />

Even when different amounts of water form, the **mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen that reacts** stays <InlineMath math="1:8" /> with the rounded values used above. [Water](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/water/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. The <InlineMath math="2024" /> [abridged standard atomic weights](https://www.ciaaw.org/abridged-atomic-weights.htm) are <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(H)}=1.0080" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)}=15.999" />. Substituting those two atomic weights gives the following molar mass:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} M(\mathrm{H_2O}) &amp;=2(1.0080)+15.999 \\ &amp;=18.015\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \end{aligned}" />

Compare two samples of water, with hydrogen in violet and oxygen in orange and one grid step for <InlineMath math="4\ \mathrm{g}" />. The large sample is twice as heavy, but both split the same way, about <InlineMath math="1:8" /> by mass. That fixed split is what constant composition means.

## Testing Calcium Oxide Data

The next two experiments test the law of constant composition with calcium oxide. [Calcium oxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/calcium%20oxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula unit <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaO}" />. The CIAAW values <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(Ca)}=40.078" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)}=15.999" /> give <InlineMath math="M(\mathrm{CaO})=56.077\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}" />, which rounds to <InlineMath math="56.08\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}" />.

Suppose two experiments give the following data.

| Trial | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaO}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ca}" /> | Ratio <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Ca:O}" /> |
| :---- | :----------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2.8\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="0.8\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="2.0\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="2.0:0.8 = 2.5:1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3.5\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="1.0\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="2.5\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="2.5:1.0 = 2.5:1" /> |

The calcium mass is the **compound mass minus the oxygen** mass.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{Ca,1}} &= 2.8 - 0.8 = 2.0\ \mathrm{g} \\
m_{\mathrm{Ca,2}} &= 3.5 - 1.0 = 2.5\ \mathrm{g}
\end{aligned}" />

The two experiments form different masses of compound. Both have the same calcium-to-oxygen mass ratio, <InlineMath math="2.5:1" />. This common ratio shows that the samples follow the law of constant composition.

## Calculating Mass from a Formula

[Iron(III) oxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/ferric%20oxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula unit <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}" />.

Using <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(Fe)} = 56" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)} = 16" />, the **mass ratio of iron to oxygen** is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{Fe}} : m_{\mathrm{O}}
  &= (2 \times 56) : (3 \times 16) \\
  &= 112 : 48 \\
  &= 7 : 3
\end{aligned}" />

If the mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}" /> is <InlineMath math="64\ \mathrm{g}" />, the total ratio parts are <InlineMath math="7 + 3 = 10" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{Fe}} &= \frac{7}{10} \times 64 = 44.8\ \mathrm{g} \\
m_{\mathrm{O}} &= \frac{3}{10} \times 64 = 19.2\ \mathrm{g}
\end{aligned}" />

<InlineMath math="64\ \mathrm{g}" /> of iron(III) oxide contains <InlineMath math="44.8\ \mathrm{g}" /> of iron and <InlineMath math="19.2\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen bonded together.

The law of multiple proportions describes cases in which the same two elements form more than one compound.
