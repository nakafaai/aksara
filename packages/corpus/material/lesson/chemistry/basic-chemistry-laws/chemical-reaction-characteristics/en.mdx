## New Substance Formation Indicates a Chemical Reaction

Not every visible change is a chemical reaction. Sugar dissolved in water is still sugar. Ice that melts is still water. Neither example forms a new substance.

A **chemical reaction** happens when starting substances change into new substances. The starting substances are called **reactants**, and the substances formed are called **products**.

<BlockMath math="\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}" />

> Look past surface changes and ask whether the atoms have formed a brand new substance with different properties.

Without analysis equipment, visible changes give helpful first hints that new products may have formed. Observers watch for gas bubbles, solid precipitates, unexpected color shifts, or temperature changes, then verify whether the chemical identity actually changed.

## Atomic Rearrangement and Macroscopic Clues

Every macroscopic sign comes from what happens at the **particle scale**. When old bonds break and new bonds form, the same atoms regroup into different molecules that interact differently with heat, light, and solvents.

### Atoms Form New Partners

Visible signs of a reaction, such as gas bubbles, appear because **atoms swap partners**. Hydrogen peroxide decomposition shows this shift clearly.

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{O_2}" />

Every hydrogen and oxygen atom stays counted, yet two reactant molecules split into three product molecules.

## Four Common Clues

**Gas formation** may appear as bubbles in a liquid or as gas leaving the mixture. Smoke can accompany combustion, but the US Environmental Protection Agency classifies smoke as airborne particulate matter, so smoke is not itself proof of a gaseous product. Never deliberately smell [an unknown substance](https://inchemistry.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/about/governance/committees/chemicalsafety/publications/safety-in-academic-chemistry-laboratories-students.pdf). Waft vapors only when specifically instructed.

**A precipitate** is a new solid formed from a solution. For example, two clear solutions can produce a white solid because their ions form a compound that does not dissolve well.

**A color change** can be a strong clue when no dye is added. The color changes because a new product can interact with light differently.

**An energy change** can feel warm or cold, or appear as light. Breaking the reactants' bonds requires energy, while forming product bonds releases energy. The overall reaction warms the surroundings when more energy is released than absorbed, and cools them when the reverse is true.

The key distinction between a chemical and physical change is <Highlight>whether a new substance forms</Highlight>.

A thermometer turns one clue into numbers. In two illustrative mixtures that start at room temperature, one warms up because the reaction releases energy, and the other cools down because it absorbs energy. A temperature change alone is still only a clue, so check for a new substance too. Zoom in on the first minutes, where each curve turns.

## Verifying New Products

Because physical changes can imitate bubbles, color shifts, or heat transfer, chemists never rely on a single surface clue. Instead, they cross-check multiple observations and test **whether a new chemical structure formed** before deciding that a reaction took place.

### Checking Multiple Signs of a Chemical Reaction

One sign is not always enough. Boiling water produces bubbles, but it is a **physical change** because the substance is still <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. Dissolved salt also looks different, but it can be recovered by evaporating the water.

The table shows how several events can be assessed from the signs that are observed.

| Event | Conclusion | Reason |
| :---- | :--------- | :----- |
| Ice melts | Physical change | The substance is still <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. |
| Paper burns | Chemical reaction | Gaseous products and ash form, while energy is released as heat and light. |
| Two clear solutions produce a solid | Likely chemical reaction | A precipitate appears that was not there before. |
| Red dye is mixed with water | Physical change | The dye spreads through the water and changes its color. The dye remains the same substance. |

To be sure, we need to check whether the products are truly different from the reactants. In the laboratory, that can be done by measuring mass, testing a gas, observing a precipitate, or analyzing composition.

## Reaction Evidence and Basic Chemical Laws

Conservation of mass applies to chemical and physical changes when all matter is counted. To determine a product's composition, we must **identify the substance formed**, rather than rely only on bubbles or a color change.

Take metal oxides as an example. Nickel can form one of its oxides with the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NiO}" />, and cobalt can also form one of its oxides with the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CoO}" />. Each formula says that a metal and oxygen combine in a definite ratio.

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{Ni} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NiO}" />

After the evidence confirms a chemical reaction, measure the masses or composition of the substances. The appropriate chemical law can then relate the measured reactants and products.
