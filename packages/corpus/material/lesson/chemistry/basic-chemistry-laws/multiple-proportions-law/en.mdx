## Same Two Elements Form Multiple Compounds

The law of multiple proportions applies when **the same two elements** form **more than one compound**. Hold the mass of one element fixed, then compare the corresponding masses of the other element. Those masses form a small whole-number ratio.

Suppose two compounds are both made from elements <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" />. To read Dalton's law, first hold the mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" /> fixed, then compare the mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" /> that combines with it.

For the same mass of element <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" />, let <InlineMath math="m_{B,1}" /> and <InlineMath math="m_{B,2}" /> denote the masses of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" /> in compounds <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />. Their ratio <InlineMath math="m_{B,2}/m_{B,1}" /> reduces to small whole numbers.

The mass of one element <Highlight>must be the same in both compounds</Highlight> before the other element's masses are compared. If both element masses change at once, the calculation cannot directly test whether the second element's mass ratio is a small whole number.

> Lock the mass of one element first. Only then do the masses of the second element line up in a clean whole-number ratio.

## How Extra Atoms Shift the Mass Ratio

When the same pair of elements builds two different compounds, adding one whole atom changes the partner mass in a fixed step. Holding the **reference element mass constant** reveals that jump directly across both interactive models and mass ladders below.

### Adding One Oxygen Atom at a Time

A second oxide often forms when the first molecule **picks up one more oxygen atom**. Per mole of nitrogen (<InlineMath math="14\ \mathrm{g}" />), turning <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO}" /> into <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" /> raises the oxygen mass from <InlineMath math="16\ \mathrm{g}" /> to <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" />, giving <InlineMath math="1:2" />. Per mole of sulfur (<InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" />), turning <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SO_2}" /> into <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SO_3}" /> adds the same <InlineMath math="16\ \mathrm{g}" /> step, lifting oxygen from <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> to <InlineMath math="48\ \mathrm{g}" />, or <InlineMath math="2:3" />.

## Worked Mass Ratio Comparisons

Applying Dalton's law to real formulas starts by matching one element's mass across every compound in the table. Once that **shared mass basis** is set, dividing the partner element's masses reveals clean whole-number ratios for two or even three related oxides.

### Water and Hydrogen Peroxide

[Water](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/water/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, while [hydrogen peroxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/hydrogen%20peroxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" />.

Using <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(H)} = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)} = 16" />, the hydrogen mass in the two formulas can be matched at <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" />.

| Compound | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> |
| :------- | :--------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="16\ \mathrm{g}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> |

Because the **hydrogen mass is already the same**, compare the oxygen masses directly.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{O\ in\ H_2O}} : m_{\mathrm{O\ in\ H_2O_2}} = 16:32 \\ &amp;= 1:2 \end{aligned}" />

Hydrogen peroxide carries exactly twice as much oxygen as water for the same <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> of hydrogen, which confirms Dalton's law directly.

### Dinitrogen Monoxide and Nitrogen Dioxide

Nitrogen and oxygen can form several compounds. Two examples are [dinitrogen monoxide, or nitrous oxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/nitrous%20oxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON), <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" />, and [nitrogen dioxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/nitrogen%20dioxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON), <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" />.

Using the rounded relative atomic masses <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{N})=14" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{O})=16" />, compare **two moles** of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" /> with **one mole** of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" />. The table notation <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> denotes two portions of the compound, not a new substance formula. Both samples contain <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen.

| Compared compound | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> |
| :---------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="56\ \mathrm{g}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="14\ \mathrm{g}" /> |

Now that the oxygen mass matches in both rows, we divide the nitrogen masses to read the ratio.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{N\ in\ 2N_2O}} : m_{\mathrm{N\ in\ NO_2}} = 56:14 \\ &amp;= 4:1 \end{aligned}" />

Multiplying <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" /> to <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> puts oxygen on the same mass basis while the compound formula itself stays <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" />.

## Checking the Reference Mass First

Before dividing any numbers, verify two conditions. First, check that both compounds contain the **exact same two elements** (water and carbon dioxide cannot be paired under this law because hydrogen and carbon are different elements). Second, compare only the **partner element's masses after fixing the reference mass**, rather than dividing total compound masses like <InlineMath math="18\ \mathrm{g}" /> of water by <InlineMath math="34\ \mathrm{g}" /> of hydrogen peroxide, since each total mixes both elements together.

For a single compound, the law of constant composition tracks the fixed mass ratio of its elements. When two or more compounds share the same element pair, the law of multiple proportions compares those ratios across compounds. The resulting small integers gave Dalton strong evidence that atoms combine in whole-number counts.

Each ray below plots oxygen mass against carbon mass, following <InlineMath math="m_\mathrm{O}=\tfrac{4}{3}m_\mathrm{C}" /> for <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO}" /> and <InlineMath math="m_\mathrm{O}=\tfrac{8}{3}m_\mathrm{C}" /> for <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" />. Fixing carbon along the vertical reference line at <InlineMath math="12\ \mathrm{g}" /> puts both oxygen readings directly on one vertical slice.
