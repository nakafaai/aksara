## Two Elements Can Make Several Compounds

The law of multiple proportions applies when **the same two elements** form **more than one compound**. Hold the mass of one element fixed, then compare the corresponding masses of the other element. Those masses form a small whole-number ratio.

## Fix the Mass of One Element for Comparison

Suppose two compounds are both made from elements <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" />. To read Dalton's law, first hold the mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" /> fixed, then compare the mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" /> that combines with it.

For the same mass of element A, let <InlineMath math="m_{B,1}" /> and <InlineMath math="m_{B,2}" /> denote the masses of B in compounds 1 and 2. Their ratio <InlineMath math="m_{B,2}/m_{B,1}" /> is a simple whole-number ratio.

The mass of one element <Highlight>must be the same in both compounds</Highlight> before the other element's masses are compared. If both element masses change at once, the calculation cannot directly test whether the second element's mass ratio is a simple whole number.

## Water and Hydrogen Peroxide

[Water](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/water/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />, while [hydrogen peroxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/hydrogen%20peroxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON) has the formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" />.

Using <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(H)} = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)} = 16" />, the hydrogen mass in the two formulas can be made the same, <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" />.

| Compound | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> |
| :------- | :--------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="16\ \mathrm{g}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> |

Because the **hydrogen mass is already the same**, compare the oxygen masses directly.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{O\ in\ H_2O}} : m_{\mathrm{O\ in\ H_2O_2}} = 16:32 \\ &amp;= 1:2 \end{aligned}" />

The ratio <InlineMath math="1:2" /> is a simple whole-number ratio. That means these data fit the law of multiple proportions.

## Nitrogen Oxides

Nitrogen and oxygen can form several compounds. Two examples are [dinitrogen monoxide, or nitrous oxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/nitrous%20oxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON), <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" />, and [nitrogen dioxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/nitrogen%20dioxide/property/MolecularFormula/JSON), <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" />.

Using the rounded relative atomic masses <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{N})=14" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{O})=16" />, compare **two moles** of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" /> with **one mole** of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" />. The table notation <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> denotes two portions of the compound, not a new substance formula. Both samples contain <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen.

| Compared compound | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | Mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N}" /> |
| :---------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="56\ \mathrm{g}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NO_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" /> | <InlineMath math="14\ \mathrm{g}" /> |

Now that the oxygen mass is the same, compare the nitrogen masses:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{N\ in\ 2N_2O}} : m_{\mathrm{N\ in\ NO_2}} = 56:14 \\ &amp;= 4:1 \end{aligned}" />

Multiplying <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" /> to <InlineMath math="2\mathrm{N_2O}" /> puts oxygen on the same mass basis. The compound formula stays <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O}" />.

With hydrogen fixed at <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" />, the oxygen bars stand <InlineMath math="16:32=1:2" />. With oxygen fixed at <InlineMath math="32\ \mathrm{g}" />, the nitrogen bars stand <InlineMath math="56:14=4:1" />. Each pair is a simple whole-number ratio only because one element was held fixed first.

## Mistakes to Avoid

Do not compare the total masses of water and hydrogen peroxide, because each total includes both hydrogen and oxygen. Compare only the **oxygen masses after fixing the hydrogen mass**. Water and carbon dioxide are not a suitable pair either, because their other elements differ.

For one compound, the law of constant composition compares the mass ratio of its elements. For two or more compounds made from the same element pair, the law of multiple proportions compares those ratios. The resulting small whole-number ratios supported Dalton's claim that atoms combine in whole-number counts.

Run every comparison through these steps so the totals don't fool you.
