## Turning a Reaction Description into an Equation

A [chemical reaction equation](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01034) is a compact way to record starting substances, products, and their relative stoichiometric amounts. Reactants are written **on the left** and products **on the right**. Reaction symbols such as arrows connect the two sides.

The basic pattern looks like this.

<BlockMath math="\text{Reactants} \rightarrow \text{Products}" />

Read the arrow as "yields". A plus sign means there is more than one substance on the same side.

## Methane Combustion Equation

Start from an ordinary sentence: methane reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water. **Reactants go on the left**, products go on the right.

| Step | Reaction form |
| :--- | :------------ |
| Reaction sentence | methane + oxygen <InlineMath math="\rightarrow" /> carbon dioxide + water |
| Starting formulas | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}" /> |
| Balanced equation | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4} + 2\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" /> |

The starting formulas are not balanced because the left side has only <InlineMath math="2" /> oxygen atoms, while the right side has <InlineMath math="3" />. After the coefficients are added, each element has the same atom count on both sides.

Start with carbon, which already has one atom on each side. There are four hydrogen atoms on the left, so put coefficient <InlineMath math="2" /> before <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" /> to give <InlineMath math="2\times2=4" /> hydrogen atoms on the right. The right now has <InlineMath math="2+2=4" /> oxygen atoms, so <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> needs coefficient <InlineMath math="2" />. Check the result:

| Element | Left | Right |
| :------ | :--- | :---- |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> |

The coefficients read as the ratio <InlineMath math="1:2:1:2" />. Because all four substances in this example are molecular, the ratio may be pictured as <InlineMath math="1" /> methane molecule reacting with <InlineMath math="2" /> oxygen molecules to produce <InlineMath math="1" /> carbon dioxide molecule and <InlineMath math="2" /> water molecules. On a laboratory scale, the same coefficients give the mole ratio.

The molecule model is specific to molecular substances. For an ionic or network solid, the coefficient gives a stoichiometric amount or formula-unit ratio. In every case, the coefficients give relative mole amounts for a macroscopic reaction.

## Coefficients Do Not Change Formulas

Atom counts come from **multiplying coefficients by subscripts**. Coefficients can change because they state relative amounts.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_4} + 2\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" />

The coefficient <InlineMath math="2" /> before <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> means two oxygen molecules. The subscript in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> is part of the oxygen formula, so it must not be changed into <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_4}" />.

Count the atoms of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4}+2\mathrm{O_2}\rightarrow\mathrm{CO_2}+2\mathrm{H_2O}" /> by multiplying each coefficient by its subscripts. In the scene, each element has a back bar for the reactants and a front bar for the products, one grid step for <InlineMath math="2" /> atoms. Rotate it: every pair is equally tall, so the equation is balanced.

## State Symbols Come After Formulas

When the physical states are known, write the state symbol after each formula. At the high temperature of a methane flame, the water product is vapor, so the equation can be written as:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + 2\mathrm{O_2(g)} \\ &amp;\rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(g)} \end{aligned}" />

State symbols do not change atom counts. They tell the reader the **physical form of each substance** under the stated conditions. If the products cool enough for water to condense, the water would instead be written as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O(l)}" />.

| Symbol | Read as | Example |
| :----- | :------ | :------ |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(s)}" /> | solid | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{AgCl(s)}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(l)}" /> | liquid | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O(l)}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(g)}" /> | gas | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2(g)}" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(aq)}" /> | dissolved in water | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaCl(aq)}" /> |

## Silver Chloride Precipitation Example

Two clear solutions can form a new solid. For example, silver nitrate solution reacts with sodium chloride solution to produce a silver chloride precipitate.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\mathrm{AgNO_3(aq)} + \mathrm{NaCl(aq)} \\ &amp;\rightarrow \mathrm{AgCl(s)} + \mathrm{NaNO_3(aq)} \end{aligned}" />

This equation is already balanced with the coefficient ratio <InlineMath math="1:1:1:1" />. The <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(s)}" /> after <InlineMath math="\mathrm{AgCl(s)}" /> identifies silver chloride as the **solid product**.

## Steps for Writing a Reaction Equation

1. Write reactant names on the left and product names on the right.
2. Convert each substance name into the correct chemical formula.
3. Count each element on the left and right.
4. Adjust coefficients until the atoms balance.
5. Add state symbols when the information is available.

A wrong chemical formula <Highlight>cannot be fixed by balancing the equation</Highlight>. Check each formula before adjusting the coefficients.
