## Reactions Have Patterns

Changes in heat, color, precipitate formation, or gas production provide the first clues. The arrangement of reactants and products then determines the **reaction type**.

Chemists group reactions in two ways, either by the underlying process such as oxidation-reduction and acid-base transfer, or by observable products such as a new solid or gas. Our four main examples sit across both groups. Combustion and rusting are oxidation-reduction processes, whereas precipitation and gas formation highlight what appears in the vessel, so one reaction <Highlight>can satisfy more than one description</Highlight>.

## Three Reaction Patterns

A reaction pattern is easiest to spot from what happens to the molecules. When water forms, **two substances combine into one**, so the molecule count drops from three to two. When water or hydrogen peroxide breaks down, **one substance splits into several**, so the molecule count rises from two to three.

The three balanced equations below show these synthesis and decomposition patterns side by side.

## Four Examples of Chemical Reactions

The four examples below show different clues: energy release, metal change, solid formation, and gas formation. No single clue settles the question on its own, so each example names the **observation that separates a chemical change from a physical one**.

### Combustion

In the common oxygen-combustion examples studied here, a substance reacts with oxygen and **releases energy as heat or light**. White phosphorus combustion follows the balanced equation below.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{P_4} + 5\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{P_4O_{10}}" />

[Phosphorus pentoxide](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/P4O10) has the molecular formula <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}" />. <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P_2O_5}" /> is the commonly used empirical formula. The molecular form is used here because the particle model explicitly counts <InlineMath math="4" /> phosphorus atoms and <InlineMath math="10" /> oxygen atoms.

### Rusting

Rusting is an electrochemical corrosion process **involving iron, oxygen, and water**. Rust is a mixture of iron oxides and oxyhydroxides, and its composition can change with the conditions. The expression below is a simplified model of that composition.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;4\mathrm{Fe} + 3\mathrm{O_2} + 2x\mathrm{H_2O} \\ &amp;\rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe_2O_3} \cdot x\mathrm{H_2O} \end{aligned}" />

The letter <InlineMath math="x" /> makes the variable hydration explicit. Because the product has coefficient <InlineMath math="2" />, the left side uses <InlineMath math="2x\mathrm{H_2O}" /> to keep this simplified expression balanced. Corrosion products can contain [several iron oxides and oxyhydroxides](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/TechnicalNotes/NIST.TN.2377.pdf). Their proportions depend on the conditions.

### Precipitation

Precipitation happens when the solution conditions produce a substance with low solubility. For example, limewater can react with dissolved sodium hydrogen carbonate and form calcium carbonate as a white precipitate.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2(aq)} + \mathrm{NaHCO_3(aq)} \\ &amp;\rightarrow \mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} + \mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} \end{aligned}" />

The equation labels <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}" /> as the solid product. A precipitate is a **newly formed product with low solubility**. Material that was already present and merely sank does not count.

### Gas Formation

In alcoholic fermentation, yeast can convert glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide. The bubbles are an observable clue, while the equation identifies the gaseous product.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{C_2H_5OH} + 2\mathrm{CO_2}" />

Check the **atom count** of each element on both sides. Each ethanol molecule contains two carbon atoms, six hydrogen atoms, and one oxygen atom.

| Element | Left | Right |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| C | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="2\times2+2\times1=6" /> |
| H | <InlineMath math="12" /> | <InlineMath math="2\times6=12" /> |
| O | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="2\times1+2\times2=6" /> |

Look back at white phosphorus combustion to see how atom counts verify a balanced equation. Rotate the bars below to compare the four phosphorus atoms and ten oxygen atoms on each side.

## Balancing Reaction Patterns Safely

A coefficient is the number placed before a chemical formula. We may change coefficients so the number of atoms before and after the reaction matches.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{P_4} + 5\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{P_4O_{10}}" />

In this equation, <InlineMath math="5" /> before <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> is a coefficient. The subscripts in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P_4}" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}" /> are part of the formulas and **must not be altered** to force a balance.

> Always check the balanced equation alongside what you see in the beaker. Visual clues point you in the right direction, and matching atom counts confirm the reaction.

## A Double Bond Opens Up

There are also **addition reactions**, where a small molecule joins a double bond. In ethene, the double bond between the two carbon atoms opens and takes in <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> or <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}" />. Every coefficient is <InlineMath math="1" />, and every reactant atom ends up in one product.

Both addition reactions merge two molecules into a single product with zero leftover atoms.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\mathrm{C_2H_4} + \mathrm{H_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_6} \\ &amp;\mathrm{C_2H_4} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_5OH} \end{aligned}" />

## Determining Reaction Types from Evidence

Names such as combustion, precipitation, or gas formation suggest an initial classification. Confirm that classification with the **balanced equation, state symbols, reaction conditions**, and observations that apply to the reaction.

Use the following table to check each reaction type.

| Reaction type | Nearby clue | What to check |
| :------------ | :---------- | :------------ |
| Combustion | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> is a reactant and energy is released. | Check the products, conditions, and balanced atom counts. |
| Rusting | An iron surface develops a rust layer. | Check for iron, oxygen, water, and a corrosion product mixture. |
| Precipitation | A new solid appears from solution. | Check that <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(aq)}" /> reactants form a low-solubility <InlineMath math="\mathrm{(s)}" /> product. |
| Gas formation | Bubbles or gas leave the mixture. | Check for a gas product such as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2(g)}" /> and rule out boiling. |

After identifying the reaction type, write the reactants on the left and the products on the right. Then adjust the coefficients until each type of atom has the same count on both sides.

Bubbles offer the first clue in glucose fermentation, and the balanced equation confirms the mass tally. Taking one mole of glucose with <InlineMath math="\mathrm{C}=12" />, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H}=1" />, and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O}=16" /> shows that <InlineMath math="180\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sugar turns into ethanol and carbon dioxide with the exact same total mass.
