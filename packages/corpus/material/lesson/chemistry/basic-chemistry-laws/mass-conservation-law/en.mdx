## Total System Mass Stays Constant

When a chemical reaction happens, atoms do not vanish or appear from nothing. They are rearranged into new substances. That is why the mass of the whole system before a reaction equals the mass of the whole system after the reaction.

<BlockMath math="m_{\text{system before}} = m_{\text{system after}}" />

The system boundary determines what is included in the mass. In a closed container, every substance remains inside the part being weighed. In an open container, gas may leave that boundary. The balance then shows less mass because the escaped gas is no longer on it. <Highlight>The total amount of matter</Highlight> stays the same during chemical and physical changes, so the reading dropped while the matter did not disappear.

### A New Solid with the Same Mass

A reaction can make a completely new substance without changing the mass. Silver nitrate solution and table salt solution are both clear. When you mix them, silver ions and chloride ions join into silver chloride, which doesn't dissolve, so a **white precipitate** appears like a cloud.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{AgNO_3(aq)} + \mathrm{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{AgCl(s)} + \mathrm{NaNO_3(aq)}" />

Each beaker holds <InlineMath math="50.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of water with <InlineMath math="0.0050\ \mathrm{mol}" /> of its salt, that's <InlineMath math="0.85\ \mathrm{g}" /> of silver nitrate and <InlineMath math="0.29\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sodium chloride. Both beakers stand on the same balance before and after the pour.

## Measurements by Lavoisier

Before modern chemistry, combustion was often explained with phlogiston, as if something left a material when it burned. Antoine Lavoisier challenged that idea through careful weighing. He tracked the mass of reactants and products, **including gases**, and showed that combustion involves a reaction with oxygen.

His best-known experiment is recorded in [Lavoisier's book Elements of Chemistry](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30775). He heated four ounces of mercury in a glass flask for twelve days. The flask's neck opened into the air under a glass bell jar that stood in a trough of mercury. Red particles, which he called calx of mercury, formed on the mercury, and the air under the bell jar shrank from about <InlineMath math="50" /> to between <InlineMath math="42" /> and <InlineMath math="43" /> cubic inches.

The difference, <InlineMath math="45-41.5=3.5" /> grains, is the mass of gas the mercury had taken from the air. **Mass doesn't appear from nothing**: the calx is heavier because oxygen from the air joined it.

Lavoisier helped establish the law of conservation of mass and connected combustion with oxygen.

## Mass Balance in an Open System

The open view starts with the same <InlineMath math="10.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc and <InlineMath math="2.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur. It then models <InlineMath math="1.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur **crossing the boundary as vapor** before it reacts. The 2024 [abridged standard atomic weights](https://www.ciaaw.org/abridged-atomic-weights.htm) are <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{Zn})=65.38" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{S})=32.06" />. With these values, the remaining sulfur reacts with about <InlineMath math="2.04\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="m_{\mathrm{Zn}}=1\ \mathrm{g}\times\frac{65.38}{32.06}\approx 2.04\ \mathrm{g}" />

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{ZnS}} \approx 2.04 + 1.00 = 3.04\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{Zn\ left}} \approx 10.00 - 2.04 = 7.96\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{on\ balance}} \approx 3.04 + 7.96 = 11.00\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{enlarged\ system}} = 11.00 + 1.00 \\ &amp;= 12.00\ \mathrm{g} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The balance measures only the matter that remains inside the chosen boundary, so it reads <InlineMath math="11.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> after <InlineMath math="1.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur vapor leaves. If the escaping vapor is included in the system, the total mass remains <InlineMath math="12.00\ \mathrm{g}" />.

The three columns below are what the balance reads, with one grid step for <InlineMath math="4\ \mathrm{g}" />. With every substance kept inside, the closed column stays at <InlineMath math="12.00\ \mathrm{g}" />. In the open view, <InlineMath math="1.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur crosses the boundary before it reacts, so the balance shows only <InlineMath math="11.00\ \mathrm{g}" />. The mass didn't vanish: it left the system you're weighing.

Gas can leave an open system, but gas from the air can also come in and take part in a reaction. The next two experiments show both directions on the same kind of balance.

### Gas Escapes from an Open Flask

Baking soda, sodium bicarbonate, reacts with the acetic acid in vinegar and makes carbon dioxide gas.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{NaHCO_3} + \mathrm{CH_3COOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH_3COONa} + \mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{CO_2}" />

Each mole of baking soda gives **one mole of carbon dioxide**. From <InlineMath math="2.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of baking soda you get <InlineMath math="2.00\times\frac{44.01}{84.01}\approx1.05\ \mathrm{g}" /> of gas. The balance is tared with the empty flask and dish, so its first reading is the <InlineMath math="100.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of vinegar plus the baking soda.

### Oxygen from the Air Joins In

Steel wool that burns actually gets **heavier**. Iron reacts with oxygen from the air and forms a bluish-black iron oxide.

<BlockMath math="3\mathrm{Fe} + 2\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe_3O_4}" />

Every <InlineMath math="3\times55.845=167.535\ \mathrm{g}" /> of iron binds <InlineMath math="4\times15.999=63.996\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen. If all <InlineMath math="1.50\ \mathrm{g}" /> of steel wool burns, the oxygen that joins is <InlineMath math="1.50\times\frac{63.996}{167.535}\approx0.57\ \mathrm{g}" />. [The Royal Society of Chemistry's iron wool combustion experiment](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/the-combustion-of-iron-wool/717.article) shows this gain in mass on a simple balance.

## Zinc and Sulfur

In one experiment, <InlineMath math="10.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc is heated with <InlineMath math="2.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur. After the reaction, <InlineMath math="6.08\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc sulfide forms and <InlineMath math="5.92\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc **remains unreacted**.

The particle-level stoichiometric shorthand is:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{S(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnS(s)}" />

The equation states a ratio of one zinc atom for each sulfur atom incorporated into zinc sulfide. This simplified notation is used to calculate the atomic ratio in the reaction. It does not display the particle structure of bulk elemental sulfur.

The product mass does not have to equal the mass of every starting substance if one reactant is left over. First count only the zinc that actually reacted.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{Zn\ reacted}} = 10.00 - 5.92 = 4.08\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{reactants\ reacted}} = 4.08 + 2.00 \\ &amp;= 6.08\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{product}} = 6.08\ \mathrm{g} \end{aligned}" />

In this example, mass conservation holds for the part that reacts: the mass of zinc that reacts plus the mass of sulfur equals the mass of zinc sulfide formed.

If the whole container is counted, the mass is also unchanged:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{before}} &= 10.00 + 2.00 = 12.00\ \mathrm{g} \\
m_{\mathrm{after}} &= 6.08 + 5.92 = 12.00\ \mathrm{g}
\end{aligned}" />

## Three Mass Quantities in the Calculation

Keep three questions separate.

- What is the **product mass**? Only the new product counts, so it is <InlineMath math="6.08\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc sulfide.
- Is the **system mass** conserved? Yes, when the product and leftover reactant are counted together.
- Can the **balance reading** appear lower? Yes, if gas leaves an open container and is not weighed.

Chemical equations must be balanced because both sides contain the same number of atoms of each element. Mass calculations follow conservation of matter.

The law of constant composition answers a different question. When two elements form the same compound, does their mass ratio stay fixed?

Keep the three masses of the zinc and sulfur experiment apart. Before the reaction the column holds the zinc that will react, the zinc that won't, and the sulfur. After it, the reacted part has become <InlineMath math="6.08\ \mathrm{g}" /> of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{ZnS}" /> beside the same leftover zinc. Rotate the columns: both totals are <InlineMath math="12.00\ \mathrm{g}" />.
