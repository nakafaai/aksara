## Gas Volumes Follow Coefficients

The law of combining volumes states that reacting gases, and gaseous products, have <Highlight>volumes in simple whole-number ratios</Highlight> when measured at the same temperature and pressure. This law is also known as Gay-Lussac's law of combining gas volumes.

When all gas volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure, their ratios follow the coefficients in the balanced equation. Hydrogen and oxygen combine by volume in a <InlineMath math="2:1" /> ratio to form water.

This rule applies only to substances in the gas phase. Their volumes must also be compared at the same temperature and pressure.

We use the **ideal-gas approximation** and count the volumes that actually react or form. Any unreacted excess gas must be counted separately.

### Coefficients Count Molecules

Gas volumes come in the same ratio as the coefficients because **the coefficients count molecules**. Choose the hydrogen chloride reaction and drag the slider. <InlineMath math="1" /> molecule of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> molecule of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl_2}" /> give <InlineMath math="2" /> molecules of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{HCl}" />, exactly the volume ratio <InlineMath math="1 : 1 : 2" />.

## Water Must Be in the Gas Phase

Look at the balanced equation for forming water vapor.

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(g)}" />

The coefficients <InlineMath math="2:1:2" /> mean <InlineMath math="2" /> volume parts of hydrogen react with <InlineMath math="1" /> volume part of oxygen to form <InlineMath math="2" /> volume parts of water vapor.

<BlockMath math="V_{\mathrm{H_2}} : V_{\mathrm{O_2}} : V_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = 2:1:2" />

So, if every gas is measured at the **same temperature and pressure**, <InlineMath math="200\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of hydrogen reacts with <InlineMath math="100\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of oxygen to produce <InlineMath math="200\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of water vapor. If the water has condensed into liquid, the liquid volume is no longer read directly from this gas ratio.

Each block below is one part of gas at the same temperature and pressure. Two parts of hydrogen and one part of oxygen give two parts of water vapor, so the volume ratio <InlineMath math="2:1:2" /> is just the coefficients. Notice that the volumes do not add up: three parts become two.

## Ammonia Decomposition Example

Suppose <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of ammonia gas decomposes completely at the same temperature and pressure. The balanced equation is:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{NH_3(g)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{N_2(g)}" />

The **coefficients give this volume ratio**:

<BlockMath math="V_{\mathrm{NH_3}} : V_{\mathrm{H_2}} : V_{\mathrm{N_2}} = 2:3:1" />

Because <InlineMath math="2" /> parts of ammonia equal <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{mL}" />, <InlineMath math="1" /> volume part equals <InlineMath math="300\ \mathrm{mL}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{H_2}} &= 3 \times 300 = 900\ \mathrm{mL} \\
V_{\mathrm{N_2}} &= 1 \times 300 = 300\ \mathrm{mL}
\end{aligned}" />

So decomposing <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of ammonia gas produces <InlineMath math="900\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of hydrogen gas and <InlineMath math="300\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of nitrogen gas.

### The Molecules in the Ammonia Example

Ammonia breaks down into nitrogen and hydrogen. Turn all three molecules and count their atoms. Each <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" /> carries <InlineMath math="1" /> nitrogen atom, while each <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2}" /> needs <InlineMath math="2" />. That is why <InlineMath math="2" /> volumes of ammonia **give only** <InlineMath math="1" /> volume of nitrogen.

## Conditions for Using Volume Ratios

- The reaction equation must already be balanced.
- The substances being compared **must be gases**.
- All gas volumes must be measured at the same temperature and pressure.
- If a product turns into a liquid or solid, that product volume is not read directly from the gas coefficients.

If any of these conditions is missing, compute the mole amounts first and convert them to volumes.

Here is why every volume needs the same temperature and pressure. A gas takes up <InlineMath math="V=n\cdot V_m" />, and at <InlineMath math="1\ \mathrm{atm}" /> one mole fills <InlineMath math="22.4\ \mathrm{L}" /> at <InlineMath math="0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}" /> but <InlineMath math="24.5\ \mathrm{L}" /> at <InlineMath math="25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}" />. Zoom in at two moles: the same amount of gas gives two different volumes, so volumes measured under different conditions can't be compared through coefficients.
