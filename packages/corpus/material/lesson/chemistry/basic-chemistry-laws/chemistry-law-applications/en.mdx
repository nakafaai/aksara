## Choose the Law from Data

The type of data in a problem indicates <Highlight>which basic law of chemistry applies</Highlight>. The problem may involve total mass, the composition of one compound, two compounds made from the same elements, or gas volumes under the same conditions.

Stoichiometric calculations use factors from balanced equations to relate amounts of substances. Before calculating, we need to check the balanced equation, the substance or quantity to calculate, and the units of the given data.

The following list connects each type of data with the appropriate law.

- If every substance in the system is counted, use conservation of mass.
- If one pure compound is being analyzed, use constant composition.
- If two different compounds are made from the same two elements, use multiple proportions.
- If all compared substances are gases at the same temperature and pressure, use combining volumes.

Before you pick a law, check whether the system was closed. Each point is one experiment, mass before across and mass after up, so a closed system sits on the gray line. When <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" /> of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaCO_3}" /> reacts with acid in an open beaker, <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}\cdot\tfrac{44}{100}=0.88\ \mathrm{g}" /> of carbon dioxide escapes, and the point drops below the line. ## Mass and Volume Patterns

Before calculating, identify **which quantity stays fixed** and which quantities are being compared. That choice determines the law and ratio used in the calculation. The patterns below separate mass ratios from volume ratios, because the two follow different laws.

### Reaction System

Suppose <InlineMath math="10.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc reacts with <InlineMath math="2.00\ \mathrm{g}" /> of sulfur. After the reaction, <InlineMath math="6.08\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc sulfide forms and <InlineMath math="5.92\ \mathrm{g}" /> of zinc remains.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{S(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnS(s)}" />

The reacting zinc is not <InlineMath math="10.00\ \mathrm{g}" />, because some zinc is left over.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;m_{\mathrm{Zn\ reacted}} = 10.00 - 5.92 = 4.08\ \mathrm{g} \\ &amp;m_{\mathrm{reactants\ reacted}} = 4.08 + 2.00 \\ &amp;= 6.08\ \mathrm{g} \end{aligned}" />

The mass of the **reactants that actually reacted** equals the product mass. Counting all substances in the container, without the container mass, gives the same before-and-after mass of <InlineMath math="12.00\ \mathrm{g}" />.

### One Compound Composition

Calcium oxide, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CaO}" />, can be checked with two different samples. The first sample has mass <InlineMath math="2.8\ \mathrm{g}" /> and contains <InlineMath math="0.8\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen. The second sample has mass <InlineMath math="3.5\ \mathrm{g}" /> and contains <InlineMath math="1.0\ \mathrm{g}" /> of oxygen.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{Ca,1}} &= 2.8 - 0.8 = 2.0\ \mathrm{g} \\
m_{\mathrm{Ca,2}} &= 3.5 - 1.0 = 2.5\ \mathrm{g} \\
2.0:0.8 &= 2.5:1 \\
2.5:1.0 &= 2.5:1
\end{aligned}" />

The sample masses may differ, but the **calcium-to-oxygen mass ratio** stays the same. That is the pattern of constant composition.

### Comparing Different Compounds Made from the Same Elements

Water and hydrogen peroxide are both made from hydrogen and oxygen, but their formulas are different.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H_2O}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{H_2O_2}" />

With <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(H)} = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="A_r\mathrm{(O)} = 16" />, the hydrogen mass in both compounds can be made the same: <InlineMath math="2\ \mathrm{g}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{H_2O} &: 2\ \mathrm{g\ H} + 16\ \mathrm{g\ O} \\
\mathrm{H_2O_2} &: 2\ \mathrm{g\ H} + 32\ \mathrm{g\ O} \\
m_{\mathrm{O}} &: 16:32 = 1:2
\end{aligned}" />

Because the **hydrogen mass is already the same**, compare the oxygen mass. The ratio <InlineMath math="1:2" /> is a simple whole-number ratio, matching the law of multiple proportions.

### Gases Under Matching Conditions

In this decomposition example, assume ammonia decomposes **completely**, gases follow the ideal-gas approximation, and all volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{NH_3(g)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{N_2(g)}" />

If <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of ammonia decomposes, then <InlineMath math="2" /> parts equal <InlineMath math="600\ \mathrm{mL}" /> and <InlineMath math="1" /> part equals <InlineMath math="300\ \mathrm{mL}" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{H_2}} &= 3 \times 300 = 900\ \mathrm{mL} \\
V_{\mathrm{N_2}} &= 1 \times 300 = 300\ \mathrm{mL}
\end{aligned}" />

The scene below stacks the gases as equal blocks, one block for each coefficient and every block worth <InlineMath math="300\ \mathrm{mL}" />. Rotate it and count: two blocks of ammonia become three blocks of hydrogen and one block of nitrogen. The block size never changes, so the volume ratio <InlineMath math="2:3:1" /> is just the coefficient ratio, and it only works because every gas is measured at the same temperature and pressure.

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms on the reactant and product sides. At the same temperature and pressure, gas volume ratios follow the equation coefficients.

## When One Problem Uses Two Laws

One problem can use more than one law. Product mass and leftover reactant use conservation of mass, but if the problem then asks for element composition in the same compound, that part uses constant composition. Do not force one law to answer every question.

Define the **system boundary**, then choose the part of the data that is actually being compared.

- If the problem asks about total mass, define the system boundary first.
- If it asks about one compound's composition, compare the masses of its elements.
- If it compares two compounds from the same elements, hold one element fixed before comparing the other.
- If it asks about gas volume, confirm that all gases are measured at the same temperature and pressure.

## Methane Combustion

Methane burns according to this balanced equation.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + 2\mathrm{O_2(g)}
  &\rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} \\
  &\quad + 2\mathrm{H_2O(g)}
\end{aligned}" />

If <InlineMath math="50\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of methane gas reacts completely and every gas is measured at the **same temperature and pressure**, what volume of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{O_2}" /> is needed and what volume of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> forms?

The coefficients give the volume ratio <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4:O_2:CO_2:H_2O} = 1:2:1:2" />. Therefore:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{O_2}} &= 2 \times 50 = 100\ \mathrm{mL} \\
V_{\mathrm{CO_2}} &= 1 \times 50 = 50\ \mathrm{mL}
\end{aligned}" />

The answer is <InlineMath math="100\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of oxygen gas and <InlineMath math="50\ \mathrm{mL}" /> of carbon dioxide gas. If the water product has condensed into liquid, its volume is not read directly from the gas ratio.

The laws of definite and multiple proportions show mass patterns that support the idea that atoms combine in fixed ratios.

Read the coefficients of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_4(g)}+2\mathrm{O_2(g)}\rightarrow\mathrm{CO_2(g)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(g)}" /> as volumes and scale every one by the same <InlineMath math="50\ \mathrm{mL}" />. Rotate the bars: the gases react in the ratio <InlineMath math="1:2:1:2" />. The water volume only holds while the water stays a gas.
