## Acids and Alkalis Depend on Water

An **acid** is a chemical that ionises in water to produce **hydrogen ions**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" />. An **alkali** is a base that dissolves in water and ionises to produce **hydroxide ions**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" />. Acidity is measured by the pH value, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}]" />, on a scale from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="14" />. In the models here, you dilute an acid and watch its pH change, run a titration, and build a neutralisation curve.

The <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ion actually joins a water molecule to form a **hydroxonium ion**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_3O^+}" />:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{HCl} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_3O^+} + \mathrm{Cl^-}" />

Without water, those ions never form. Hydrogen chloride dissolved in methylbenzene stays as molecules, so the solution doesn't turn blue litmus paper red. Dissolve hydrogen chloride in water instead, and its molecules ionise and acidic properties appear. Alkalis behave the same way: dry ammonia doesn't change red litmus, but aqueous ammonia turns it blue because water lets <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions form.

A **base** is any substance that reacts with an acid to produce a salt and water only. Every alkali is a base, but not every base dissolves in water. Copper(II) oxide, for example, is a base but not an alkali.

## pH and the Concentration of Hydrogen Ions

A smaller pH means a higher concentration of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions. A neutral solution at <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> has <InlineMath math="\mathrm{pH} = 7" />. Acidic solutions have a pH below <InlineMath math="7" />, and alkaline solutions have a pH above <InlineMath math="7" />. For an alkali, find the pOH first, then use this relationship:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pOH} = -\log[\mathrm{OH^-}]" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14" />
</MathContainer>

The pH scale is logarithmic. Every time the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> concentration drops **tenfold**, the pH rises by just **one unit**. Move the dilution factor slider for <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid and watch the curve flatten out.

So a solution at pH <InlineMath math="2" /> isn't twice as acidic as one at pH <InlineMath math="4" />. The difference is two units, which means a **hundred times** the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> concentration:

<BlockMath math="\frac{10^{-2}}{10^{-4}} = 100" />

## Strength and Concentration of an Acid

The **strength** of an acid or alkali depends on its **degree of ionisation** in water, not on its concentration. A strong acid like hydrochloric acid ionises **completely**, so every molecule gives an <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ion. A weak acid like ethanoic acid ionises **partially**, so most of its molecules stay un-ionised. In the same way, sodium hydroxide is a strong alkali, while aqueous ammonia is a weak alkali.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COO^-} + \mathrm{H^+}" />

Compare solutions of **the same concentration**, <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />. The values for ethanoic acid and ammonia are calculated from their ionisation constants at <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" />.

**Concentration**, on the other hand, is the quantity of solute in <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}^3" /> of solution. You can write it in <InlineMath math="\text{g dm}^{-3}" /> or as **molarity**, in <InlineMath math="\text{mol dm}^{-3}" />:

<BlockMath math="M = \frac{n}{V}" />

Here <InlineMath math="n" /> is the number of moles of solute and <InlineMath math="V" /> is the volume of solution in <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3" />. A solution whose concentration is known exactly is a **standard solution**, and you prepare it in a volumetric flask.

### Example Preparing a Standard Solution

You dissolve <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" /> of sodium hydroxide in water and make it up to <InlineMath math="250\text{ cm}^3" /> of solution. Find its molarity. The molar mass of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NaOH}" /> is <InlineMath math="40\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />.

**Convert the mass to moles**, then convert the volume to <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{2.0}{40} = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V = \frac{250}{1000} = 0.250\text{ dm}^3" />
<BlockMath math="M = \frac{0.050}{0.250} = 0.20\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />
</MathContainer>

Check it with the mass concentration. The mass in <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}^3" /> is four times <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" />, which is <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ g dm}^{-3}" />, and <InlineMath math="8.0 \div 40 = 0.20" />. Both routes give the same answer.

### Example Diluting a Solution

When you add water, the number of moles of solute doesn't change. Only the volume grows. That's the basis of the **dilution formula**:

<BlockMath math="M_1V_1 = M_2V_2" />

To prepare <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid from <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> acid:

<BlockMath math="V_1 = \frac{M_2V_2}{M_1} = \frac{0.10 \times 500}{1.0} = 50\text{ cm}^3" />

Measure out <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of the concentrated acid, then add distilled water until the volume reaches <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" />. Check: the moles at the start are <InlineMath math="1.0 \times 0.050 = 0.050" /> mol, and the moles at the end are <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 0.500 = 0.050" /> mol. They match.

## Neutralisation and Titration

**Neutralisation** is the reaction between an acid and a base that produces a salt and water only. In every neutralisation of an acid by an alkali, the real reaction is hydrogen ions joining hydroxide ions:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{OH^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}" />

A **titration** finds the exact volume needed for complete neutralisation. One solution runs from a burette into the other in a conical flask until the indicator changes colour. That moment is the **end point**. Run the titration below and watch the phenolphthalein.

Now use a titration to find an unknown concentration. A <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> portion of <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide is neutralised by <InlineMath math="12.50\text{ cm}^3" /> of sulfuric acid. Find the molarity of the sulfuric acid.

Write the balanced equation first, because the mole ratio comes from it:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H_2SO_4} + 2\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na_2SO_4} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Sulfuric acid is a **diprotic acid**, so one mole of acid needs two moles of alkali. Use <InlineMath math="\dfrac{M_aV_a}{M_bV_b} = \dfrac{a}{b}" /> with <InlineMath math="a = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 2" />:

<BlockMath math="M_a = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{0.10 \times 25.0}{12.50} = 0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />

Check it with moles. The sodium hydroxide is <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 0.0250 = 0.0025" /> mol. The sulfuric acid is <InlineMath math="0.10 \times 0.01250 = 0.00125" /> mol, exactly half. The <InlineMath math="1 : 2" /> ratio holds.

## Salts and How to Prepare Them

A **salt** is an ionic compound formed when the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions of an acid are replaced by metal ions or ammonium ions. Examples are sodium chloride from hydrochloric acid and copper(II) sulfate from sulfuric acid. How you prepare a salt depends on **whether it dissolves in water**:

| Salt | Soluble in water | Insoluble in water |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts | All | None |
| Nitrate salts | All | None |
| Chloride salts | Most | Lead(II) chloride and silver chloride |
| Sulfate salts | Most | Barium sulfate, lead(II) sulfate and calcium sulfate |
| Carbonate salts | Sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonate | The rest |

Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are prepared by **titration** of an acid with an alkali, because both reactants dissolve and the product can't be filtered off. Other soluble salts are prepared by adding a metal, metal oxide or metal carbonate **in excess** to an acid, filtering off the excess, and crystallising the salt from the filtrate.

Insoluble salts are prepared by a **precipitation reaction**, also called double decomposition. You mix two salt solutions that contain the cation and the anion of the salt, and a precipitate forms at once. Pour silver nitrate solution into sodium chloride and watch the white precipitate of silver chloride:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{AgCl(s)}" />

## Common Mistakes about Acids and Salts

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | How to check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A solution at pH <InlineMath math="2" /> is twice as acidic as one at pH <InlineMath math="4" />. | The pH scale is logarithmic. Two units mean <InlineMath math="100" /> times the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> concentration. | Work out <InlineMath math="10^{-2} \div 10^{-4}" />. |
| A strong acid means a concentrated acid. | Strength depends on the degree of ionisation. Concentration depends on the amount of solute. | Concentrated ethanoic acid is still a weak acid. |
| Hydrogen chloride in methylbenzene shows acidic properties. | Without water, hydrogen chloride doesn't ionise, so there are no <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions. | Look for water in the system. |
| Silver chloride is prepared by adding silver metal to hydrochloric acid. | Silver doesn't react with hydrochloric acid, and silver chloride is an insoluble salt. | Insoluble salts are prepared by precipitation. |
| In a titration of sulfuric acid with sodium hydroxide, the ratio is one to one. | Sulfuric acid is diprotic, so the mole ratio of acid to alkali is <InlineMath math="1 : 2" />. | Balance the equation first. |

The second mistake is the hardest to spot because the two words sound alike. Keep this pair in mind: **strong or weak** describes ionisation, while **concentrated or dilute** describes the amount of solute.

## Practice on Acids and Alkalis

These questions move from a pH calculation to titrations and explanation. **Write the units at every step** so that a mix-up between <InlineMath math="\text{cm}^3" /> and <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3" /> shows up quickly.

1. Calculate the pH of <InlineMath math="0.01\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid.

2. What volume of <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide solution do you need to prepare <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> solution?

3. A <InlineMath math="20.0\text{ cm}^3" /> portion of <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid neutralises <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of potassium hydroxide solution. Find the molarity of the potassium hydroxide solution.

4. Hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid, both <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, are titrated with the same sodium hydroxide. Explain why ethanoic acid starts at a higher pH but needs the same volume of alkali for neutralisation.

### Solutions

**Solution 1**: Hydrochloric acid is a strong, monoprotic acid, so <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{H^+}] = 0.01\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pH} = -\log(0.01) = -\log(10^{-2}) = 2" />

Check it with the dilution idea: <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> acid has pH <InlineMath math="1" />, and this acid is ten times more dilute, so the pH rises by one unit to <InlineMath math="2" />.

**Solution 2**: The moles of sodium hydroxide don't change on dilution, so use <InlineMath math="M_1V_1 = M_2V_2" />:

<BlockMath math="V_1 = \frac{0.40 \times 500}{2.0} = 100\text{ cm}^3" />

Measure out <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> of the concentrated solution and add distilled water up to <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" />. Check: <InlineMath math="2.0 \times 0.100 = 0.20" /> mol, and <InlineMath math="0.40 \times 0.500 = 0.20" /> mol.

**Solution 3**: The equation is <InlineMath math="\mathrm{HCl} + \mathrm{KOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{KCl} + \mathrm{H_2O}" />, with a mole ratio of <InlineMath math="1 : 1" />.

<BlockMath math="M_b = \frac{M_aV_a}{V_b} = \frac{0.50 \times 20.0}{25.0} = 0.40\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />

Check: the acid is <InlineMath math="0.50 \times 0.0200 = 0.010" /> mol, and the alkali is <InlineMath math="0.40 \times 0.0250 = 0.010" /> mol. They match, so neutralisation is complete.

**Solution 4**: Ethanoic acid is a **weak acid** that ionises partially, so its starting <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> concentration is much lower than that of hydrochloric acid, which ionises completely. That's why its starting pH is higher, about <InlineMath math="2.9" /> compared with <InlineMath math="1" />. But both acids contain **the same number of moles of acid**, and both are monoprotic. As the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions remove <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions, more ethanoic acid molecules ionise, until every acid molecule has been neutralised. So the volume of sodium hydroxide needed is the same.
