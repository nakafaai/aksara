## Why Acids and Alkalis Need Water to Act

Think of fresh lemon juice tasting sharp on your tongue and soapy water feeling slippery between your fingers. Those everyday clues come from two opposing chemical families, and both stay completely quiet until water enters the glass.

An **acid** is a substance that ionises in water to produce **hydrogen ions**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" />. An **alkali** is a soluble base that ionises in water to produce **hydroxide ions**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" />. Every <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ion immediately latches onto a water molecule to form a **hydroxonium ion**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_3O^+}" />:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{HCl} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_3O^+} + \mathrm{Cl^-}" />

> Water is the switch that wakes an acid or an alkali up. Without water, hydrogen chloride stays trapped as neutral molecules and cannot release hydrogen ions.

This explains a classic lab surprise. Hydrogen chloride dissolved in methylbenzene remains as whole molecules, so blue litmus paper stays blue and dry magnesium will not fizz. Pour hydrogen chloride into water instead, and the molecules split into free ions that turn blue litmus red. Dry ammonia gas acts the same way until water dissolves it and frees <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions to turn red litmus blue.

A **base** is any substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only. Every alkali belongs to the base family, yet not every base dissolves in water. Copper(II) oxide reacts smoothly with acids, so it is a base, even though it stays insoluble and is not an alkali.

## Testing Solutions with Indicators and the pH Scale

Acidity is measured on the **pH scale** from <InlineMath math="0" /> to <InlineMath math="14" />. A neutral solution at <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" /> sits at <InlineMath math="\mathrm{pH} = 7" />, acidic solutions sit below <InlineMath math="7" />, and alkaline solutions sit above <InlineMath math="7" />. The exact pH comes from the hydrogen ion concentration:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}]" />

For an alkali, find the pOH from the hydroxide ion concentration first, then connect it to pH:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pOH} = -\log[\mathrm{OH^-}]" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14" />
</MathContainer>

## Acid Strength Versus Molar Concentration

Two labels get mixed up more than any others in chemistry: **strong** versus **concentrated**. **Strength** describes the **degree of ionisation** in water, whereas **concentration** counts how much solute sits inside one cubic decimetre of solution. Concentrated vinegar is packed with molecules, yet it stays a weak acid.

A **strong acid** like hydrochloric acid ionises completely, so every dissolved molecule splits to release an <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ion. A **weak acid** like ethanoic acid ionises only partially, leaving most molecules intact in reversible equilibrium:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COO^-} + \mathrm{H^+}" />

In the same way, sodium hydroxide is a **strong alkali** that ionises fully, whereas aqueous ammonia is a **weak alkali**. Compare four solutions that all share the exact same molarity, <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />, at <InlineMath math="25^\circ\text{C}" />.

### Calculating Molarity for a Standard Solution

**Concentration** measures solute per <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}^3" /> of solution, written in <InlineMath math="\text{g dm}^{-3}" /> or as **molarity** in <InlineMath math="\text{mol dm}^{-3}" />:

<BlockMath math="M = \frac{n}{V}" />

Here <InlineMath math="n" /> is the moles of solute and <InlineMath math="V" /> is the solution volume in <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3" />. A **standard solution** has a precisely known concentration and is made in a volumetric flask.

Suppose you dissolve <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ g}" /> of sodium hydroxide, molar mass <InlineMath math="40\text{ g mol}^{-1}" />, and make the volume up to <InlineMath math="250\text{ cm}^3" />. Convert mass into moles and volume into <InlineMath math="\text{dm}^3" /> first so unit mix-ups cannot sneak in:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n = \frac{2.0}{40} = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V = \frac{250}{1000} = 0.250\text{ dm}^3" />
<BlockMath math="M = \frac{0.050}{0.250} = 0.20\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />
</MathContainer>

Verify the answer with mass concentration: four portions of <InlineMath math="250\text{ cm}^3" /> fill <InlineMath math="1\text{ dm}^3" />, giving <InlineMath math="8.0\text{ g dm}^{-3}" />, and dividing by the molar mass confirms the same molarity:

<BlockMath math="\frac{8.0}{40} = 0.20\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />

### Diluting a Concentrated Acid or Alkali

When you pour distilled water into a stock solution, the **number of moles of solute stays constant** while the total volume expands. That conservation gives the **dilution equation**:

<BlockMath math="M_1V_1 = M_2V_2" />

To prepare <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid from <InlineMath math="1.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> stock acid, solve for the stock volume <InlineMath math="V_1" />:

<BlockMath math="V_1 = \frac{M_2V_2}{M_1} = \frac{0.10 \times 500}{1.0} = 50\text{ cm}^3" />

Measure <InlineMath math="50\text{ cm}^3" /> of stock acid and top up with distilled water to <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" />. Check the conserved moles before and after dilution:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n_1 = 1.0 \times 0.050 = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n_2 = 0.10 \times 0.500 = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
</MathContainer>

Try the same logic on a stock alkali: preparing <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.40\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide from <InlineMath math="2.0\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> stock requires:

<BlockMath math="V_1 = \frac{0.40 \times 500}{2.0} = 100\text{ cm}^3" />

Both the starting <InlineMath math="100\text{ cm}^3" /> stock and the final <InlineMath math="500\text{ cm}^3" /> diluted solution hold <InlineMath math="0.20\text{ mol}" /> of sodium hydroxide, confirming the preparation step.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{OH^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}" />

### Balancing Diprotic and Monoprotic Titration Ratios

Never assume every acid and alkali react in a <InlineMath math="1 : 1" /> ratio. Always write the **balanced chemical equation** first so the mole coefficients <InlineMath math="a" /> and <InlineMath math="b" /> come directly from the chemistry:

<BlockMath math="\frac{M_aV_a}{M_bV_b} = \frac{a}{b}" />

Suppose <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> sodium hydroxide is neutralised by <InlineMath math="12.50\text{ cm}^3" /> of sulfuric acid. Sulfuric acid is a **diprotic acid**, donating two hydrogen ions per molecule:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H_2SO_4} + 2\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na_2SO_4} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}" />

Here <InlineMath math="a = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="b = 2" />, so substituting into the titration ratio gives the sulfuric acid molarity:

<BlockMath math="M_a = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{0.10 \times 25.0}{12.50} = 0.10\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />

Check the result directly from moles to confirm the <InlineMath math="1 : 2" /> stoichiometry:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{NaOH}) = 0.10 \times 0.0250 = 0.0025\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}) = 0.10 \times 0.01250 = 0.00125\text{ mol}" />
</MathContainer>

Contrast that with a **monoprotic acid** like hydrochloric acid titrated against potassium hydroxide, where <InlineMath math="20.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of <InlineMath math="0.50\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> acid neutralises <InlineMath math="25.0\text{ cm}^3" /> of alkali. The balanced equation fixes a <InlineMath math="1 : 1" /> ratio:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{HCl} + \mathrm{KOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{KCl} + \mathrm{H_2O}" />
<BlockMath math="M_b = \frac{M_aV_a}{V_b} = \frac{0.50 \times 20.0}{25.0} = 0.40\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" />
</MathContainer>

Both flasks hold <InlineMath math="0.010\text{ mol}" /> of reactant at neutralisation, confirming complete reaction.

### Why Weak and Strong Acids Need Equal Alkali

Picture two flasks holding equal volumes of <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> hydrochloric acid and <InlineMath math="0.1\text{ mol dm}^{-3}" /> ethanoic acid. Students often guess that ethanoic acid needs less sodium hydroxide because its starting pH is higher, around <InlineMath math="2.9" /> instead of <InlineMath math="1.0" />.

In reality, both flasks require the **exact same volume of alkali**. Both acids are **monoprotic** and both contain the same total number of acid moles. As incoming <InlineMath math="\mathrm{OH^-}" /> ions remove free <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions from ethanoic acid, more un-ionised <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CH_3COOH}" /> molecules split up to replace them until every single molecule has reacted.

## Preparing Soluble and Insoluble Salts

A **salt** is an ionic compound formed when the <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H^+}" /> ions of an acid are replaced by metal ions or ammonium ions, such as sodium chloride from hydrochloric acid or copper(II) sulfate from sulfuric acid. Before touching any glassware, check the **solubility rules in water**, because solubility decides the entire preparation method.

| Salt family | Soluble in water | Insoluble in water |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sodium, potassium, and ammonium salts | All | None |
| Nitrate salts | All | None |
| Chloride salts | Most | Lead(II) chloride and silver chloride |
| Sulfate salts | Most | Barium sulfate, lead(II) sulfate, and calcium sulfate |
| Carbonate salts | Sodium, potassium, and ammonium carbonate | All others |

> Match the preparation route to solubility first. Titrate for sodium, potassium, and ammonium salts, add excess solid for other soluble salts, and precipitate for insoluble salts.

Sodium, potassium, and ammonium salts must be made by **titration** of an acid with an alkali, because both reactants dissolve and no leftover solid can be filtered away. Every other **soluble salt** is prepared by adding an insoluble metal, metal oxide, or metal carbonate **in excess** to an acid, filtering off the unreacted solid, and crystallising clean crystals from the filtrate.

**Insoluble salts** cannot be prepared by reacting an unreactive metal directly with an acid. Adding silver metal to hydrochloric acid produces nothing because silver does not react with dilute acid, and silver chloride is insoluble anyway. Instead, insoluble salts are made by a **precipitation reaction**, also known as double decomposition, mixing two soluble salt solutions that supply the target cation and anion:

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{AgCl(s)}" />
