## Dynamic Reversible Reactions

Picture a bookshop with two doors. Every minute, <InlineMath math="5" /> people walk in through the front door and <InlineMath math="5" /> people walk out through the back. Nobody stops moving, yet the headcount inside stays steady.

That's how **dynamic equilibrium** works in chemistry. In a reversible reaction, products can turn right back into reactants, so we write a double arrow.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{A} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B}" />

At the start, the flask holds only <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" />, so the forward reaction runs fast. As <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" /> builds up, the reverse reaction speeds up too until **the forward rate equals the reverse rate**. Both reactions keep going, yet the concentrations of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{A}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{B}" /> stay constant.

> Chemical equilibrium never means the reaction stops or that reactants and products reach equal amounts. Only the forward and reverse speeds become equal.

## Equilibrium Constants

At any fixed temperature, every reversible reaction carries one constant score showing whether the equilibrium mixture favors products or reactants. You can express that score through the **concentration constant <InlineMath math="K_c" />** for solutions and gases, or the **pressure constant <InlineMath math="K_p" />** for gas mixtures.

### Concentration Constant

Think of <InlineMath math="K_c" /> as a scoreboard with products on top and reactants on the bottom. A large <InlineMath math="K_c" /> means products dominate at equilibrium. For a balanced reaction, raise every concentration to its stoichiometric coefficient.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}" />
<BlockMath math="K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c\,[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a\,[\mathrm{B}]^b}" />
</MathContainer>

A common slip is putting solids or pure liquids into the fraction. Their density doesn't change as the reaction proceeds, so **only <InlineMath math="(g)" /> and <InlineMath math="(aq)" /> species belong in <InlineMath math="K_c" />**. Always balance the equation first so every exponent matches its coefficient.

Compare ammonia synthesis with limestone decomposition below. Every substance in ammonia synthesis is a gas, whereas limestone decomposition leaves only <InlineMath math="\mathrm{CO_2}" /> in the gas phase.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{N_2}(g) + 3\mathrm{H_2}(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}(g) \implies K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{NH_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{N_2}][\mathrm{H_2}]^3}" />
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{CaCO_3}(s) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO}(s) + \mathrm{CO_2}(g) \implies K_c = [\mathrm{CO_2}]" />
</MathContainer>

Try a quick check on sulfur dioxide oxidation below. Every substance is a gas, so the powers in <InlineMath math="K_c" /> follow the coefficients <InlineMath math="2" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="2" /> directly.

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{SO_2}(g) + \mathrm{O_2}(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{SO_3}(g) \implies K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{SO_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{SO_2}]^2[\mathrm{O_2}]}" />

### Pressure Constant Kp

For gas reactions, measuring partial pressures is often simpler than measuring molarities. Because ideal gas pressure scales with concentration through <InlineMath math="RT" />, we connect **<InlineMath math="K_p" /> and <InlineMath math="K_c" /> using the change in gas moles <InlineMath math="\Delta n" />**.

<BlockMath math="K_p = K_c\,(RT)^{\Delta n}, \qquad R = 0.0821\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}" />

Find <InlineMath math="\Delta n" /> by subtracting the moles of gaseous reactants from the moles of gaseous products, and keep temperature <InlineMath math="T" /> in Kelvin.

Take <InlineMath math="\mathrm{N_2O_4}" /> dissociation with <InlineMath math="K_c = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}" /> at <InlineMath math="298\text{ K}" />. The right side has <InlineMath math="2\text{ mol}" /> of gas and the left has <InlineMath math="1\text{ mol}" />, giving a difference of positive one.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\mathrm{N_2O_4}(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NO_2}(g), \qquad \Delta n = 2 - 1 = 1" />
<BlockMath math="K_p = 4.0 \times 10^{-3} \times (0.0821 \times 298)^1 \approx 0.098" />
</MathContainer>

Check the calculation backward by dividing <InlineMath math="0.098" /> by <InlineMath math="24.47" />, which lands right back at <InlineMath math="4.0 \times 10^{-3}" />.

## Finding Yield and Direction

Knowing <InlineMath math="K_c" /> unlocks two practical jobs in chemical equilibrium problems. You can calculate **final equilibrium concentrations** from starting amounts, or predict **which way a fresh mixture will shift** before equilibrium arrives.

### Equilibrium Table Steps

To track how much of each substance gets used and made, build an **initial, change, equilibrium table**. Think of it like a simple bank ledger: starting balance plus or minus each transaction gives the final balance.

Suppose you place <InlineMath math="1\text{ mol}" /> of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> and <InlineMath math="1\text{ mol}" /> of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{I_2}" /> into a <InlineMath math="1\text{ L}" /> vessel with <InlineMath math="K_c = 64" /> for the reaction below.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{H_2}(g) + \mathrm{I_2}(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{HI}(g)" />

Since the vessel volume is <InlineMath math="1\text{ L}" />, both <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{I_2}" /> start at <InlineMath math="1\text{ M}" />. Let <InlineMath math="x" /> react, so <InlineMath math="\mathrm{HI}" /> grows by <InlineMath math="2x" /> to match its coefficient.

| Substance | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{H_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{I_2}" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathrm{HI}" /> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Initial | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> |
| Change | <InlineMath math="-x" /> | <InlineMath math="-x" /> | <InlineMath math="+2x" /> |
| Equilibrium | <InlineMath math="1 - x" /> | <InlineMath math="1 - x" /> | <InlineMath math="2x" /> |

Substitute the equilibrium row into <InlineMath math="K_c" />. Both sides are perfect squares and <InlineMath math="x" /> lies between <InlineMath math="0" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />, so you can take the positive square root directly.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{(2x)^2}{(1 - x)^2} = 64 \implies \frac{2x}{1 - x} = 8" />
<BlockMath math="2x = 8 - 8x \implies x = 0.8" />
</MathContainer>

At equilibrium, <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{H_2}]" /> and <InlineMath math="[\mathrm{I_2}]" /> are each <InlineMath math="0.2\text{ M}" />, while **<InlineMath math="[\mathrm{HI}] = 1.6\text{ M}" />**. Plug those numbers back into the expression to verify your algebra.

<BlockMath math="K_c = \frac{1.6^2}{0.2 \times 0.2} = \frac{2.56}{0.04} = 64" />

| Situation | Meaning | Direction of shift |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <InlineMath math="Q < K" /> | too little product so far | to the right, forming products |
| <InlineMath math="Q = K" /> | mixture already at equilibrium | no shift |
| <InlineMath math="Q > K" /> | too much product | to the left, forming reactants |

## Le Chatelier Shifts

Imagine a balanced seesaw. Push down on one end and the system responds to relieve your push. That idea is **Le Chatelier's principle**: when you disturb a system at equilibrium, it shifts in the direction that partly cancels the disturbance.

### Amount and Pressure

Two disturbances move the equilibrium position without changing <InlineMath math="K" /> at all. First, **adding a reactant** pushes the reaction right to consume it, while adding a product pushes it left.

Second, **raising pressure by shrinking the volume** crowds the gas molecules, so the reaction shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas. When both sides carry the same number of gas moles, pressure changes cause no shift.

> Adding reactants or products, changing pressure, or inserting a catalyst never changes the numerical value of the equilibrium constant <InlineMath math="K" />.

Many learners think a catalyst increases the final yield. In reality, a catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse paths equally, so **a catalyst only shortens the time to reach equilibrium** without changing how much product you get.

</MathContainer>

### Heat and Haber Bosch

**Temperature is the only disturbance that changes <InlineMath math="K" />**. Treat heat like a substance in the equation: raise the temperature and the system absorbs that extra heat by shifting in the endothermic direction.

For an exothermic reaction (<InlineMath math="\Delta H < 0" />), heating shifts equilibrium left and shrinks <InlineMath math="K" />. For an endothermic reaction (<InlineMath math="\Delta H > 0" />), heating grows <InlineMath math="K" />. That curve explains the industrial trade-off behind the **Haber-Bosch process** used to manufacture fertilizer ammonia.

<BlockMath math="\mathrm{N_2}(g) + 3\mathrm{H_2}(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}(g), \qquad \Delta H = -92.2\text{ kJ}" />

Because <InlineMath math="4\text{ mol}" /> of gas become <InlineMath math="2\text{ mol}" />, factories apply high pressure to push the equilibrium right. A cold reactor would give a larger <InlineMath math="K" />, yet the reaction would crawl too slowly for production.

Engineers compromise with a **moderate <InlineMath math="400" /> to <InlineMath math="500^\circ\text{C}" /> and an iron catalyst**, while continually condensing and removing <InlineMath math="\mathrm{NH_3}" /> so equilibrium keeps shifting right.
