## Groups Decide Chemical Properties

Elements in **the same group** carry **the same number of valence electrons**, which gives them matching chemical behaviour. Group <InlineMath math="1" /> atoms hold one valence electron and lose it readily, Group <InlineMath math="17" /> atoms hold seven and attract one more, and Group <InlineMath math="18" /> atoms already hold a complete outer shell. Moving down any group adds extra electron shells and expands atomic radius.

> Opposite reactivity trends down Groups 1 and 17 come from whether atoms lose or gain electrons.

Because atomic size grows going down a group, the two flagship reactivity trends run in **opposite directions**. **Alkali metal reactivity increases** down Group <InlineMath math="1" /> because the outer electron sits further from the nucleus and escapes more easily. **Halogen reactivity decreases** down Group <InlineMath math="17" /> because the incoming electron sits further from the nucleus and feels a weaker pull.

## The Unreactive Noble Gases

Noble gases such as helium, neon, and argon possess a **stable duplet or octet arrangement**, so they neither lose, gain, nor share electrons. Avoid saying that noble gases stay unreactive because their outer shell is empty. Checking argon's arrangement (<InlineMath math="2.8.8" />) confirms that the outer shell is **completely full**. They exist as monatomic gases, and going down Group <InlineMath math="18" />, larger atoms strengthen van der Waals forces and lift boiling points.

Chemical inertness shapes how each gas is used. Light, non-flammable helium lifts weather balloons, argon fills filament bulbs to prevent hot tungsten from oxidising, and neon glows bright red-orange in electrical discharge signs.

## The Reactive Alkali Metals

Lithium, sodium, and potassium are **alkali metals**. They are soft, low in density, and melt at low temperatures that **decrease** down Group <InlineMath math="1" /> as metallic bonding weakens across larger atoms. Every alkali metal reacts with water to produce an alkaline metal hydroxide solution and hydrogen gas:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{Na(s)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}" />

Drop each metal into water containing phenolphthalein below. The indicator turns pink as hydroxide ions form, and the reaction grows noticeably faster from lithium to potassium.

Alkali metals also burn in oxygen to form basic oxides and react vigorously with chlorine gas to produce white chloride salts:

<BlockMath math="4\mathrm{Na(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Na_2O(s)}" />

Because moist air oxidises them rapidly, alkali metals are stored submerged under paraffin oil.

### Predicting Rubidium Reactivity

Rubidium sits directly below potassium in Group <InlineMath math="1" />. Predict **its behaviour in water** and write the balanced equation.

Rubidium carries an extra electron shell compared with potassium, placing its valence electron even further from nuclear attraction and lowering the energy needed to remove it. It reacts **more vigorously than potassium** following the same <InlineMath math="2 : 1" /> stoichiometry:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{Rb(s)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{RbOH(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}" />

Every Group <InlineMath math="1" /> metal forms a hydroxide <InlineMath math="\mathrm{MOH}" /> and hydrogen gas in the same molar ratio.

### Calculating Hydrogen Gas Volume

Suppose <InlineMath math="2.3\text{ g}" /> of sodium reacts completely with excess water. Find **the volume of hydrogen gas** collected at room conditions using <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{Na}) = 23" /> and molar volume <InlineMath math="24\text{ dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{Na}) = \frac{2.3}{23} = 0.10\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{0.10}{2} = 0.050\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0.050 \times 24 = 1.2\text{ dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

The balanced equation supplies the <InlineMath math="2 : 1" /> ratio, so two moles of sodium generate one mole of hydrogen gas.

## Halogens and Displacement Reactions

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and iodine are **halogens** that exist as **diatomic molecules** such as <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl_2}" /> held together by van der Waals forces. Moving down Group <InlineMath math="17" />, larger molecules create stronger intermolecular forces, so melting and boiling points climb steadily from gases (fluorine, chlorine) to a liquid (bromine) and a solid (iodine) at room temperature.

Chlorine also reacts with water to produce hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid, and reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide to form bleach:

## Period Three Trends and Oxides

Moving across Period <InlineMath math="3" /> from sodium to chlorine, proton number increases while the **number of electron shells stays constant at three**. Do not assume extra electrons make the atom larger across a period. Stronger nuclear charge pulls the same three shells inward, so **atomic radius shrinks** and electronegativity rises as elements shift from metals through a metalloid to non-metals.

Sodium oxide and magnesium oxide are **basic**, **aluminium oxide is amphoteric** (not merely basic) because it reacts with both acids and alkalis, and the oxides of silicon, phosphorus, and sulfur are **acidic**.

## Transition Elements Between Groups

**Transition elements** occupy the block between Group <InlineMath math="2" /> and Group <InlineMath math="13" />. Alongside typical metallic strength and high melting points, they display **four signature chemical properties**: forming coloured ions or compounds, exhibiting multiple oxidation numbers, acting as industrial catalysts, and forming complex ions. For instance, aqueous copper(II) sulfate appears blue, iron(II) ions appear pale green, iron(III) ions appear yellowish-brown, and solid iron catalyses ammonia synthesis in the Haber process.

## Applying Periodic Trends to Problems

Use <InlineMath math="A_r(\mathrm{K}) = 39" /> and **connect every explanation back to atomic size and shell count**, then check your reasoning against the solutions right below.

1. Write the balanced chemical equation for potassium burning in chlorine gas.

2. Explain why fluorine is far more reactive than iodine.

3. A sample of <InlineMath math="0.78\text{ g}" /> of potassium reacts completely with excess water. Calculate the volume of hydrogen gas released at room conditions.

4. Identify the Period <InlineMath math="3" /> oxide that reacts with both hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide, and state its acid-base classification.

**Answer 1**: Potassium loses one electron to form <InlineMath math="\mathrm{K^+}" /> and chlorine gains one electron to form <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Cl^-}" />, yielding potassium chloride:

<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{K(s)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{KCl(s)}" />

**Answer 2**: Fluorine has the smallest atomic radius in Group <InlineMath math="17" />, placing the incoming electron **closest to the nucleus** where electrostatic pull is strongest. Iodine carries more electron shells, so nuclear attraction on an extra electron is weaker and iodine reacts far more slowly.

**Answer 3**: Apply the <InlineMath math="2 : 1" /> molar ratio from the balanced water reaction:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="2\mathrm{K} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{KOH} + \mathrm{H_2}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{K}) = \frac{0.78}{39} = 0.020\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{0.020}{2} = 0.010\text{ mol}" />
<BlockMath math="V(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0.010 \times 24 = 0.24\text{ dm}^3" />
</MathContainer>

**Answer 4**: **Aluminium oxide**, <InlineMath math="\mathrm{Al_2O_3}" />, reacts with both acids and alkalis and is therefore classified as **amphoteric**. Sodium and magnesium oxides react only with acids, whereas non-metal oxides on the right react with alkalis.
