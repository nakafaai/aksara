## Quadratic Form Definitions

For a real symmetric matrix <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}" />, consider the quadratic form

<BlockMath math="q(x)=x^TAx" />

The complex version uses a Hermitian matrix and <InlineMath math="q(x)=x^HAx" />. The sign of this scalar classifies the matrix:

| Class | Condition for every nonzero <InlineMath math="x" /> |
| --- | --- |
| Positive definite | <InlineMath math="x^TAx>0" /> |
| Positive semidefinite | <InlineMath math="x^TAx\ge0" /> |
| Negative definite | <InlineMath math="x^TAx<0" /> |
| Negative semidefinite | <InlineMath math="x^TAx\le0" /> |
| Indefinite | the quadratic form takes both positive and negative values |

Symmetry is part of the usual definition. For a nonsymmetric real matrix,

<BlockMath math="x^TAx=x^T\left(\frac{A+A^T}{2}\right)x" />

This identity shows that the quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part of <InlineMath math="A" />.

## Spectral Criterion

The spectral theorem writes a real symmetric matrix as

<BlockMath math="A=Q\Lambda Q^T" />

In this decomposition, <InlineMath math="Q" /> is orthogonal and <InlineMath math="\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,ldots,lambda_n)" /> is real and diagonal. Substituting <InlineMath math="y=Q^Tx" /> gives

<BlockMath math="x^TAx=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i y_i^2" />

The calculation gives:

- <InlineMath math="A" /> is positive definite exactly when every <InlineMath math="\lambda_i>0" />.
- <InlineMath math="A" /> is positive semidefinite exactly when every <InlineMath math="\lambda_i\ge0" />.
- <InlineMath math="A" /> is indefinite exactly when it has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

Every diagonal entry of a positive definite matrix is positive because

<BlockMath math="a_{ii}=e_i^TAe_i>0" />

The converse is false. Positive diagonal entries alone do not control the cross terms.

## Ellipsoid Geometry

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is positive definite, then

<BlockMath math="E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:x^TAx=1\}" />

The set <InlineMath math="E" /> is an ellipsoid centered at the origin. In eigenvector coordinates, its equation becomes

<BlockMath math="\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i y_i^2=1" />

This equation shows that the principal axes of the ellipsoid follow the eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" />. The <InlineMath math="i" />th semiaxis has length <InlineMath math="1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}" />. Small eigenvalues produce longer semiaxes, while large eigenvalues produce shorter semiaxes.

## Principal Minor Tests

Let <InlineMath math="A_k" /> denote the upper-left <InlineMath math="k\times k" /> block of a real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix. Sylvester's criterion states

<BlockMath math="A\text{ is positive definite}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\det(A_k)>0\text{ for }k=1,ldots,n" />

This leading-principal-minor test does not extend by replacing <InlineMath math=">" /> with <InlineMath math="\ge" />. Positive semidefiniteness requires every principal minor to be nonnegative. Checking the leading ones alone is insufficient.

## Worked Two by Two Checks

Consider

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}" />

Its leading principal minors are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det(A_1)=5>0" />
<BlockMath math="\det(A)=20-1=19>0" />
</MathContainer>

Both leading principal minors are positive, so <InlineMath math="A" /> is positive definite. Both eigenvalues are also positive:

<BlockMath math="\lambda_1=\frac{9+\sqrt5}{2}\approx5.61803,\qquad \lambda_2=\frac{9-\sqrt5}{2}\approx3.38197" />

Now compare

<BlockMath math="B=\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\5&4\end{pmatrix}" />

Although both diagonal entries are positive,

<BlockMath math="\det(B)=4-25=-21<0" />

The negative determinant shows that the two eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="B" /> have opposite signs. Therefore, <InlineMath math="B" /> is indefinite.

## Cholesky Characterization

A real symmetric matrix is positive definite exactly when it has a Cholesky factorization

<BlockMath math="A=LL^T" />

In this factorization, <InlineMath math="L" /> is lower triangular and has strictly positive diagonal entries. Then

<BlockMath math="x^TAx=x^TLL^Tx=\|L^Tx\|_2^2>0\qquad(x\ne0)" />

Conversely, the Cholesky algorithm completes without a nonpositive pivot for every positive definite matrix. For a symmetric input in exact arithmetic, this completion tests positive definiteness. Once <InlineMath math="A=LL^T" /> is available, solve <InlineMath math="Ly=b" /> and then <InlineMath math="L^Tx=y" />.

## Gram Matrices from Transposed Products

For every real matrix <InlineMath math="M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}" />,

<BlockMath math="x^TM^TMx=\|Mx\|_2^2\ge0" />

This equation shows that <InlineMath math="M^TM" /> is always positive semidefinite. The matrix <InlineMath math="M^TM" /> is positive definite exactly when

<BlockMath math="\ker(M)=\{0\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{rank}(M)=n" />

Full column rank automatically implies <InlineMath math="m\ge n" />. It does not need to be assumed before the semidefinite statement.

More generally, a Gram matrix <InlineMath math="G_{ij}=\langle v_i,v_j\rangle" /> is positive semidefinite and becomes positive definite exactly when the vectors <InlineMath math="v_i" /> are linearly independent.

## Optimization Meaning

Consider the quadratic objective

<BlockMath math="F(x)=\frac12x^TAx-b^Tx+c" />

Its gradient and Hessian are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\nabla F(x)=Ax-b" />
<BlockMath math="\nabla^2F(x)=A" />
</MathContainer>

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is positive definite, <InlineMath math="F" /> is strictly convex and has the unique minimizer

<BlockMath math="x_*=A^{-1}b" />

Positive definiteness alone does not guarantee an easy or numerically stable computation. The curvature ratio

<BlockMath math="\kappa_2(A)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A)}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}" />

The condition number can still be very large. As a result, the objective has elongated contours and algorithms may run slowly or become sensitive to numerical error.

## Spectral Shifts and Inverses

For Hermitian <InlineMath math="A" />, the eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="A-tI" /> are <InlineMath math="\lambda_i(A)-t" />. Hence

<BlockMath math="A-tI\succ0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t<\lambda_{\min}(A)" />

If <InlineMath math="A\succ0" />, then <InlineMath math="A^{-1}\succ0" /> as well, with eigenvalues <InlineMath math="1/\lambda_i(A)" />. In numerical algorithms, solve the linear system directly because forming an explicit inverse requires more operations and can amplify rounding error.
