## Unitary Diagonalization Characterization

The complex spectral theorem characterizes exactly when eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormally. It connects the geometry of a unitary basis with the algebraic condition of normality.

For <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}" />, the following statements are equivalent:

1. There is an orthonormal basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}^n" /> consisting of eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" />.
2. The matrix is normal: <InlineMath math="A^HA=AA^H" />.
3. There is a unitary matrix <InlineMath math="U" /> and a diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> such that <InlineMath math="A=U\Lambda U^H" />.

The theorem is stronger than ordinary diagonalizability. A diagonalizable matrix can have a badly conditioned eigenvector basis, whereas <Highlight>a unitary basis preserves norms and angles</Highlight>.

## Proof of the First Direction

First, suppose an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors exists. We will show directly that the matrix is normal.

Let <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" /> be an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of matrix <InlineMath math="A" />. For each <InlineMath math="j = 1, \ldots, n" /> we have <InlineMath math="Av_j = \lambda_j v_j" />.

Since it's an orthonormal basis, we have the following sequence of calculations:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(A^H v_i)^H v_j = v_i^H (Av_j) = v_i^H (\lambda_j v_j)" />
<BlockMath math="= \lambda_j v_i^H v_j = \lambda_j \delta_{ij}" />
</MathContainer>

This means <InlineMath math="A^H v_i = \overline{\lambda_i} v_i" />, so that:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="AA^H v_i = A(\overline{\lambda_i} v_i) = \overline{\lambda_i} \lambda_i v_i" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \lambda_i \overline{\lambda_i} v_i \\ &amp;= \lambda_i (A^H v_i) \\ &amp;= A^H (\lambda_i v_i) \\ &amp;= A^H A v_i \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Since this holds for **all basis vectors**, then <InlineMath math="AA^H = A^H A" />, which means <InlineMath math="A" /> is normal.

## Proof from Normality to Diagonalization

Now we prove the converse by **mathematical induction**. The base case starts the argument, and the induction step reduces an <InlineMath math="n \times n" /> matrix to a normal <InlineMath math="(n-1) \times (n-1)" /> block.

### Mathematical Induction Structure

The proof is built by induction, so the steps below fix the base case, state the induction assumption, and then take the step from <InlineMath math="n-1" /> to <InlineMath math="n" />.

**Base Step**
For <InlineMath math="n = 0" />, the empty matrix satisfies the statement.

**Induction Hypothesis**
Assume the statement is true for all normal matrices of size <InlineMath math="(n-1) \times (n-1)" />.

**Induction Step**
Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}" /> be normal. Based on the fundamental theorem of algebra, there exists an eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 \in \mathbb{C}" /> of <InlineMath math="A" />. Let <InlineMath math="v_1 \in \mathbb{C}^n" /> be a corresponding eigenvector with <InlineMath math="v_1^H v_1 = 1" />.

We have <InlineMath math="Av_1 = \lambda_1 v_1" />. The following lemma shows that <InlineMath math="A^H v_1 = \overline{\lambda_1} v_1" /> as well.

### Why the Adjoint Has the Same Eigenvector

Let <InlineMath math="B=A-\lambda_1I" />. Since <InlineMath math="A" /> is normal, <InlineMath math="B" /> is normal, and therefore

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\|B^Hv_1\|_2^2=v_1^HBB^Hv_1" />
<BlockMath math="=v_1^HB^HBv_1=\|Bv_1\|_2^2=0" />
</MathContainer>

Hence <InlineMath math="B^Hv_1=0" />, which is exactly <InlineMath math="A^Hv_1=\overline{\lambda_1}v_1" />. This identity makes the **orthogonal complement** of <InlineMath math="v_1" /> invariant.

### Formation of Invariant Subspace

Complete <InlineMath math="v_1" /> to an orthonormal basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}^n" /> with vectors <InlineMath math="v_2, \ldots, v_n" />. Define:

<BlockMath math="W = \operatorname{span}(v_2, \ldots, v_n)" />

Here the span of vectors <InlineMath math="v_2, \ldots, v_n" /> is the set of all linear combinations of those vectors. In other words, <InlineMath math="W" /> contains all vectors that can be written as <InlineMath math="a_2 v_2 + a_3 v_3 + \cdots + a_n v_n" /> with <InlineMath math="a_2, a_3, \ldots, a_n \in \mathbb{C}" />.

For every <InlineMath math="w \in W" />, we have:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(Aw)^H v_1 = w^H (A^H v_1) = w^H (\overline{\lambda_1} v_1)" />
<BlockMath math="= \overline{\lambda_1} w^H v_1 = 0" />
</MathContainer>

So <InlineMath math="Aw \in W" />, which means:

<BlockMath math="A(W) = \{Aw \mid w \in W\} \subset W" />

In other words, <InlineMath math="W" /> is **invariant under** <InlineMath math="A" />: applying <InlineMath math="A" /> to any vector in <InlineMath math="W" /> produces another vector in <InlineMath math="W" />.

### Unitary Transformation and Preservation of Normality

Let <InlineMath math="T \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}" /> be a unitary matrix with columns <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" />. Then <InlineMath math="T \cdot T^H = T \cdot T^{-1} = I" />.

In this unitary basis, <InlineMath math="A" /> has the following block form:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="T^H \cdot A \cdot T = T^{-1} \cdot A \cdot T" />
<BlockMath math="= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0^T \\ 0 & A' \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The lower-right block <InlineMath math="A'" /> has size

<BlockMath math="A' \in \mathbb{C}^{(n-1) \times (n-1)}" />

Since unitary similarity transformations **preserve normality**, we can show that:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(T^H \cdot A \cdot T) \cdot (T^H \cdot A \cdot T)^H" />
<BlockMath math="= T^H \cdot A \cdot T \cdot T^H \cdot A^H \cdot T" />
<BlockMath math="= T^H \cdot A \cdot A^H \cdot T" />
<BlockMath math="= T^H \cdot A^H \cdot A \cdot T" />
<BlockMath math="= (T^H \cdot A \cdot T)^H \cdot (T^H \cdot A \cdot T)" />
</MathContainer>

The block diagonal matrix is also normal, which means <InlineMath math="A'" /> is also normal.

This proof uses <InlineMath math="\|Ax\|_2=\|A^Hx\|_2" /> for a normal matrix, because <InlineMath math="\|Ax\|_2^2=x^HA^HAx=x^HAA^Hx=\|A^Hx\|_2^2" />. Take <InlineMath math="x=(2,1)" />. The normal matrix <InlineMath math="N=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}" /> gives <InlineMath math="Nx=(1,3)" /> and <InlineMath math="N^Tx=(3,-1)" />, both of length <InlineMath math="\sqrt{10}" />. The shear <InlineMath math="J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}" /> is not normal, and <InlineMath math="Jx=(3,1)" /> is shorter than <InlineMath math="J^Tx=(2,3)" />.

## Construction of Complete Orthonormal Basis

By the **induction hypothesis**, there exists an orthonormal basis <InlineMath math="v'_2, \ldots, v'_n" /> of eigenvectors for <InlineMath math="A'" />. Let <InlineMath math="S'" /> be a unitary matrix with those columns, where:

<BlockMath math="S' \in \mathbb{C}^{(n-1) \times (n-1)}" />

In the basis <InlineMath math="S'" />, the matrix <InlineMath math="A'" /> becomes diagonal:

<BlockMath math="S'^H \cdot A' \cdot S' = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_2 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}" />

The diagonal entries <InlineMath math="\lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n" /> are the eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="A'" />.

Now we define:

<BlockMath math="S = T \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0^T \\ 0 & S' \end{pmatrix}" />

Then <InlineMath math="S" /> is a unitary matrix with:

<BlockMath math="S \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}" />

Substituting the matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> into the similarity transformation gives

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;S^H \cdot A \cdot S \\ &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} 1 &amp; 0^T \\ 0 &amp; S'^H \end{pmatrix} \cdot T^H \cdot A \cdot T \\ &amp;\quad{}\cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 &amp; 0^T \\ 0 &amp; S' \end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 &amp; 0^T \\ 0 &amp; S'^H A' S' \end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 &amp; &amp; \\ &amp; \ddots &amp; \\ &amp; &amp; \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />

The columns of <InlineMath math="S" /> form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of matrix <InlineMath math="A" />.

Take <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}" />. The first step picks the eigenvector <InlineMath math="u_1=e_3" /> for <InlineMath math="\lambda=5" />. Because <InlineMath math="A" /> is normal, it maps the plane <InlineMath math="u_1^\perp" /> into itself, so the construction continues inside that plane and finds <InlineMath math="u_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)" /> for <InlineMath math="\lambda=3" /> and <InlineMath math="u_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1,0)" /> for <InlineMath math="\lambda=1" />.

## Real Matrix Case

For real matrices, the distinction is whether the required eigenvalues and eigenvectors remain real. A real matrix:

<BlockMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" />

The real matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is called normal if

<BlockMath math="A \cdot A^T = A^T \cdot A" />

Real normal matrices are still unitarily diagonalizable over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" />, but their eigenvectors and eigenvalues need not be real. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" />, an orthogonal change of basis gives a block diagonal matrix with real eigenvalue blocks <InlineMath math="(\lambda)" /> and blocks

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}" />

Each <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> block represents a conjugate eigenvalue pair <InlineMath math="a\pm bi" />. A real normal matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" /> exactly when **all its eigenvalues are real**.

Symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices are always normal. For a symmetric matrix <InlineMath math="A^T = A" />:

<BlockMath math="A \cdot A^T = A \cdot A = A^T \cdot A" />

For an orthogonal matrix <InlineMath math="A^T = A^{-1}" />, we have:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A \cdot A^T = A \cdot A^{-1} = I" />
<BlockMath math="= A^{-1} \cdot A = A^T \cdot A" />
</MathContainer>

### Rotation Example

A **quarter turn** is represented by the matrix:

<BlockMath math="R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="R" /> is real and orthogonal, hence normal. Its eigenvalues are <InlineMath math="i" /> and <InlineMath math="-i" />, with the following normalized eigenvectors:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;u_+=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}, \\ &amp;u_-=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;U=(u_+\quad u_-), \\ &amp;U^HRU=\operatorname{diag}(i,-i) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

<InlineMath math="R" /> is unitarily diagonalizable over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" /> but cannot be diagonalized by a real orthogonal matrix. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" />, the rotation itself is the required <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> block.

The real normal matrix <InlineMath math="A=0.9R(30^\circ)" /> has no real eigenvector, because its eigenvalues <InlineMath math="0.9e^{\pm i\pi/6}" /> are complex. That is the geometric meaning of the real <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> block in the real normal form.
