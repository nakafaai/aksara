## Shifted Inverse Iteration

Shifted inverse iteration <Highlight>targets the eigenvalue nearest a chosen shift</Highlight> <InlineMath math="\mu" />. We first study a real symmetric matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" />. All its eigenvalues are real, and its eigenvectors can be chosen as an orthonormal basis.

Choose <InlineMath math="\mu" /> that is not itself an eigenvalue and a unit starting vector <InlineMath math="q_0" /> with a nonzero component in the desired eigenvector direction. Factor <InlineMath math="A-\mu I" /> once, then reuse that factorization.

Assume the targeted eigenvalue is **uniquely closest to the shift**. Equally close eigenvalues do not produce a unique dominant transformed magnitude. Convergence means convergence of the eigendirection. A negative transformed eigenvalue can alternate the vector sign.

### One Iteration

For <InlineMath math="k=0,1,2,\ldots" />, perform:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(A-\mu I)y_{k+1}=q_k" />
<BlockMath math="q_{k+1}=\frac{y_{k+1}}{\|y_{k+1}\|_2}" />
<BlockMath math="\rho_{k+1}=q_{k+1}^T A q_{k+1}" />
<BlockMath math="r_{k+1}=Aq_{k+1}-\rho_{k+1}q_{k+1}" />
</MathContainer>

The Rayleigh quotient <InlineMath math="\rho_{k+1}" /> estimates the eigenvalue. A reliable stopping rule **checks the residual** and accounts for the scale of the problem:

<BlockMath math="\|r_{k+1}\|_2 \leq \tau\bigl(\|A\|_2+|\rho_{k+1}|\bigr)" />

For an eigenpair satisfying <InlineMath math="Av_i=\lambda_i v_i" />, the shifted inverse acts as follows.

<BlockMath math="(A-\mu I)^{-1}v_i=\frac{1}{\lambda_i-\mu}v_i" />

This relation shows that the eigenvalue closest to <InlineMath math="\mu" /> produces the largest transformed magnitude. In implementation, we solve the linear system without forming <InlineMath math="(A-\mu I)^{-1}" /> explicitly.

## Power Iteration

Power iteration targets the eigenvalue of **largest magnitude**. Starting from a unit vector <InlineMath math="q_0" />, repeat:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="y_{k+1}=Aq_k" />
<BlockMath math="q_{k+1}=\frac{y_{k+1}}{\|y_{k+1}\|_2}" />
<BlockMath math="\rho_{k+1}=q_{k+1}^T A q_{k+1}" />
<BlockMath math="r_{k+1}=Aq_{k+1}-\rho_{k+1}q_{k+1}" />
</MathContainer>

For a symmetric matrix, convergence to the dominant eigenvector requires the following spectral gap.

<BlockMath math="|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|" />

The starting vector must also have a nonzero component in the direction of the eigenvector for <InlineMath math="\lambda_1" />. The asymptotic convergence factor is approximately <InlineMath math="|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|" />. Equal or tightly clustered dominant magnitudes can slow or prevent single-vector convergence.

## The Eigenvalue Each Method Finds

The word “smallest” is ambiguous, so the target should always be stated precisely.

| Method | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Power iteration on <InlineMath math="A" /> | Eigenvalue with largest absolute value |
| Inverse iteration with <InlineMath math="\mu=0" /> and invertible <InlineMath math="A" /> | Nonzero eigenvalue with smallest absolute value |
| Shifted inverse iteration | Eigenvalue closest to <InlineMath math="\mu" /> |
| Inverse iteration on a positive-definite matrix | Smallest algebraic eigenvalue, which is also smallest in absolute value |

For an indefinite matrix, the eigenvalue with **smallest absolute value need not** be the most negative eigenvalue. This distinction prevents a common interpretation error.

For <InlineMath math="A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)" /> from the example below, power iteration multiplies the component along <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" /> by <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" />, while inverse iteration with shift <InlineMath math="\mu=1" /> multiplies it by <InlineMath math="1/(\lambda_i-1)" />. The largest factor wins, so the first method heads to <InlineMath math="5" /> and the second to <InlineMath math="2" />.

## Worked Shifted Inverse Example

Consider

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\mu=1,\qquad q_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The shifted system for the first iteration gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="y_1=(A-I)^{-1}q_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1/4\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="q_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{17}}\begin{pmatrix}4\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\rho_1=q_1^TAq_1=\frac{37}{17}\approx2.176" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="2" /> is closer to the shift <InlineMath math="1" /> than <InlineMath math="5" />, further iterations drive the **second component toward zero** and <InlineMath math="\rho_k" /> toward <InlineMath math="2" />.

Check the first residual as well. The estimate is improving but has not converged:

<BlockMath math="r_1=\frac1{17\sqrt{17}}\begin{pmatrix}-12\\48\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|r_1\|_2=\frac{12}{17}" />

Both methods below start from the same violet vector <InlineMath math="q_0" />. Inverse iteration with shift <InlineMath math="\mu=1" /> multiplies the second component by <InlineMath math="1/4" /> at every step, so the teal segments <InlineMath math="q_k\propto(1,4^{-k})^T" /> fall onto the first axis, the eigenvector for <InlineMath math="2" />. Plain power iteration uses <InlineMath math="A" /> itself, so the orange segments <InlineMath math="p_k\propto(2^k,5^k)^T" /> climb toward the eigenvector for <InlineMath math="5" />. The shift decides which eigenvalue you get.

## Practical Checks

These checks decide whether a computed eigenvalue and eigenvector pair is correct without repeating the whole determinant. Work through them in order for the pair you obtained.

1. Factor the shifted matrix with a stable method and pivoting where appropriate. If the shift lands exactly on an eigenvalue, the shifted matrix is singular and the solve must be handled or the shift adjusted.
2. Stop using a **residual norm**. A small change in consecutive eigenvalue estimates alone does not prove that the eigenpair is accurate.
3. Eigenvector sign is arbitrary: <InlineMath math="q" /> and <InlineMath math="-q" /> represent the same eigendirection.
4. For clustered eigenvalues or general nonnormal matrices, use algorithms designed for multiple vectors or Schur form, using Lanczos for symmetric problems and Arnoldi or <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" />-based eigensolvers.

In the example above, <InlineMath math="q_k\propto(4^k,1)" /> gives <InlineMath math="\rho_k=\tfrac{2\cdot16^k+5}{16^k+1}" /> and the residual <InlineMath math="\|r_k\|_2=\tfrac{3\cdot4^k}{16^k+1}" />. For <InlineMath math="k=1" /> that is <InlineMath math="12/17" />, the value computed above, and every step divides it by about <InlineMath math="4" />.
