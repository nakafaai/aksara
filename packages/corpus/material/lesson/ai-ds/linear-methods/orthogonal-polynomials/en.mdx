## Orthogonality for Functions

Use real continuous functions on a finite interval <InlineMath math="a&lt;b" /> and an integrable weight that is positive in the interval interior. Integrable endpoint singularities are allowed, as in the Chebyshev weight below.

The weighted inner product and its induced norm are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int_a^b f(x)g(x)w(x)\,dx" />
<BlockMath math="\|f\|_w=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_w}" />
</MathContainer>

Two functions are orthogonal when <InlineMath math="\langle f,g\rangle_w=0" />. This weighted <InlineMath math="L^2" /> geometry measures the weighted squared error across the interval.

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\|f-g\|_w^2 \\ &amp;=\int_a^b|f(x)-g(x)|^2w(x)\,dx \end{aligned}" />

By contrast, the maximum norm measures the <Highlight>largest pointwise error</Highlight>.

<BlockMath math="\|f-g\|_\infty=\max_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)-g(x)|" />

Orthogonal projection minimizes the weighted <InlineMath math="L^2" /> error. Chebyshev polynomials also have a separate minimax property. The weighted inner product and the maximum norm measure different errors.

## Projection onto an Orthogonal Basis

Suppose <InlineMath math="p_0,\ldots,p_n" /> are mutually orthogonal. The degree-<InlineMath math="n" /> projection of <InlineMath math="f" /> is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Pi_n f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n c_kp_k(x)" />
<BlockMath math="c_k=\frac{\langle f,p_k\rangle_w}{\langle p_k,p_k\rangle_w}" />
</MathContainer>

Because cross terms vanish, <Highlight>each coefficient can be computed independently</Highlight>. This is the functional analogue of resolving a vector along perpendicular coordinate axes.

## Building the Sequence Step by Step

Applying Gram-Schmidt to <InlineMath math="1,x,x^2,\ldots" /> produces a monic orthogonal sequence <InlineMath math="\pi_k" />. Under a positive inner product it satisfies

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\pi_0(x)=1" />
<BlockMath math="\pi_1(x)=x-\alpha_0" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \pi_{k+1}(x) &amp;=(x-\alpha_k)\pi_k(x) \\ &amp;\quad{}-\beta_k\pi_{k-1}(x) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The recurrence coefficients are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\alpha_k=\frac{\langle x\pi_k,\pi_k\rangle_w}{\|\pi_k\|_w^2}" />
<BlockMath math="\beta_k=\frac{\|\pi_k\|_w^2}{\|\pi_{k-1}\|_w^2}>0" />
</MathContainer>

The three-term recurrence and <InlineMath math="\beta_k" /> apply for <InlineMath math="k\ge1" />.

Multiplication by <InlineMath math="x" /> raises the degree by one. Orthogonality removes every component below <InlineMath math="\pi_{k-1}" />, so the recurrence needs only **three terms**.

## Chebyshev Polynomials of the First Kind

On <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" />, choose

<BlockMath math="w(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}" />

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="T_0(x)=1,\qquad T_1(x)=x" />
<BlockMath math="T_{k+1}(x)=2xT_k(x)-T_{k-1}(x)" />
<BlockMath math="T_k(x)=\cos\bigl(k\arccos x\bigr)" />
</MathContainer>

Their weighted orthogonality is

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{T_j(x)T_k(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx \\ &amp;=\begin{cases}\pi,&amp;j=k=0,\\\frac{\pi}{2},&amp;j=k>0,\\0,&amp;j\ne k.\end{cases} \end{aligned}" />

Since <InlineMath math="|T_k(x)|\le1" /> on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" /> and the leading coefficient is <InlineMath math="2^{k-1}" /> for <InlineMath math="k\ge1" />, the monic polynomial <InlineMath math="2^{1-k}T_k" /> has the **smallest maximum norm** among all monic degree-<InlineMath math="k" /> polynomials. It therefore solves the minimax approximation problem for monic polynomials of degree <InlineMath math="k" />.

## Legendre Polynomials

For the unweighted inner product on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" />,

<BlockMath math="\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)g(x)\,dx" />

For this inner product, the standard Legendre polynomials satisfy

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P_0(x)=1,\qquad P_1(x)=x" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} (k+1)P_{k+1}(x) &amp;=(2k+1)xP_k(x) \\ &amp;\quad{}-kP_{k-1}(x) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Their orthogonality and normalization are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\int_{-1}^{1}P_j(x)P_k(x)\,dx=\frac{2}{2k+1}\delta_{jk}" />
<BlockMath math="\varphi_k(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2k+1}{2}}P_k(x)" />
<BlockMath math="\langle\varphi_j,\varphi_k\rangle=\delta_{jk}" />
</MathContainer>

For example,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P_2(x)=\frac12(3x^2-1)" />
<BlockMath math="x^2=\frac13P_0(x)+\frac23P_2(x)" />
</MathContainer>

The second identity gives the **exact orthogonal expansion**.

With the weight <InlineMath math="1" /> on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" />, Gram-Schmidt on <InlineMath math="1,x,x^2,x^3" /> gives the Legendre polynomials <InlineMath math="P_0=1" />, <InlineMath math="P_1=x" />, <InlineMath math="P_2=\tfrac12(3x^2-1)" />, and <InlineMath math="P_3=\tfrac12(5x^3-3x)" />, scaled so that <InlineMath math="P_k(1)=1" />.

## Changing the Interval of Orthogonal Polynomials

To map the interval <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" /> to <InlineMath math="[a,b]" />, use the following **affine change of variables**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}t" />
<BlockMath math="t=\frac{2x-a-b}{b-a}" />
<BlockMath math="dx=\frac{b-a}{2}\,dt" />
</MathContainer>

If <InlineMath math="\varphi_k" /> is orthonormal on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" />, then

<BlockMath math="\widetilde{\varphi}_k(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{b-a}}\,\varphi_k\left(\frac{2x-a-b}{b-a}\right)" />

The function <InlineMath math="\widetilde{\varphi}_k" /> is orthonormal on <InlineMath math="[a,b]" /> because

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\int_a^b\widetilde{\varphi}_j(x)\widetilde{\varphi}_k(x)\,dx \\ &amp;=\int_{-1}^{1}\varphi_j(t)\varphi_k(t)\,dt \\ &amp;=\delta_{jk} \end{aligned}" />

## Connection to Gaussian Quadrature

Let <InlineMath math="p_n" /> be the degree-<InlineMath math="n" /> polynomial orthogonal for the same positive weight and interval as an integral. Its <InlineMath math="n" /> roots are the Gaussian quadrature nodes. Suitable positive weights <InlineMath math="w_i" /> then give

<BlockMath math="\int_a^b f(x)w(x)\,dx\approx\sum_{i=1}^n w_i f(x_i)" />

The rule is **exact for every polynomial** of degree at most <InlineMath math="2n-1" />. Legendre roots produce Gauss-Legendre quadrature for the weight <InlineMath math="w(x)=1" />. Chebyshev roots produce Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature for <InlineMath math="w(x)=1/\sqrt{1-x^2}" />.

For two Gauss-Legendre nodes, <InlineMath math="P_2(x)=0" /> gives <InlineMath math="x=\pm1/\sqrt3" /> and both weights are <InlineMath math="1" />. Check the quadratic integral directly:

<BlockMath math="\int_{-1}^1x^2\,dx=\frac23=1\left(-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)^2+1\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)^2" />

| Basis | Weight on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" /> | Best suited to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chebyshev <InlineMath math="T_k" /> | <InlineMath math="1/\sqrt{1-x^2}" /> | stable interpolation grids, near-minimax approximation, spectral methods |
| Legendre <InlineMath math="P_k" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | unweighted <InlineMath math="L^2" /> projection and Gauss-Legendre quadrature |

Always match the polynomial family, inner product, approximation objective, and quadrature weight. Changing one of them changes the problem being solved.

The two-point Gauss rule uses the roots <InlineMath math="\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt3}" /> of <InlineMath math="P_2" /> as nodes, both with weight <InlineMath math="1" />. It integrates every cubic exactly: for <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^3+x^2" />, <InlineMath math="f(\tfrac1{\sqrt3})+f(-\tfrac1{\sqrt3})=\tfrac23=\int_{-1}^1f(x)\,dx" />.
