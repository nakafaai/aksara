## Why Numerical Methods Are Different

An eigenpair of a square matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> satisfies

<BlockMath math="Av=\lambda v,\qquad v\ne0" />

For a small symbolic matrix, one can solve <InlineMath math="\det(A-\lambda I)=0" />. Numerical software usually avoids forming and rooting the characteristic polynomial because its coefficients can be sensitive to rounding. When only part of the spectrum of a large sparse matrix is needed, Krylov methods use matrix-vector products without forming that polynomial. Dense algorithms for the complete spectrum instead work with matrix factorizations.

Different algorithms answer different questions:

- Power iteration estimates one eigenvalue of largest magnitude.
- Shifted inverse iteration targets an eigenvalue near a chosen shift.
- The <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> algorithm computes the complete spectrum of a dense matrix.
- Lanczos and Arnoldi methods target a small part of the spectrum of a large sparse matrix.

## Power Iteration

Choose a nonzero starting vector <InlineMath math="q_0" /> and normalize it. Repeat

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="z_k=Aq_{k-1}" />
<BlockMath math="q_k=\frac{z_k}{\|z_k\|_2}" />
<BlockMath math="\mu_k=q_k^TAq_k" />
</MathContainer>

The scalar <InlineMath math="\mu_k" /> is the Rayleigh quotient. For a diagonalizable matrix with a unique dominant magnitude,

<BlockMath math="|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots\ge|\lambda_n|" />

If the displayed inequality holds and the starting vector has a nonzero component in the dominant eigendirection, <InlineMath math="q_k" /> approaches that eigendirection. The asymptotic error commonly shrinks in proportion to

<BlockMath math="\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|^k" />

This explains two failure modes. Equal dominant magnitudes can prevent convergence to one vector, while a starting vector orthogonal to the dominant eigenspace never discovers it.

### Stop with the Residual

Do not stop only because consecutive eigenvalue estimates look similar. Check the eigenpair residual

<BlockMath math="r_k=Aq_k-\mu_kq_k" />

A scale-aware condition is

<BlockMath math="\|r_k\|_2\le \tau\bigl(\|A\|_2+|\mu_k|\bigr)" />

Here, <InlineMath math="\tau" /> is the requested tolerance. A small residual certifies that the computed pair nearly satisfies the eigenvalue equation, although it does not by itself identify which nearby eigenvalue was found.

## Worked Power Iteration Example

Consider

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="q_0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The first step gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="z_1=Aq_0=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="q_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\mu_1=q_1^TAq_1=\frac{14}{5}=2.8" />
</MathContainer>

The second step gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="q_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{41}}\begin{pmatrix}5\\4\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\mu_2=\frac{122}{41}\approx2.97561" />
</MathContainer>

The iterates approach

<BlockMath math="q_*=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_*=3" />

The other eigenvalue is <InlineMath math="1" />, so the expected convergence factor is <InlineMath math="|1/3|" /> per iteration after the asymptotic regime is reached.

## Shifted Inverse Iteration

Power iteration favors the largest magnitude. To target an eigenvalue near a shift <InlineMath math="\sigma" />, repeat

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(A-\sigma I)z_k=q_{k-1}" />
<BlockMath math="q_k=\frac{z_k}{\|z_k\|_2}" />
<BlockMath math="\mu_k=q_k^TAq_k" />
</MathContainer>

The eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="(A-\sigma I)^{-1}" /> are <InlineMath math="1/(\lambda_i-\sigma)" />, so the eigenvalue closest to <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> becomes dominant. Never form the inverse explicitly. Factor <InlineMath math="A-\sigma I" /> and solve a linear system at each iteration. A fixed shift allows the factorization to be reused.

If <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> is extremely close to an eigenvalue, the shifted system is ill conditioned. Accurate linear solves and residual checks are therefore essential.

## QR Algorithm for the Full Spectrum

Start with <InlineMath math="A_0=A" />. At iteration <InlineMath math="k" />, compute

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A_k=Q_kR_k" />
<BlockMath math="A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k=Q_k^TA_kQ_k" />
</MathContainer>

Every step is an orthogonal similarity transformation, so it preserves the eigenvalues. Practical <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> algorithms first reduce a dense matrix to Hessenberg form, use shifts, and deflate converged blocks. For a real nonsymmetric matrix, the limit is generally a real Schur form with <InlineMath math="1\times1" /> and <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> diagonal blocks. A symmetric matrix reduces to tridiagonal form and converges to a diagonal matrix.

## Choosing the Right Method

| Goal and matrix structure | Suitable method | Important check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One dominant eigenpair | Power iteration | unique dominant magnitude and nonzero starting component |
| Eigenpair near <InlineMath math="\sigma" /> | Shifted inverse iteration | stable solves with <InlineMath math="A-\sigma I" /> |
| Several extreme eigenpairs of a large symmetric sparse matrix | Lanczos | loss of orthogonality and residuals |
| Several eigenpairs of a large nonsymmetric sparse matrix | Arnoldi | subspace size, restarts, and residuals |
| Complete spectrum of a dense matrix | Shifted <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> to Schur form | converged diagonal or quasi-diagonal blocks |

The final acceptance test is always tied to the requested eigenpairs: verify residuals, preserve complex conjugate pairs for real matrices, and avoid claiming an eigenvector is accurate merely because an eigenvalue estimate has stabilized.
