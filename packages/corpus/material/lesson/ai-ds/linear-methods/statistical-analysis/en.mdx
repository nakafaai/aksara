## Start with the Statistical Model

Least squares becomes a statistical estimator only after we state how the observations were generated. Let

<BlockMath math="y=X\beta+\varepsilon" />

In this model, <InlineMath math="X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}" /> is a fixed design matrix, <InlineMath math="\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p" /> is the unknown parameter vector, and <InlineMath math="y\in\mathbb{R}^m" /> contains the observations. For ordinary Gaussian least squares, assume

<BlockMath math="\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_m)" />

The second assumption is that <InlineMath math="X" /> has full column rank. Under these two assumptions, the observation vector <InlineMath math="y" /> follows <InlineMath math="\mathcal N(X\beta,\sigma^2I_m)" />. Its mean is <InlineMath math="X\beta" />, and its covariance matrix is <InlineMath math="\sigma^2I_m" />.

## Fisher Information and Estimator Covariance

Up to a constant that does not depend on <InlineMath math="\beta" />, the log-likelihood is

<BlockMath math="\ell(\beta)=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2" />

Its score, Hessian, and Fisher information are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\nabla\ell(\beta)=\frac1{\sigma^2}X^T(y-X\beta)" />
<BlockMath math="\nabla^2\ell(\beta)=-\frac1{\sigma^2}X^TX" />
<BlockMath math="\mathcal I(\beta)=-\mathbb E[\nabla^2\ell(\beta)]=\frac1{\sigma^2}X^TX" />
</MathContainer>

<InlineMath math="X^TX" /> is the Fisher information only in the standardized case <InlineMath math="\sigma^2=1" />. Solving the score equation gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat\beta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty" />
<BlockMath math="\hat\beta=\beta+(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\varepsilon" />
</MathContainer>

Because a linear transformation of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian,

<BlockMath math="\hat\beta\sim\mathcal N\left(\beta,\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}\right)" />

The covariance is exactly the inverse Fisher information under this model:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}=\mathcal I(\beta)^{-1}" />

The diagonal entry <InlineMath math="c_{jj}" /> is the variance of <InlineMath math="\hat\beta_j" />. An off-diagonal entry <InlineMath math="c_{jk}" /> measures how two coefficient estimates vary together, and their correlation is

<BlockMath math="\rho_{jk}=\frac{c_{jk}}{\sqrt{c_{jj}c_{kk}}}" />

## Confidence Intervals When the Variance Is Unknown

If <InlineMath math="\sigma^2" /> is unknown and <InlineMath math="m>p" />, estimate it from the residuals:

<BlockMath math="s^2=\frac{\lVert y-X\hat\beta\rVert_2^2}{m-p}" />

Under the Gaussian model, an exact two-sided <InlineMath math="100(1-\alpha)\%" /> confidence interval for coefficient <InlineMath math="\beta_j" /> is

<BlockMath math="\hat\beta_j\pm t_{m-p,\,1-\alpha/2}\,s\sqrt{[(X^TX)^{-1}]_{jj}}" />

The interval is a repeated-sampling statement about the procedure. It is not the probability that a fixed parameter lies in one already computed interval.

## Stable Computation with QR

Let <InlineMath math="X=QR" /> be the reduced <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> decomposition, with <InlineMath math="Q^TQ=I_p" /> and nonsingular upper-triangular <InlineMath math="R" />. Then

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat\beta=R^{-1}Q^Ty" />
<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2R^{-1}R^{-T}" />
</MathContainer>

In numerical code, solve the triangular system <InlineMath math="R\hat\beta=Q^Ty" /> directly. This avoids forming <InlineMath math="R^{-1}" />. <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> also avoids squaring the condition number through <InlineMath math="X^TX" />.

## Weighted and Generalized Least Squares

Suppose instead that the errors have known positive-definite covariance <InlineMath math="\Omega" />:

<BlockMath math="\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\Omega)" />

Generalized least squares minimizes

<BlockMath math="(y-X\beta)^T\Omega^{-1}(y-X\beta)" />

The generalized least-squares solution, Fisher information, and estimator covariance are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}}=(X^T\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X^T\Omega^{-1}y" />
<BlockMath math="\mathcal I(\beta)=X^T\Omega^{-1}X" />
<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}})=(X^T\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}" />
</MathContainer>

For independent errors with variances <InlineMath math="\sigma_i^2" />, use <InlineMath math="\Omega=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_m^2)" />. The objective becomes

<BlockMath math="\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{(y_i-x_i^T\beta)^2}{\sigma_i^2}" />

Each observation <InlineMath math="i" /> receives weight <InlineMath math="1/\sigma_i^2" />. Whitening with <InlineMath math="W=\Omega^{-1/2}" /> converts the problem to ordinary least squares for <InlineMath math="\widetilde X=WX" /> and <InlineMath math="\widetilde y=Wy" />.

## Fitting a Weighted Linear Model

Consider three observations for an intercept and slope:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\5\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\Omega=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,4),\qquad \Omega^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,1,\frac14\right)" />
</MathContainer>

The third observation has standard deviation <InlineMath math="2" />, so it receives only one quarter of the weight of either of the first two. The weighted normal equations are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="X^T\Omega^{-1}X=\begin{pmatrix}\frac94&\frac32\\\frac32&2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="X^T\Omega^{-1}y=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{17}4\\\frac92\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Solving the weighted normal equations gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac79\\\frac53\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac89&-\frac23\\-\frac23&1\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The weighting has a probabilistic meaning only if the variance model is defensible. Incorrect weights can produce misleading standard errors, and exact Gaussian confidence intervals also require the linear mean, covariance, and normal-error assumptions to be adequate.
