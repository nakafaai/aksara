## What an Inner Product Adds

A vector space describes addition and scalar multiplication. An inner product adds geometry: it lets us measure length, angle, and orthogonality. This lesson uses the convention that a complex inner product is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second.

For <InlineMath math="x,y,z\in\mathbb{C}^n" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}" />, an inner product satisfies

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle \lambda x+\mu y,z\rangle=\overline\lambda\langle x,z\rangle+\overline\mu\langle y,z\rangle" />
<BlockMath math="\langle x,\lambda y+\mu z\rangle=\lambda\langle x,y\rangle+\mu\langle x,z\rangle" />
<BlockMath math="\langle y,x\rangle=\overline{\langle x,y\rangle}" />
<BlockMath math="\langle x,x\rangle\ge0,\qquad \langle x,x\rangle=0\Longleftrightarrow x=0" />
</MathContainer>

Conjugate symmetry guarantees that <InlineMath math="\langle x,x\rangle" /> is real. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^n" />, complex conjugation disappears, so the inner product is symmetric and bilinear.

## Matrix-Weighted Inner Products

For real vectors, the formula

<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^TAy" />

defines an inner product exactly when <InlineMath math="A" /> is symmetric positive definite. Symmetry of <InlineMath math="A" /> gives symmetry of the pairing, and <InlineMath math="x^TAx>0" /> for every nonzero <InlineMath math="x" /> gives strict positivity.

For complex vectors, the corresponding formula is

<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^HAy" />

where <InlineMath math="A" /> must be Hermitian positive definite. Merely saying that <InlineMath math="A" /> has positive entries or positive diagonal entries is not enough.

The standard Euclidean inner products are recovered with <InlineMath math="A=I" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle=x^Ty\qquad\text{over }\mathbb{R}^n" />
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle=x^Hy\qquad\text{over }\mathbb{C}^n" />
</MathContainer>

## Length, Angle, and Orthogonality

Every inner product induces the norm

<BlockMath math="\|x\|_A=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_A}" />

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

<BlockMath math="|\langle x,y\rangle_A|\le\|x\|_A\|y\|_A" />

ensures that, for nonzero real vectors, the angle is well defined by

<BlockMath math="\cos\theta_A=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle_A}{\|x\|_A\|y\|_A}" />

Vectors are <InlineMath math="A" />-orthogonal when <InlineMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=0" />. Changing <InlineMath math="A" /> changes the geometry, so vectors that are perpendicular in the Euclidean metric need not be perpendicular in a weighted metric.

## Worked Weighted Example

Let

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\lambda(A)=\{1,3\}" />
</MathContainer>

The positive eigenvalues confirm that <InlineMath math="A" /> is symmetric positive definite. Although <InlineMath math="x^Ty=0" />, the weighted geometry gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^TAy=1" />
<BlockMath math="\|x\|_A=\|y\|_A=\sqrt2" />
<BlockMath math="\cos\theta_A=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\sqrt2}=\frac12,\qquad \theta_A=60^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

Thus the same coordinate vectors are perpendicular in the Euclidean geometry but form a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> angle in the <InlineMath math="A" />-geometry.

## Complex Example

For the standard inner product, take

<BlockMath math="x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}" />

Then

<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle=x^Hy=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}=i-i=0" />

The conjugate in <InlineMath math="x^H" /> is essential. Using <InlineMath math="x^Ty" /> instead would not define the standard complex inner product because <InlineMath math="x^Tx" /> need not be a nonnegative real number.

## Projection in a Weighted Geometry

The projection of <InlineMath math="x" /> onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by nonzero <InlineMath math="u" /> is

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{proj}^{A}_{u}(x)=u\frac{\langle u,x\rangle_A}{\langle u,u\rangle_A}" />

Its residual is <InlineMath math="A" />-orthogonal to <InlineMath math="u" />:

<BlockMath math="\left\langle u,x-\operatorname{proj}^{A}_{u}(x)\right\rangle_A=0" />

This is the same geometric principle behind weighted least squares: the fitted residual is orthogonal to the model space under the chosen inner product.

## Matrix Representation and Gram Matrices

In the standard basis <InlineMath math="e_1,\ldots,e_n" />, every finite-dimensional inner product has a unique representation matrix with entries

<BlockMath math="a_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle" />

The matrix is symmetric positive definite over the reals and Hermitian positive definite over the complex numbers.

For vectors <InlineMath math="v_1,\ldots,v_k" />, their Gram matrix is

<BlockMath math="G_{ij}=\langle v_i,v_j\rangle_A" />

It is Hermitian positive semidefinite, and it is positive definite exactly when the vectors are linearly independent. Gram matrices turn geometric questions about angles and independence into matrix questions.
