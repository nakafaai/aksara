## Using Inner Products to Describe Geometry

A vector space has operations for addition and scalar multiplication. An inner product determines vector lengths, angles between vectors, and whether two vectors are orthogonal. For complex vectors, the inner product used below is **conjugate-linear in its first argument** and linear in its second.

For <InlineMath math="x,y,z\in\mathbb{C}^n" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}" />, an inner product satisfies

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle \lambda x+\mu y,z\rangle=\overline\lambda\langle x,z\rangle+\overline\mu\langle y,z\rangle" />
<BlockMath math="\langle x,\lambda y+\mu z\rangle=\lambda\langle x,y\rangle+\mu\langle x,z\rangle" />
<BlockMath math="\langle y,x\rangle=\overline{\langle x,y\rangle}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\langle x,x\rangle\ge0, \\ &amp;\langle x,x\rangle=0\Longleftrightarrow x=0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Conjugate symmetry guarantees that <InlineMath math="\langle x,x\rangle" /> is real. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^n" />, complex conjugation disappears, so the inner product is symmetric and bilinear.

## Matrix Weighted Inner Products

For real vectors, a matrix weighted inner product is written as:

<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^TAy" />

This formula defines an inner product exactly when <InlineMath math="A" /> is **symmetric positive definite**. Symmetry of <InlineMath math="A" /> makes the result symmetric in <InlineMath math="x" /> and <InlineMath math="y" />, while <InlineMath math="x^TAx>0" /> for every nonzero <InlineMath math="x" /> gives strict positivity.

For complex vectors, the corresponding formula is

<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^HAy" />

In this formula, <InlineMath math="A" /> must be Hermitian positive definite. Merely saying that <InlineMath math="A" /> has positive entries or positive diagonal entries is not enough.

The standard Euclidean inner products are recovered with <InlineMath math="A=I" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle=x^Ty\qquad\text{over }\mathbb{R}^n" />
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle=x^Hy\qquad\text{over }\mathbb{C}^n" />
</MathContainer>

## Vector Lengths with Angles and Orthogonality

Every inner product defines the following norm:

<BlockMath math="\|x\|_A=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_A}" />

This norm satisfies the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

<BlockMath math="|\langle x,y\rangle_A|\le\|x\|_A\|y\|_A" />

For nonzero real vectors, this inequality ensures that the angle can be defined by

<BlockMath math="\cos\theta_A=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle_A}{\|x\|_A\|y\|_A}" />

Vectors are <InlineMath math="A" />-orthogonal when <InlineMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=0" />. Changing <InlineMath math="A" /> changes the geometry, so vectors that are perpendicular in the Euclidean metric <Highlight>need not be perpendicular in a weighted metric</Highlight>.

## Comparing Euclidean and Weighted Angles

Let

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;A=\begin{pmatrix}2&amp;1\\1&amp;2\end{pmatrix}, \\ &amp;x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \\ &amp;y=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\lambda(A)=\{1,3\}" />
</MathContainer>

The positive eigenvalues confirm that <InlineMath math="A" /> is symmetric positive definite. Although <InlineMath math="x^Ty=0" />, the **weighted geometry gives**

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^TAy=1" />
<BlockMath math="\|x\|_A=\|y\|_A=\sqrt2" />
<BlockMath math="\cos\theta_A=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\sqrt2}=\frac12,\qquad \theta_A=60^\circ" />
</MathContainer>

The same coordinate vectors are perpendicular in the Euclidean geometry but form a <InlineMath math="60^\circ" /> angle in the <InlineMath math="A" />-geometry.

## Orthogonal Complex Vectors with Conjugation

For the standard inner product, take

<BlockMath math="x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}" />

Then

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \langle x,y\rangle &amp;=x^Hy \\ &amp;=\begin{pmatrix}1&amp;-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;=i-i \\ &amp;=0 \end{aligned}" />

The **conjugate in** <InlineMath math="x^H" /> is essential. Using <InlineMath math="x^Ty" /> instead would not define the standard complex inner product because <InlineMath math="x^Tx" /> need not be a nonnegative real number.

## Projection in a Weighted Geometry

The projection of <InlineMath math="x" /> onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by nonzero <InlineMath math="u" /> is

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{proj}^{A}_{u}(x)=u\frac{\langle u,x\rangle_A}{\langle u,u\rangle_A}" />

Its **residual is** <InlineMath math="A" />-orthogonal to <InlineMath math="u" />:

<BlockMath math="\left\langle u,x-\operatorname{proj}^{A}_{u}(x)\right\rangle_A=0" />

This is the same geometric principle behind weighted least squares: the fitted residual is orthogonal to the model space under the chosen inner product.

Using the same <InlineMath math="A" /> as above, project <InlineMath math="x=e_2" /> onto <InlineMath math="u=e_1" />. The weighted coefficient is <InlineMath math="1/2" />, not the Euclidean coefficient zero:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{proj}^{A}_{e_1}(e_2)=\frac12e_1,\qquad r=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad e_1^TAr=2(-1/2)+1=0" />

The orange residual <InlineMath math="r" /> doesn't look perpendicular to <InlineMath math="e_1" />, and in the Euclidean sense it isn't. Under <InlineMath math="A" /> it is, because <InlineMath math="e_1^TAr=0" />.

## Writing an Inner Product as a Matrix

In the standard basis <InlineMath math="e_1,\ldots,e_n" />, every finite-dimensional inner product has a unique representation matrix with entries

<BlockMath math="a_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle" />

The matrix is symmetric positive definite over the reals and Hermitian positive definite over the complex numbers.

For vectors <InlineMath math="v_1,\ldots,v_k" />, their Gram matrix is

<BlockMath math="G_{ij}=\langle v_i,v_j\rangle_A" />

The Gram matrix is Hermitian positive semidefinite. It is positive definite exactly when the vectors are **linearly independent**. Its entries record every pairwise inner product, so they can be used to check angles and linear independence.
