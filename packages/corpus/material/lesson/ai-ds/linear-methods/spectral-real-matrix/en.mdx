## When a Real Normal Matrix Has a Real Eigenbasis

For <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}" />, the following statements are equivalent:

1. There is an orthonormal basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^n" /> consisting of eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" />.
2. The matrix is normal and its characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> splits completely into linear factors over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" />.
3. There are a real orthogonal matrix <InlineMath math="Q" /> and a real diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> such that <InlineMath math="A=Q\Lambda Q^T" />.
4. The matrix is symmetric: <InlineMath math="A^T=A" />.

The equivalence explains the extra condition compared with [complex normal matrices](/en/subjects/ai-ds/linear-methods/spectral-complex-matrix). A complex normal matrix always has a unitary eigenbasis, but <Highlight>that basis and its eigenvalues need not be real</Highlight>.

The proof links the four statements directly. An orthonormal eigenbasis gives the columns of <InlineMath math="Q" />. From <InlineMath math="A=Q\Lambda Q^T" /> with real diagonal <InlineMath math="\Lambda" />, we obtain <InlineMath math="A^T=A" />. Conversely, the real spectral theorem orthogonally diagonalizes every real symmetric matrix. Finally, if a real normal matrix has only real eigenvalues, its complex unitary diagonalization satisfies <InlineMath math="A^H=A" />. Because <InlineMath math="A" /> is real, this is exactly <InlineMath math="A^T=A" />.

| Matrix class | Field | Guaranteed spectral form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complex normal | <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" /> | Unitary diagonalization. Eigenvalues may be complex |
| Complex Hermitian | <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" /> | Unitary diagonalization with real eigenvalues |
| Real symmetric | <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" /> | Orthogonal diagonalization with real eigenvalues |
| Real orthogonal | <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" /> | In general, the spectral form contains real rotation blocks. A real diagonal form exists only when all eigenvalues are real |

The symmetric matrix <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}" /> is normal, with real eigenvalues <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" /> and the orthonormal eigenvectors <InlineMath math="\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)" /> and <InlineMath math="\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)" />. It maps the unit circle to an ellipse whose axes lie along those eigenvectors.

## A Normal Matrix without a Real Eigenbasis

Consider the rotation through an angle <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> that is not an integer multiple of <InlineMath math="\pi" />:

<BlockMath math="R_\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}" />

The matrix is orthogonal, so <InlineMath math="R_\alpha^TR_\alpha=I=R_\alpha R_\alpha^T" /> and it is normal. Its characteristic polynomial is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\chi_{R_\alpha}(t)=(t-\cos\alpha)^2+\sin^2\alpha" />
<BlockMath math="=t^2-2(\cos\alpha)t+1" />
</MathContainer>

Its discriminant is

<BlockMath math="\Delta=4\cos^2\alpha-4=-4\sin^2\alpha<0" />

Because <InlineMath math="\alpha" /> is not an integer multiple of <InlineMath math="\pi" />, <InlineMath math="\sin\alpha\neq0" />. Thus the characteristic polynomial has **no real root**, and <InlineMath math="R_\alpha" /> has no real eigenvector basis. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" />, the eigenvalues are <InlineMath math="e^{i\alpha}" /> and <InlineMath math="e^{-i\alpha}" />.

At <InlineMath math="\alpha=\pi/2" />, the teal vector <InlineMath math="v=(1,1)^T" /> becomes the orange vector <InlineMath math="Rv=(-1,1)^T" />. Their dot product is zero. More generally, <InlineMath math="(x,y)\cdot(-y,x)=0" />, so every nonzero real vector turns by <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />, rather than staying on its original line.

## A Diagonalizable Matrix That Is Not Normal

Consider the following matrix over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" />:

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&2\end{pmatrix}" />

Its eigenvalues <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="2" /> are distinct, so the matrix is diagonalizable. The corresponding eigenspaces are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E_A(1)=\ker(A-I)=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="E_A(2)=\ker(A-2I)=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Using these eigenvectors as columns gives an explicit diagonalization:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;AS=SD \\ &amp;\Longrightarrow \\ &amp;S^{-1}AS=D \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

However, the two eigenvectors are not orthogonal because

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}=3\neq0" />

Direct multiplication also shows that <InlineMath math="A" /> is not normal:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="AA^T=\begin{pmatrix}10&6\\6&4\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\3&13\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

This example separates the two ideas: ordinary diagonalization permits any invertible eigenvector matrix <InlineMath math="S" />, while <Highlight>orthogonal diagonalization requires an orthonormal eigenbasis</Highlight> and therefore a real symmetric matrix.

The matrix <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}" /> has the distinct eigenvalues <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="2" />, so it is diagonalizable, with eigenvectors <InlineMath math="(1,0)" /> and <InlineMath math="(1,1)" />. They meet at <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />, not <InlineMath math="90^\circ" />: <InlineMath math="A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&5\end{pmatrix}" /> differs from <InlineMath math="AA^T=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}" />, so <InlineMath math="A" /> is not normal and has no orthonormal eigenbasis.
