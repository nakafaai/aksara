## Quadratic Forms with Symmetric Matrices

A quadratic equation with a <Highlight>cross term</Highlight> does not state its principal directions directly. Its quadratic form collects the coefficients in a symmetric matrix, and the eigenvectors of that matrix give the principal directions. In two dimensions, let

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="A" /> is real and symmetric, it has real eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1,\lambda_2" /> and an orthonormal eigenbasis <InlineMath math="v_1,v_2" />. Put those eigenvectors into <InlineMath math="S=(v_1\quad v_2)" />. Then <InlineMath math="S^TS=I" /> and <InlineMath math="S^TAS=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)" />.

For the equation <InlineMath math="x^TAx+p^Tx+f=0" />, use the rotated coordinates <InlineMath math="\xi=S^Tx" /> and the rotated linear coefficient <InlineMath math="(\delta,\epsilon)^T=S^Tp" />. The cross term disappears:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lambda_1 \xi_1^2 + \lambda_2 \xi_2^2 + \delta \xi_1 + \epsilon \xi_2 + f = 0" />
</MathContainer>

## Completing the Square

For both variables with <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \neq 0" />, the completing the square process is performed separately:

Name the completed-square brackets to keep both **added and subtracted terms** visible:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="B_1=\xi_1^2+2\frac\delta{2\lambda_1}\xi_1 +\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1^2}" />

  <BlockMath math="B_2=\xi_2^2+2\frac\epsilon{2\lambda_2}\xi_2 +\frac{\epsilon^2}{4\lambda_2^2}" />

  <BlockMath math="0=\lambda_1B_1-\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1} +\lambda_2B_2-\frac{\epsilon^2}{4\lambda_2}+f" />
</MathContainer>

Collecting the completed squares gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 0 &amp;= \lambda_1 \left( \xi_1 + \frac{\delta}{2\lambda_1} \right)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \lambda_2 \left( \xi_2 + \frac{\epsilon}{2\lambda_2} \right)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \left( f - \frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1} - \frac{\epsilon^2}{4\lambda_2} \right) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Define the center in rotated coordinates and the translated constant by

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(m_1,m_2)=\left(-\frac{\delta}{2\lambda_1},-\frac{\epsilon}{2\lambda_2}\right)" />
<BlockMath math="\gamma=\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1}+\frac{\epsilon^2}{4\lambda_2}-f" />
</MathContainer>

Then

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lambda_1 (\xi_1 - m_1)^2 + \lambda_2 (\xi_2 - m_2)^2 - \gamma \\ &amp;= 0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The signs of <InlineMath math="\lambda_1" />, <InlineMath math="\lambda_2" />, and <InlineMath math="\gamma" /> determine whether the real solution set is an ellipse, a hyperbola, a parabola, a degenerate conic, or empty. The next two cases assume <InlineMath math="\gamma>0" />.

## Curve Classification

With **positive translated constant** <InlineMath math="\gamma" />, two nonzero eigenvalues of the same positive sign give an ellipse, while opposite signs give a hyperbola. A zero eigenvalue requires checking the remaining linear term: it yields a parabola only when that term is nonzero.

### Two Positive Eigenvalues

If <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 > 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 > 0" />, then the conic section formed is an **ellipse**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\frac{(\xi_1 - m_1)^2}{r_1^2} + \frac{(\xi_2 - m_2)^2}{r_2^2} = 1" />
</MathContainer>

With semi-axis lengths <InlineMath math="r_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\lambda_1}}" /> in the direction of <InlineMath math="v_1" /> and <InlineMath math="r_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\lambda_2}}" /> in the direction of <InlineMath math="v_2" />.

### One Zero Eigenvalue

A special case occurs when <InlineMath math="\lambda_1\neq0" />, <InlineMath math="\lambda_2=0" />, and <InlineMath math="\epsilon\neq0" />. Completing the square gives

Keep the same completed-square brackets visible, because the added and subtracted terms must cancel separately:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="B_1=\xi_1^2+2\frac\delta{2\lambda_1}\xi_1 +\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1^2}" />

  <BlockMath math="0=\lambda_1B_1-\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1} +\epsilon\xi_2+f" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 0 &amp;= \lambda_1 \left( \xi_1 + \frac{\delta}{2\lambda_1} \right)^2 + \epsilon \xi_2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \left( f - \frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1} \right) \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="= \lambda_1 (\xi_1 - m_1)^2 + \epsilon \xi_2 - \gamma" />
</MathContainer>

Here the zero eigenvalue means we use only the first completed square, so define:

<BlockMath math="m_1=-\frac{\delta}{2\lambda_1},\qquad \gamma=\frac{\delta^2}{4\lambda_1}-f" />

Solving for <InlineMath math="\xi_2" /> shows the **parabola directly**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\xi_2 = -\frac{\lambda_1}{\epsilon} (\xi_1 - m_1)^2 + \frac{\gamma}{\epsilon}" />
</MathContainer>

## Rotating and Translating a Two Dimensional Conic

Consider

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="5x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2-6x_1-9=0" />
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad p=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\0\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The eigenpairs are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\lambda_1=6, \\ &amp;v_1=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}, \\ &amp;\lambda_2=1, \\ &amp;v_2=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="S=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \xi=S^Tx" />
</MathContainer>

The **center in the original coordinates** is <InlineMath math="h=(1,-1)^T" /> because <InlineMath math="2Ah+p=0" />. In rotated coordinates, <InlineMath math="m=S^Th=(1/\sqrt5,-3/\sqrt5)^T" />. With <InlineMath math="\eta=\xi-m" />, the equation becomes

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="6\eta_1^2+\eta_2^2=12" />
<BlockMath math="\frac{\eta_1^2}{2}+\frac{\eta_2^2}{12}=1" />
</MathContainer>

It is an ellipse centered at <InlineMath math="(1,-1)" />. Its semiaxes have lengths <InlineMath math="\sqrt2" /> along <InlineMath math="v_1" /> and <InlineMath math="2\sqrt3" /> along <InlineMath math="v_2" />. Substituting the center into the original equation gives <InlineMath math="-12" />, which confirms the translated right-hand side.

## Quadratic Surfaces and Transformation

For a symmetric matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" />, vector <InlineMath math="b \in \mathbb{R}^n" />, and scalar <InlineMath math="c \in \mathbb{R}" />, the **quadratic surface** <InlineMath math="Q" /> is defined as the solution set of the general quadratic equation:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x^T A x + b^T x + c = 0" />
</MathContainer>

Writing the same equation entry by entry gives:

<BlockMath math="\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^n x_ja_{jk}x_k +\sum_{j=1}^n b_jx_j+c=0" />

If <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" /> is symmetric and <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n" /> is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with <InlineMath math="A \cdot v_i = \lambda_i \cdot v_i" />, then the orthonormal matrix <InlineMath math="S = (v_1 \quad \ldots \quad v_n)" /> enables diagonalization <InlineMath math="A \cdot S = S \cdot \Lambda" /> or <InlineMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S = S^T \cdot A \cdot S" />.

In the rotated coordinates <InlineMath math="\xi=S^Tx" />, write <InlineMath math="\mu=S^Tb" />. The coordinates <InlineMath math="\xi\in\mathbb R^n" /> of points on <InlineMath math="Q" /> satisfy:

<BlockMath math="\xi^T S^T A S \xi + b^T S \xi + c = 0" />

Diagonalizing <InlineMath math="A" /> gives:

<BlockMath math="\xi^T\Lambda\xi+\mu^T\xi+c=0" />

Written entry by entry:

<BlockMath math="\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j\xi_j^2 +\sum_{j=1}^n\mu_j\xi_j+c=0" />

Collecting the terms and moving <InlineMath math="c" /> to the right gives:

<BlockMath math="\sum_{j=1}^n (\lambda_j \xi_j^2 + \mu_j \xi_j) = -c" />

In the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, the quadratic form has a diagonal structure. This transformation is called **principal axis transformation** because the new coordinate axes are <Highlight>aligned with the directions of the matrix eigenvectors</Highlight>.
