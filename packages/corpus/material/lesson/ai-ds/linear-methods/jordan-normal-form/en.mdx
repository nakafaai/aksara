## When diagonalization is not possible

Diagonalization requires a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors. Such a basis does not always exist. A weaker result is still available: if the characteristic polynomial of <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> splits into linear factors over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" />, then an invertible matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> transforms <InlineMath math="A" /> into an upper triangular matrix.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="R=S^{-1}AS" />
<BlockMath math="R=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&*&\cdots&*\\0&\lambda_2&\cdots&*\\\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&\cdots&0&\lambda_n\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The diagonal entries are the eigenvalues, repeated according to their algebraic multiplicities. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" />, every characteristic polynomial splits. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" />, this condition can fail when nonreal eigenvalues occur.

Jordan normal form sharpens this triangular description. It records the eigenvalues and shows exactly <Highlight>how many eigenvectors are missing from a basis</Highlight>.

## One Jordan block

A Jordan block of size <InlineMath math="s" /> for the eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="J_s(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1&0&\cdots&0\\0&\lambda&1&\ddots&\vdots\\\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\0&\cdots&0&\lambda&1\\0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="J_s(\lambda)=\lambda I_s+N_s,\qquad N_s^s=0" />
</MathContainer>

The **nilpotent matrix** <InlineMath math="N_s" /> has ones on the superdiagonal and zeros elsewhere. A block of size <InlineMath math="1" /> is simply <InlineMath math="(\lambda)" />. A larger block signals that one eigenvector must be extended by generalized eigenvectors.

The block <InlineMath math="J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}" /> has the single eigenvalue <InlineMath math="2" />, yet only one eigendirection. The vector <InlineMath math="e_2" /> does not go to <InlineMath math="2e_2" /> but to <InlineMath math="2e_2+e_1" />, so <InlineMath math="(J-2I)e_2=e_1" /> and <InlineMath math="e_2" /> is a generalized eigenvector.

## Generalized eigenvector chains

Vectors <InlineMath math="v_1,\ldots,v_s" /> form a Jordan chain for <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> when

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(A-\lambda I)v_1=0" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;(A-\lambda I)v_{j+1}=v_j, \\ &amp;j=1,\ldots,s-1 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The **first vector** is an ordinary eigenvector. We require <InlineMath math="v_1\neq0" />. The recurrence then forces the chain vectors to be linearly independent. Each following vector maps to the previous one after applying <InlineMath math="A-\lambda I" />. In the ordered basis <InlineMath math="(v_1,\ldots,v_s)" />, the restriction of <InlineMath math="A" /> to this chain is represented by <InlineMath math="J_s(\lambda)" />.

## The complete Jordan form

Suppose the characteristic polynomial of <InlineMath math="A" /> has the following form.

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_i-t)^{a_i}" />

If this polynomial splits over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" /> with distinct eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k" />, there is an invertible matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> giving the following block-diagonal form. Group blocks associated with the same eigenvalue:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;D_i=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(J_{s_{i1}}(\lambda_i), \\ &amp;\ldots,J_{s_{iq_i}}(\lambda_i)\bigr) \\ &amp;i=1,\ldots,k \end{aligned}" />

  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;S^{-1}AS=J \\ &amp;=\operatorname{diag}(D_1,\ldots,D_k) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

For each eigenvalue, the block sizes satisfy

<BlockMath math="s_{i1}+\cdots+s_{iq_i}=a_i" />

The **number of blocks** for <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" /> equals the geometric multiplicity <InlineMath math="\dim\ker(A-\lambda_iI)" />. The block sizes are uniquely determined by <InlineMath math="A" />, although their order along the diagonal is not.

| Question | What the Jordan blocks show |
| --- | --- |
| Is <InlineMath math="A" /> diagonalizable? | Yes exactly when every Jordan block has size <InlineMath math="1" />. |
| How many independent eigenvectors belong to <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" />? | One for each Jordan block associated with <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" />. |
| Why is a repeated eigenvalue not enough to decide diagonalizability? | The same algebraic multiplicity can be split among blocks of different sizes. |

## Reading Jordan Blocks from a Matrix

Consider

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}=J_2(1)\oplus J_1(2)" />

Its characteristic polynomial is

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t)=(1-t)^2(2-t)" />

For <InlineMath math="\lambda=1" />,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A-I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\ker(A-I)=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right\}" />
</MathContainer>

The eigenvalue <InlineMath math="1" /> has algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="2" /> but **only one independent eigenvector**. Therefore <InlineMath math="A" /> is not diagonalizable. Consider the following two vectors.

<BlockMath math="v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}" />

The two vectors form a Jordan chain because <InlineMath math="(A-I)v_1=0" /> and <InlineMath math="(A-I)v_2=v_1" />. The eigenvalue <InlineMath math="2" /> contributes the separate block <InlineMath math="J_1(2)" />.

Suppose the eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> has Jordan blocks of sizes <InlineMath math="3" /> and <InlineMath math="1" />. Each block of size <InlineMath math="s" /> adds <InlineMath math="\min(k,s)" /> to <InlineMath math="\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)^k" />, so the dimensions run <InlineMath math="2,3,4,4" />. The first value counts the blocks, and the sequence stops growing at <InlineMath math="4=\mu_A(\lambda)" />.

## Matrix Powers from Jordan Blocks

Writing <InlineMath math="J_s(\lambda)=\lambda I+N" /> with <InlineMath math="N^s=0" /> makes powers explicit:

For integers <InlineMath math="m\ge0" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda\neq0" />:

<BlockMath math="J_s(\lambda)^m=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,s-1)}\binom{m}{j}\lambda^{m-j}N^j" />

When <InlineMath math="\lambda=0" />, use <InlineMath math="J_s(0)^m=N^m" /> directly, with <InlineMath math="N^0=I" />. For example, the nonzero superdiagonal accumulates with each multiplication:

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^3=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix}" />

This formula explains why powers of a non-diagonalizable matrix contain **polynomial factors** in <InlineMath math="m" /> in addition to powers of the eigenvalues. It gives the exact matrix powers used to analyze recurrences and differential equations.

Jordan form is primarily an exact algebraic classification. It is highly sensitive to small numerical perturbations, so numerical software usually computes a Schur decomposition for stable calculations.

Follow the magenta points below from <InlineMath math="x_0=e_2" />. With <InlineMath math="J_2(0.8)^m=\begin{pmatrix}0.8^m&m\cdot0.8^{m-1}\\0&0.8^m\end{pmatrix}" />, both eigenvalues equal <InlineMath math="0.8<1" />, so everything eventually decays to zero. Still, the first component first grows to <InlineMath math="2.048" /> at <InlineMath math="m=4" /> and <InlineMath math="m=5" />, because of the factor <InlineMath math="m" /> from the Jordan block. The diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="0.8I" /> only shrinks <InlineMath math="e_2" /> straight down, like the gray points.
