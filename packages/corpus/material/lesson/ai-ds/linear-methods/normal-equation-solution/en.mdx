## What the System Solves

Given <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}" /> and <InlineMath math="b \in \mathbb{R}^m" />, a linear least-squares problem asks for the parameter vector that makes the residual as small as possible:

<BlockMath math="\min_x \|Ax-b\|_2^2" />

Every minimizer satisfies the normal equation

<BlockMath math="A^T A\hat{x}=A^T b" />

This equation is <Highlight>always consistent</Highlight>. The minimizer is unique exactly when <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A)=n" />, which requires <InlineMath math="m\ge n" />. In that full-column-rank case,

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}=(A^T A)^{-1}A^T b" />

When <InlineMath math="A" /> is rank deficient, the fitted vector <InlineMath math="A\hat{x}" /> is still unique, but the parameter vector is not. If <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> is one solution, then every solution is in

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}+\ker(A)" />

### Why a Solution Always Exists

The column space of <InlineMath math="A" /> is a finite-dimensional closed subspace of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^m" />. Therefore, <InlineMath math="b" /> has a **unique orthogonal projection** onto it. Any <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> satisfying <InlineMath math="A\hat{x}=\operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{col}(A)}b" /> is a least-squares solution.

The normal equation expresses the same geometry because

<BlockMath math="A^T(A\hat{x}-b)=0" />

This equation says that the residual <InlineMath math="A\hat{x}-b" /> is orthogonal to every column of <InlineMath math="A" />.

## Moore Penrose Pseudoinverse

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is defined for every real matrix, including rectangular and rank-deficient matrices. Write its singular value decomposition as

<BlockMath math="A=U\Sigma V^T" />

The displayed factorization is a singular value decomposition. To form the pseudoinverse, replace every nonzero singular value <InlineMath math="\sigma_i" /> with <InlineMath math="1/\sigma_i" /> and leave every zero singular value at zero. This gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\Sigma^{\dagger}_{ii}=\begin{cases}1/\sigma_i,&\sigma_i>0,\\0,&\sigma_i=0,\end{cases}" />
<BlockMath math="A^{\dagger}=V\Sigma^{\dagger}U^T" />
<BlockMath math="x^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}b" />
</MathContainer>

The vector <InlineMath math="x^{\dagger}" /> is <Highlight>the unique least-squares solution with minimum Euclidean norm</Highlight>. When <InlineMath math="A" /> has full column rank, the general definition simplifies to

<BlockMath math="A^{\dagger}=(A^T A)^{-1}A^T" />

The pseudoinverse is uniquely characterized by the four Penrose identities:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="AA^{\dagger}A=A" />
<BlockMath math="A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}" />
<BlockMath math="(AA^{\dagger})^T=AA^{\dagger}" />
<BlockMath math="(A^{\dagger}A)^T=A^{\dagger}A" />
</MathContainer>

Take <InlineMath math="\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(2,\,0.5,\,0)" />. The pseudoinverse inverts each positive singular value and keeps the zero: <InlineMath math="\Sigma^\dagger=\operatorname{diag}(0.5,\,2,\,0)" />. A small singular value becomes a large entry, which is why near-zero singular values make <InlineMath math="x^\dagger" /> sensitive.

## Direct Solution with QR Decomposition

For a full-column-rank matrix with <InlineMath math="m\ge n" />, use the thin <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> decomposition

<BlockMath math="A=Q_1R_1,\qquad Q_1^TQ_1=I" />

The least-squares objective separates into

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \|Ax-b\|_2^2 &amp;=\|R_1x-Q_1^Tb\|_2^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+\|(I-Q_1Q_1^T)b\|_2^2 \end{aligned}" />

The second term does not depend on <InlineMath math="x" />. Therefore, the minimizer is obtained by solving the **upper-triangular system**

<BlockMath math="R_1\hat{x}=Q_1^Tb" />

This route solves the original least-squares problem directly. It does not form <InlineMath math="A^TA" /> and then solve the normal equation.

## Worked Polynomial Fit Example

The numerical factors and coefficients below are **rounded to five decimal places**. The exact matrix equalities hold before rounding.

Fit <InlineMath math="y=at^2+bt+c" /> to seven observations. The design matrix and observation vector are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}9&-3&1\\4&-2&1\\1&-1&1\\0&0&1\\1&1&1\\4&2&1\\9&3&1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="b=\begin{pmatrix}-2.2\\-4.2\\-4.2\\-1.8\\1.8\\8.2\\15.8\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Each row of <InlineMath math="A" /> is <InlineMath math="[t_i^2,t_i,1]" />. One valid thin <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> decomposition is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}-0.64286&-0.56695&0.16496\\-0.28571&-0.37796&-0.24744\\-0.07143&-0.18898&-0.49487\\0&0&-0.57735\\-0.07143&0.18898&-0.49487\\-0.28571&0.37796&-0.24744\\-0.64286&0.56695&0.16496\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} R_1 &amp;=\begin{pmatrix}-14&amp;0&amp;-2\\0&amp;5.29150&amp;0\\0&amp;0&amp;-1.73205\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Signs of matching columns of <InlineMath math="Q_1" /> and rows of <InlineMath math="R_1" /> may change between valid <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> implementations. The product <InlineMath math="Q_1R_1" /> and final fit do not change.

First compute

<BlockMath math="Q_1^Tb=\begin{pmatrix}-9.71429\\16.02569\\3.48060\end{pmatrix}" />

Then use **back substitution**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;-1.73205x_3=3.48060 \\ &amp;\Longrightarrow \\ &amp;x_3=-2.00952 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;5.29150x_2=16.02569 \\ &amp;\Longrightarrow \\ &amp;x_2=3.02857 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;-14x_1-2x_3=-9.71429 \\ &amp;\Longrightarrow \\ &amp;x_1=0.98095 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0.98095\\3.02857\\-2.00952\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \hat{y}(t) &amp;=0.98095t^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+3.02857t -2.00952 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\|A\hat{x}-b\|_2\approx0.41861" />
</MathContainer>

## Choosing a Numerical Method

| Method | Approximate dense cost for <InlineMath math="m\ge n" /> | Main strength | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal equations and Cholesky | form <InlineMath math="A^TA" /> in <InlineMath math="O(mn^2)" />, then factor in about <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{3}n^3" /> flops | fast when the problem is well conditioned and full rank | <InlineMath math="\kappa_2(A^TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2" /> |
| Householder <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> | about <InlineMath math="2mn^2-\frac{2}{3}n^3" /> flops | avoids forming <InlineMath math="A^TA" /> and is usually more stable | requires a rank-aware variant when columns are dependent |
| <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SVD}" /> | more work than <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> | determines rank and gives the **minimum-norm solution** | highest cost of the three |

Use <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> as the usual choice for dense, full-rank problems. Use <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SVD}" /> when the matrix is rank deficient, severely ill conditioned, or requires a minimum-norm solution. Form the normal matrix only when the speed gain is worth the loss of numerical stability. Do not compute an inverse explicitly in production code.

Forming <InlineMath math="A^TA" /> squares the condition number, <InlineMath math="\kappa(A^TA)=\kappa(A)^2" />. With about <InlineMath math="16" /> digits in double precision, a problem with <InlineMath math="\kappa(A)=10^6" /> can lose about <InlineMath math="6" /> digits through <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> but about <InlineMath math="12" /> through the normal equations.
