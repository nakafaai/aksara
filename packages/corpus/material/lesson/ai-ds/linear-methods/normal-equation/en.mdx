## Conditions for a Least Squares Minimum

The normal equation converts the first-order optimality condition for a linear least-squares problem into a linear system.

The least squares problem seeks the <InlineMath math="x" /> that keeps <InlineMath math="A \cdot x" /> as close to <InlineMath math="b" /> as possible:

<BlockMath math="\min_x \|A \cdot x - b\|_2^2" />

A minimizer satisfies the following linear system:

<BlockMath math="A^T A \cdot x = A^T b" />

This is called the **normal equation** because the residual at a minimizer <Highlight>is normal, or perpendicular, to the column space</Highlight> of <InlineMath math="A" />.

For the example at the end of this lesson, <InlineMath math="\|Ax-b\|_2^2=(x-2)^2+(2x-1)^2=5x^2-8x+5" />. The minimum sits where the derivative <InlineMath math="10x-8" /> vanishes, which is exactly the normal equation <InlineMath math="A^TAx=A^Tb" />, here <InlineMath math="5x=4" />.

## Least Squares and the Normal Equation

A vector <InlineMath math="\hat{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n" /> minimizes <InlineMath math="\|A \cdot x - b\|_2^2" /> if and only if it satisfies <InlineMath math="A^T A \cdot \hat{x} = A^T b" />. The optimization problem and the normal equation therefore have the **same solution set**. That equivalence is what turns a minimization problem into a linear system you can solve with elimination or a factorization.

## The Residual Is Orthogonal to the Column Space

When <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> minimizes <InlineMath math="\|A\hat{x} - b\|_2^2" />, the residual vector <InlineMath math="A\hat{x} - b" /> is **orthogonal to every vector** in the column space of <InlineMath math="A" />.

This column space consists of all vectors that can be written as <InlineMath math="Ax" /> for <InlineMath math="x \in \mathbb{R}^n" />. The orthogonality condition means

<BlockMath math="0 = \langle Ax, A\hat{x} - b \rangle" />

This orthogonality condition holds for every vector <InlineMath math="x \in \mathbb{R}^n" />. The properties of the inner product give

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} 0 &amp;= (Ax)^T (A\hat{x} - b) \\ &amp;= x^T (A^T A\hat{x} - A^T b) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Because this equality holds for every vector <InlineMath math="x" />, the vector in parentheses must be zero:

<BlockMath math="A^T A\hat{x} - A^T b = 0" />

Rearranging this equation gives <InlineMath math="A^TA\hat{x}=A^Tb" />, the normal equation.

## Proof Using Pythagorean Theorem

We can also verify this result in a different way. Let <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> be the solution of the normal equation system and <InlineMath math="x" /> be any vector in <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^n" />.

Using the Pythagorean theorem, we can write

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\|Ax - b\|_2^2 \\ &amp;= \|A\hat{x} - b - A(\hat{x} - x)\|_2^2 \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \|A\hat{x} - b\|_2^2 + \|A(\hat{x} - x)\|_2^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}- 2 \langle A\hat{x} - b, A(\hat{x} - x) \rangle \end{aligned}" />
  <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;=\|A\hat x-b\|_2^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+\underbrace{\|A(\hat x-x)\|_2^2}_{\ge0} \\ &amp;\quad{}-2\underbrace{(\hat x-x)^T(A^TA\hat x-A^Tb)}_{=0} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Because <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> satisfies the normal equation, the **cross term is zero**. This leaves:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \|Ax - b\|_2^2 &amp;= \|A\hat{x} - b\|_2^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \|A(\hat{x} - x)\|_2^2 \end{aligned}" />

The added squared norm is nonnegative. Therefore, the squared norm of the residual at any <InlineMath math="x" /> cannot be smaller than at <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" />:

<BlockMath math="\|Ax-b\|_2^2\geq\|A\hat{x}-b\|_2^2" />

This inequality proves that <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" /> indeed gives the minimum value.

## Existence and Uniqueness

The normal equation is consistent for every matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}" /> and every vector <InlineMath math="b \in \mathbb{R}^m" /> because a least-squares minimizer always exists in finite dimensions. The minimizer is unique exactly when <InlineMath math="A" /> has **full column rank**:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A)=n" />

In that case, <InlineMath math="A^{\mathsf T}A" /> is invertible and

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}=(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}b" />

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is rank deficient, the normal equation still has solutions, but they are not unique. For any particular solution <InlineMath math="\hat{x}" />, the complete set is

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}+\ker(A)" />

All these parameter vectors produce the same fitted vector <InlineMath math="A\hat{x}" />.

When <InlineMath math="A" /> lacks full column rank, the minimizer is not unique. For <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}" /> and <InlineMath math="b=(1,2)" />, every <InlineMath math="x" /> with <InlineMath math="x_1+2x_2=1" /> solves <InlineMath math="Ax=b" /> exactly. Among them, <InlineMath math="(\tfrac15,\tfrac25)" /> has the smallest norm, because it is perpendicular to the line.

## Null Space and definiteness proof

For every <InlineMath math="z\in\mathbb{R}^n" />,

<BlockMath math="z^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Az=\|Az\|_2^2\ge0" />

This equation shows that <InlineMath math="A^{\mathsf T}A" /> is always symmetric positive semidefinite. Moreover,

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;A^{\mathsf T}Az=0 \\ &amp;\Longleftrightarrow \\ &amp;z^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Az=0 \\ &amp;\Longleftrightarrow \\ &amp;Az=0 \end{aligned}" />

The calculation gives:

<BlockMath math="\ker(A^{\mathsf T}A)=\ker(A)" />

This **equality of null spaces** shows that <InlineMath math="A^{\mathsf T}A" /> is positive definite exactly when <InlineMath math="A" /> has full column rank.

## Numerical warning

Positive definiteness gives uniqueness and permits a Cholesky solve, but it **does not guarantee good conditioning**. For a full-column-rank matrix,

<BlockMath math="\kappa_2(A^{\mathsf T}A)=\kappa_2(A)^2" />

Explicitly forming <InlineMath math="A^{\mathsf T}A" /> can therefore magnify sensitivity and rounding effects. The inverse formula proves an algebraic identity. In software, use <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> for a well-determined full-rank least-squares solve and <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SVD}" /> when rank deficiency or severe ill-conditioning matters.

## An Example with Two Observations

Let <InlineMath math="A=(1,2)^T" /> and <InlineMath math="b=(2,1)^T" />. One scalar parameter cannot match both observations: the first equation asks for <InlineMath math="x=2" />, while the second asks for <InlineMath math="x=1/2" />. The least-squares solution balances their **squared residuals**.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A^TA=1^2+2^2=5,\qquad A^Tb=1\cdot2+2\cdot1=4" />
<BlockMath math="5\hat x=4\Rightarrow\hat x=\frac45" />
<BlockMath math="A\hat x=\begin{pmatrix}4/5\\8/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad r=A\hat x-b=\begin{pmatrix}-6/5\\3/5\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A^Tr=1\cdot(-6/5)+2\cdot(3/5)=0" />
</MathContainer>

The residual is perpendicular to the line of possible predictions. Moving along that line adds a nonnegative squared distance, as the Pythagorean proof requires.
