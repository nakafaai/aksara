## Definitions and Entrywise Meaning

A real square matrix <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}" /> is **symmetric** when

<BlockMath math="A^T=A" />

Entry by entry, the same condition is <InlineMath math="a_{ji}=a_{ij}" />. For example,

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&5\\3&5&6\end{pmatrix}" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is symmetric because its entries match across the main diagonal.

A complex square matrix <InlineMath math="H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}" /> is **Hermitian** when

<BlockMath math="H^H=H" />

Entry by entry, the Hermitian condition means <InlineMath math="h_{ji}=\overline{h_{ij}}" />. An example with complex entries is

<BlockMath math="H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1+i\\1-i&3\end{pmatrix}" />

> Every real symmetric matrix is Hermitian when viewed over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" />. The converse is false because a Hermitian matrix may have complex off-diagonal entries.

On the diagonal, the Hermitian condition gives <InlineMath math="h_{ii}=\overline{h_{ii}}" />, so every diagonal entry is real.

## Real and Complex Inner Products

The transpose and conjugate transpose must not be mixed. For <InlineMath math="x\in\mathbb{R}^n" />,

<BlockMath math="x^Tx=\sum_{k=1}^n x_k^2\geq0" />

For <InlineMath math="x\in\mathbb{C}^n" />, use the conjugate transpose:

<BlockMath math="x^Hx=\sum_{k=1}^n\overline{x_k}x_k=\sum_{k=1}^n|x_k|^2\geq0" />

In either field, the relevant expression equals zero exactly when <InlineMath math="x=0" />. For a complex vector, <InlineMath math="x^Tx" /> need not even be real, which is why complex geometry uses <InlineMath math="x^Hx" />.

## Quadratic and Hermitian Forms

A real symmetric matrix defines the quadratic form

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="q:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}" />
<BlockMath math="q(x)=x^TAx" />
</MathContainer>

A Hermitian matrix defines the real-valued Hermitian form

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="q:\mathbb{C}^n\to\mathbb{R}" />
<BlockMath math="q(x)=x^HHx" />
</MathContainer>

To prove that <InlineMath math="x^HHx" /> is real, take its complex conjugate.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\overline{x^HHx}=(x^HHx)^H" />
<BlockMath math="=x^HH^Hx=x^HHx" />
</MathContainer>

A scalar equals its complex conjugate exactly when it is real. In the real case, the skew-symmetric part of any matrix contributes nothing to a quadratic form:

<BlockMath math="x^TBx=x^T\left(\frac{B+B^T}{2}\right)x" />

The skew-symmetric part contributes nothing because <InlineMath math="x^T(B-B^T)x=0" />. Thus, quadratic forms depend only on the symmetric part.

## Why Hermitian Eigenvalues Are Real

Suppose <InlineMath math="Hv=\lambda v" /> with <InlineMath math="v\neq0" />. Hermitian symmetry gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lambda v^Hv=v^HHv" />
<BlockMath math="=v^HH^Hv=(Hv)^Hv=\overline{\lambda}v^Hv" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="v^Hv>0" />, it follows that <InlineMath math="\lambda=\overline{\lambda}" /> and therefore <InlineMath math="\lambda\in\mathbb{R}" />. Real symmetric matrices inherit the same conclusion.

## Why Distinct Eigenspaces Are Orthogonal

Let <InlineMath math="Hv=\lambda v" /> and <InlineMath math="Hw=\mu w" /> with <InlineMath math="\lambda\neq\mu" />. Because both eigenvalues are real,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lambda w^Hv=w^HHv" />
<BlockMath math="=w^HH^Hv=(Hw)^Hv=\mu w^Hv" />
</MathContainer>

Hence <InlineMath math="(\lambda-\mu)w^Hv=0" />, so <InlineMath math="w^Hv=0" />. With a repeated eigenvalue, arbitrary eigenvectors inside the same eigenspace may fail to be orthogonal. Gram-Schmidt can produce an orthonormal basis within that eigenspace.

## Spectral Decomposition and Quadratic Geometry

The spectral theorem collects these facts into a complete decomposition:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=A^T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\qquad A=Q\Lambda Q^T" />
<BlockMath math="H=H^H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}:\qquad H=U\Lambda U^H" />
</MathContainer>

The columns of <InlineMath math="Q" /> or <InlineMath math="U" /> form an orthonormal eigenbasis, and every diagonal entry of <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> is real. If <InlineMath math="u_1,\ldots,u_n" /> are the columns of <InlineMath math="U" />, then

<BlockMath math="x^HHx=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j|u_j^Hx|^2" />

This formula shows immediately that <InlineMath math="H" /> is positive definite exactly when every eigenvalue is positive.

## Worked Hermitian Diagonalization

For the earlier matrix

<BlockMath math="H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1+i\\1-i&3\end{pmatrix}" />

For the matrix <InlineMath math="H" />, the characteristic polynomial is

<BlockMath math="\det(H-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-4)" />

Normalized eigenvectors for <InlineMath math="\lambda_1=1" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2=4" /> are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="u_1=\frac1{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}-(1+i)\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="u_2=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}1+i\\2\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

They satisfy <InlineMath math="u_1^Hu_2=0" />. With <InlineMath math="U=(u_1\quad u_2)" />,

<BlockMath math="U^HHU=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)" />

This equation gives a unitary diagonalization that can be checked directly.
