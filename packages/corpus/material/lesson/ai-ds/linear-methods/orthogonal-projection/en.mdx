## The Best Approximation Theorem

Let <InlineMath math="V" /> be a real inner-product space and let <InlineMath math="S\subseteq V" /> be a finite-dimensional subspace. For every <InlineMath math="f\in V" />, there is a unique vector <InlineMath math="g\in S" /> such that

<BlockMath math="\|f-g\|=\min_{\varphi\in S}\|f-\varphi\|" />

The vector <InlineMath math="g" /> is the orthogonal projection of <InlineMath math="f" /> onto <InlineMath math="S" />, written <InlineMath math="g=P_Sf" />. It is characterized by two conditions:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g\in S" />
<BlockMath math="f-g\in S^{\perp}" />
</MathContainer>

Equivalently,

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\langle f-g,\varphi\rangle=0 \\ &amp;\text{for every }\varphi\in S \end{aligned}" />

In a Hilbert space the same theorem holds for <Highlight>every closed subspace</Highlight>. A finite-dimensional subspace is automatically closed.

## Why Orthogonality Gives the Minimum

Let <InlineMath math="g=P_Sf" /> and choose any <InlineMath math="\varphi\in S" />. Since <InlineMath math="g-\varphi\in S" /> and <InlineMath math="f-g\perp S" />, the **Pythagorean identity** gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="f-\varphi=(f-g)+(g-\varphi)" />
<BlockMath math="\|f-\varphi\|^2=\|f-g\|^2+\|g-\varphi\|^2" />
<BlockMath math="\|f-\varphi\|^2\ge\|f-g\|^2" />
</MathContainer>

Equality holds only when <InlineMath math="\varphi=g" />, which proves both optimality and uniqueness.

## Formula for an Orthonormal Basis

If <InlineMath math="\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n" /> is an orthonormal basis of <InlineMath math="S" />, then

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P_Sf=\sum_{i=1}^n\langle f,\varphi_i\rangle\varphi_i" />
<BlockMath math="\|f-P_Sf\|^2=\|f\|^2-\sum_{i=1}^n|\langle f,\varphi_i\rangle|^2" />
</MathContainer>

The second line follows by splitting <InlineMath math="f" /> into the **perpendicular components** <InlineMath math="P_Sf" /> and <InlineMath math="f-P_Sf" />.

For a real matrix <InlineMath math="Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}" /> with orthonormal columns,

<BlockMath math="P=QQ^T" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="P" /> projects vectors in <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^m" /> onto <InlineMath math="\operatorname{col}(Q)" />.

## Projecting a Vector onto a One Dimensional Subspace

The two vectors are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="f=\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="u=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Project the vector <InlineMath math="f" /> onto <InlineMath math="S=\operatorname{span}\{u\}" />. The **projection coefficient** is

<BlockMath math="\frac{\langle f,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}=\frac{1\cdot2+3\cdot1}{2^2+1^2}=1" />

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g=P_Sf=u=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="r=f-g=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\langle r,u\rangle=(-1)2+(2)1=0" />
<BlockMath math="\|f-g\|_2=\sqrt5" />
</MathContainer>

## Construction from an Arbitrary Basis

Let <InlineMath math="\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_n" /> be any basis of <InlineMath math="S" /> and write

<BlockMath math="g=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i\psi_i" />

The orthogonality conditions give the **Gram system**

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\sum_{i=1}^n\langle\psi_i,\psi_k\rangle\alpha_i=\langle f,\psi_k\rangle, \\ &amp;k=1,\ldots,n \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="G\alpha=c" />
</MathContainer>

In this system, <InlineMath math="G_{ki}=\langle\psi_i,\psi_k\rangle" /> and <InlineMath math="c_k=\langle f,\psi_k\rangle" />. Since the basis vectors are linearly independent,

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;z^TGz=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^nz_i\psi_i\right\|^2>0 \\ &amp;\text{for }z\ne0 \end{aligned}" />

The inequality above shows that <InlineMath math="G" /> is symmetric positive definite, so the projection coefficients are unique.

If the columns of a full-column-rank matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> are the basis vectors, then

<BlockMath math="P=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="P" /> is the same orthogonal projector. This formula gives the algebraic form of the projector. For numerical computation, obtain the projection with <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> or <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SVD}" /> because forming <InlineMath math="A^TA" /> squares the condition number.

## Function Space Example

Project <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2" /> onto <InlineMath math="S=\operatorname{span}\{1,x\}" /> using

<BlockMath math="\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)g(x)\,dx" />

The functions <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="x" /> are orthogonal. Their coefficients are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="c_0=\frac{\langle x^2,1\rangle}{\langle1,1\rangle}=\frac{2/3}{2}=\frac13" />
<BlockMath math="c_1=\frac{\langle x^2,x\rangle}{\langle x,x\rangle}=0" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="P_Sf(x)=\frac13" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \|f-P_Sf\|_2^2 &amp;=\int_{-1}^{1}\left(x^2-\frac13\right)^2dx \\ &amp;=\int_{-1}^1\left(x^4-\frac23x^2+\frac19\right)dx\\&amp;=\frac25-\frac49+\frac29=\frac{8}{45} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The linear term vanishes because <InlineMath math="x^3" /> is **odd on the symmetric interval**.

The shaded area shows the error that the projection leaves. Its squared norm, <InlineMath math="\int_{-1}^1\bigl(x^2-\tfrac13\bigr)^2dx=\tfrac8{45}" />, is the smallest over all functions <InlineMath math="c_0+c_1x" />.

## Properties of an Orthogonal Projector

| Property | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="P^2=P" /> | projecting twice changes nothing |
| <InlineMath math="P^T=P" /> | the range and null space are orthogonal |
| <InlineMath math="\operatorname{range}(P)=S" /> | every output lies in the target subspace |
| <InlineMath math="\ker(P)=S^\perp" /> | perpendicular components are removed |

Idempotence alone is not enough. An oblique projector also satisfies <InlineMath math="P^2=P" /> and may be asymmetric. <Highlight>Symmetry distinguishes the Euclidean orthogonal projector</Highlight>.

For <InlineMath math="u=(2,1)" />, the projector is <InlineMath math="P=\tfrac{uu^T}{u^Tu}=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}" />. Then <InlineMath math="Pf=(2,1)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="S" />, <InlineMath math="(I-P)f=(-1,2)" /> lies on <InlineMath math="S^\perp" />, and <InlineMath math="P(Pf)=Pf" />, because a vector already in <InlineMath math="S" /> does not move.
