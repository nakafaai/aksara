## Introduction to Complex Numbers

The imaginary unit <InlineMath math="i" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="i^2=-1" />. A complex number combines a real and an imaginary coordinate, so it can represent both scaling and rotation in one scalar.

The set of complex numbers is defined as <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C} := \{z = x + iy : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}" />. In every complex number <InlineMath math="z = x + iy" />, the part <InlineMath math="x = \text{Re}(z)" /> is called the **real part** and the part <InlineMath math="y = \text{Im}(z)" /> is called the **imaginary part**.

Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by a nonzero number stay inside <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" />. With these operations, <InlineMath math="(\mathbb{C}, +, \cdot)" /> is a **field**, so its elements can serve as scalars for a vector space.

### Conjugate and Modulus

For <InlineMath math="z=x+iy" />, the **complex conjugate** is <InlineMath math="\overline z=x-iy" />. Since <InlineMath math="z\overline z=x^2+y^2" />, conjugation lets us compute both magnitudes and reciprocals, for example <InlineMath math="z^{-1}=\overline z/|z|^2" /> when <InlineMath math="z\neq0" />.

The **modulus** or absolute value of a complex number is calculated with the formula:

<BlockMath math="|z| = \sqrt{z \cdot \overline{z}} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}" />

This modulus gives the "distance" of the complex number from the origin in the complex plane.

## Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The fundamental theorem of algebra says that every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root.

Now, let's look at a very important result. Every non-constant polynomial

<BlockMath math="p(z) = a_n z^n + a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0, \quad z \in \mathbb{C}" />

with degree <InlineMath math="n \geq 1" /> and complex coefficients <InlineMath math="a_k \in \mathbb{C}" /> for <InlineMath math="k = 0, 1, \ldots, n" /> with <InlineMath math="a_n \neq 0" />, has at least one complex root. This means there exists a number <InlineMath math="z_* \in \mathbb{C}" /> such that <InlineMath math="p(z_*) = 0" />.

Applying the theorem repeatedly shows that a degree-<InlineMath math="n" /> polynomial factors completely over <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" />:

<BlockMath math="p(z)=a_n\prod_{j=1}^n(z-z_j)" />

Thus it has exactly <InlineMath math="n" /> complex roots when multiplicities are counted. The qualification "nonconstant" matters: a nonzero constant polynomial has no root.

## Definition and Axioms of Complex Vector Space

A complex vector space uses numbers from <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" /> as scalars. The operations must be closed in the stated sets and obey the same algebraic compatibility rules as a real vector space.

A set <InlineMath math="V" /> with an addition operation

<BlockMath math="+ : V \times V \to V : (x, y) \mapsto x + y" />

and a scalar multiplication operation

<BlockMath math="\cdot : \mathbb{C} \times V \to V : (\lambda, x) \mapsto \lambda \cdot x" />

is called a **complex vector space** if it satisfies the following axioms:

### Vector Addition Axioms

1. **Associative**: <InlineMath math="(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)" /> for all <InlineMath math="x, y, z \in V" />

2. **Commutative**: <InlineMath math="x + y = y + x" /> for all <InlineMath math="x, y \in V" />

3. **Identity Element**: There exists an element <InlineMath math="0 \in V" /> such that <InlineMath math="x + 0 = x = 0 + x" /> for all <InlineMath math="x \in V" />

4. **Inverse Element**: For every <InlineMath math="x \in V" /> there exists an element <InlineMath math="-x \in V" /> such that <InlineMath math="x + (-x) = 0 = (-x) + x" />

### Scalar Multiplication Axioms

5. **Associative multiplication**: <InlineMath math="(\lambda \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot (\mu \cdot x)" /> for all <InlineMath math="\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{C}, x \in V" />

6. **Unit element**: <InlineMath math="1 \cdot x = x" /> for all <InlineMath math="x \in V" />

### Distributive Axioms

7. **Distributive over vector addition**: <InlineMath math="\lambda \cdot (x + y) = \lambda \cdot x + \lambda \cdot y" /> for all <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{C}, x, y \in V" />

8. **Distributive over scalar addition**: <InlineMath math="(\lambda + \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot x + \mu \cdot x" /> for all <InlineMath math="\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{C}, x \in V" />

Elements <InlineMath math="x \in V" /> are called **vectors**, and elements <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{C}" /> are called **scalars**.

## A Concrete Example

The set <InlineMath math="V=\mathbb C^2" /> is a complex vector space with componentwise operations. For instance,

<BlockMath math="(1+i)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1+i\\1-i\end{pmatrix}" />

The scalar field changes how we count dimensions. The set <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" /> itself has basis <InlineMath math="\{1\}" /> as a complex vector space, but basis <InlineMath math="\{1,i\}" /> as a real vector space:

| Same set of vectors | Allowed scalars | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" /> | <InlineMath math="\mathbb R" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> |

## Differences from Real Vector Spaces

Purely algebraic results derived from the vector-space axioms work over both <InlineMath math="\mathbb R" /> and <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" />. Results that use order or a real-only inner product need their assumptions adjusted. For example, complex inner products use conjugation, and complex numbers have no field order compatible with their arithmetic.

The scalar field also decides which maps are linear. Complex conjugation is linear over <InlineMath math="\mathbb R" /> but not over <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" />, because <InlineMath math="\overline{iz}=-i\overline z" /> rather than <InlineMath math="i\overline z" />. Always state the scalar field before discussing a basis, dimension, or linear map.
