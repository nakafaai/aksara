## Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Most vectors change both length and direction under a matrix transformation. For an eigenvector, the result stays on the same line. For a real eigenvalue, a positive value preserves its orientation, a negative eigenvalue reverses it, and a zero value sends it to the zero vector.

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> be a square matrix. An **eigenvector** <InlineMath math="v \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> for an **eigenvalue** <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" /> is a non-zero vector <InlineMath math="v \neq 0" /> that satisfies:

<BlockMath math="A \cdot v = \lambda \cdot v" />

For real eigenvalues, multiplying <InlineMath math="v" /> by <InlineMath math="A" /> changes its scale or orientation along a real line. Complex eigenvalues describe multiplication by a complex scalar on a one-dimensional complex subspace.

> By definition, eigenvalues can equal <InlineMath math="0" />, but <Highlight>eigenvectors are always non-zero</Highlight>.

## Basic Properties of Eigenvectors

**Scalar Multiplication**: Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> and <InlineMath math="v \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> with <InlineMath math="v \neq 0" /> be an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="A" /> for eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" />. Then all multiples <InlineMath math="t \cdot v" /> with <InlineMath math="t \neq 0" /> are also eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" /> for the same eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

This follows directly from linearity:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} A \cdot (t \cdot v) &amp;= t \cdot (A \cdot v) \\ &amp;= t \cdot (\lambda \cdot v) \\ &amp;= \lambda \cdot (t \cdot v) \end{aligned}" />

Because <InlineMath math="t\neq0" />, the vector <InlineMath math="tv" /> is nonzero and satisfies the eigenvector definition for the same eigenvalue.

## Examples of Eigenvector Calculations

The examples here **verify the equation** <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" /> directly, once with a diagonal matrix and once with a symmetric one. Working through both shows where the eigenvalue comes from and how the eigenvector is found, which is the same procedure you need for a matrix that is neither.

### Diagonal Matrix

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" />

The vector <InlineMath math="v_1" /> below is an eigenvector for <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1" />. **Multiplication by** <InlineMath math="A" /> verifies this:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A \cdot v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 1 \cdot v_1" />
</MathContainer>

The vector <InlineMath math="v_2" /> below is an eigenvector for <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 2" />. **Multiplication by** <InlineMath math="A" /> verifies this:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A \cdot v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \cdot v_2" />
</MathContainer>

A vector outside these coordinate axes generally **changes direction**. For the diagonal matrix above:

<BlockMath math="A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\neq\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for every }\lambda" />

### Symmetric Matrix

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" />

The vector <InlineMath math="v_1" /> below is an eigenvector for <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 2" />. **Multiplication** by <InlineMath math="A" /> verifies this:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A \cdot v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \cdot v_1" />
</MathContainer>

The vector <InlineMath math="v_2" /> below is an eigenvector for <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 4" />. Multiplication by <InlineMath math="A" /> verifies this:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A \cdot v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = 4 \cdot v_2" />
</MathContainer>

## Linear Independence of Eigenvectors

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k \in \mathbb{K}" /> be **pairwise distinct eigenvalues** of <InlineMath math="A" />, so <InlineMath math="\lambda_i \neq \lambda_j" /> whenever <InlineMath math="i \neq j" />. Then corresponding eigenvectors <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_k \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> are linearly independent.

By mathematical induction, this theorem implies that an <InlineMath math="n \times n" /> matrix has at most <InlineMath math="n" /> distinct eigenvalues.

## Eigenspaces and Geometric Multiplicity

For each eigenvalue, we define a vector space containing every solution of <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" />, including the zero vector.

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" />. We call the following set the **eigenspace** of matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> for eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

<BlockMath math="E_A(\lambda) = \{v \in \mathbb{K}^n : A \cdot v = \lambda \cdot v\}" />

The dimension of an eigenspace is called its **geometric multiplicity**. We write it as

<BlockMath math="\dim E_A(\lambda)" />

The value <InlineMath math="\dim E_A(\lambda)" /> therefore gives the geometric multiplicity of eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> for matrix <InlineMath math="A" />.

### Properties of Eigenspaces

Each eigenspace satisfies the following properties:

1. **Zero vector is not an eigenvector**: The eigenspace <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda)" /> contains the zero vector along with all eigenvectors for <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

2. **Set of eigenvectors**: <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda) \setminus \{0\}" /> is the set of all eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" /> corresponding to <InlineMath math="\lambda" />.

3. **Eigenvalue condition**: <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is an eigenvalue of <InlineMath math="A" /> if and only if <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda) \neq \{0\}" />.

4. **Dimension bound**: <InlineMath math="0 \leq \dim E_A(\lambda) \leq n" />. If <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is an eigenvalue, the lower bound improves to <InlineMath math="1" />.

5. **Relationship with kernel**: <InlineMath math="E_A(0) = \{v \in \mathbb{K}^n : A \cdot v = 0\} = \ker A" />.

6. **General eigenspace**: <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda) = \{v \in \mathbb{K}^n : (A - \lambda \cdot I) \cdot v = 0\} = \ker(A - \lambda \cdot I)" />.

7. **Intersection of eigenspaces**: If <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2" />, then <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda_1) \cap E_A(\lambda_2) = \{0\}" />.

With <InlineMath math="A=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,1)" />, every vector of the form <InlineMath math="(a,b,0)" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="Av=2v" />. So <InlineMath math="E_A(2)" /> is a whole plane with geometric multiplicity <InlineMath math="2" />, while <InlineMath math="E_A(1)" /> is the line spanned by <InlineMath math="e_3" />.

## Relationship with Invertibility

Matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> is invertible if and only if **zero is not one** of its eigenvalues.

Indeed, <InlineMath math="A" /> is invertible if and only if <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank} A = n" />, equivalently <InlineMath math="E_A(0) = \ker A = \{0\}" />. An eigenvalue of zero therefore means that the matrix sends some nonzero vector to zero, and that is exactly what rules out invertibility.

### Eigenvalues of Inverse Matrix

If matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is invertible and <InlineMath math="v \neq 0" /> is an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="A" /> for eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" />, then <InlineMath math="v" /> is also an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> for eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\frac{1}{\lambda}" />.

**Applying** <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> to <InlineMath math="A \cdot v = \lambda \cdot v" /> gives:

<BlockMath math="v = \lambda A^{-1}v \quad \Longrightarrow \quad A^{-1}v = \frac{1}{\lambda}v" />

For <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}" />, the vector <InlineMath math="v=(2,-1)" /> gives <InlineMath math="Av=(2-2,4-4)=(0,0)=0\cdot v" />. The eigenvalue <InlineMath math="0" /> means that <InlineMath math="A" /> squeezes a whole direction to the origin, so no inverse can bring it back.

## Invertibility Criteria

For a square matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />, the following statements are equivalent:

1. <InlineMath math="A" /> is invertible
2. There exists a matrix <InlineMath math="A^{-1} \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> with <InlineMath math="A \cdot A^{-1} = I = A^{-1} \cdot A" />
3. <InlineMath math="A" /> has full rank, <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank} A = n" />, equivalently <InlineMath math="\ker A = \{0\}" />
4. The columns of <InlineMath math="A" /> are linearly independent
5. The rows of <InlineMath math="A" /> are linearly independent
6. <InlineMath math="\det A \neq 0" />
7. <InlineMath math="0" /> is not an eigenvalue of <InlineMath math="A" />

**Any one of these conditions** is enough to prove that the matrix is invertible.
