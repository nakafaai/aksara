## Deriving the Polynomial from the Matrix Equation

An eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> of a square matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> has a nonzero eigenvector <InlineMath math="v" /> satisfying

<BlockMath math="Av=\lambda v" />

Rearranging gives <InlineMath math="(A-\lambda I)v=0" />. A nonzero solution exists exactly when <InlineMath math="A-\lambda I" /> is singular, so <Highlight>its determinant must vanish</Highlight>. The characteristic polynomial records this condition for every possible scalar at once.

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />. We can form a special function:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \det(A - t \cdot I)" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \chi_A(t) &amp;= a_n \cdot t^n + a_{n-1} \cdot t^{n-1} \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \cdots + a_1 t + a_0 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

This degree-<InlineMath math="n" /> polynomial in <InlineMath math="t" />, with coefficients <InlineMath math="a_0,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb{K}" />, is the **characteristic polynomial** of <InlineMath math="A" />. Here, <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> uses the convention <InlineMath math="\det(A-tI)" />. The equally common convention <InlineMath math="\det(tI-A)" /> differs by the factor <InlineMath math="(-1)^n" /> but has the same roots.

## Matrix Trace and Polynomial Coefficients

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> be a square matrix. The **trace** of <InlineMath math="A" /> is the sum of the diagonal elements:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}" />

The matrix trace has a close relationship with the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial.

### Relationship of Coefficients with Trace and Determinant

In the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> of <InlineMath math="A" />, its coefficients have special meaning:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;a_n = (-1)^n, \\ &amp;a_{n-1} = (-1)^{n-1}\operatorname{tr}(A), \\ &amp;a_0 = \det A \end{aligned}" />

This means:
- The highest coefficient is always <InlineMath math="(-1)^n" />
- The second highest coefficient is related to the matrix trace
- The **constant term** is the determinant of the matrix

For a <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> matrix, <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)=t^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\,t+\det A" />. With <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" />, the trace is <InlineMath math="4" /> and the determinant is <InlineMath math="3" />, so <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)=t^2-4t+3=(t-1)(t-3)" />.

## Eigenvalues as Polynomial Roots

The value <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" /> is an eigenvalue of <InlineMath math="A" /> if and only if <InlineMath math="\det(A - \lambda \cdot I) = 0" />.

The eigenvalues are the **roots of the characteristic polynomial**.

For <InlineMath math="t \in \mathbb{K}" />, consider the following equation.

<BlockMath math="\det(A - t \cdot I) = 0" />

This equation is the **characteristic equation** of <InlineMath math="A" />.

## Algebraic Multiplicity

Now, what happens if an eigenvalue appears multiple times as a root of the characteristic polynomial? Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" />. The multiplicity of the root <InlineMath math="t = \lambda" /> of the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> is called the **algebraic multiplicity** <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda)" /> of the eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> of <InlineMath math="A" />. We say <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is an eigenvalue with multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda)" /> of <InlineMath math="A" />.

### Multiplicity Bounds

For every eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" />, its **algebraic multiplicity satisfies**

<BlockMath math="1 \leq \mu_A(\lambda) \leq n" />

Three <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> matrices with trace <InlineMath math="4" /> but determinants <InlineMath math="3" />, <InlineMath math="4" />, and <InlineMath math="5" /> give three kinds of roots. <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" /> has two simple roots, <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}" /> has the root <InlineMath math="2" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="2" />, and <InlineMath math="\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" /> has the complex roots <InlineMath math="2\pm i" />.

## Relationship between Geometric and Algebraic Multiplicity

Geometric and algebraic multiplicity satisfy the following bound.

For an eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> of <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />, its geometric and algebraic multiplicities satisfy

<BlockMath math="1 \leq \dim \operatorname{Eig}_A(\lambda) \leq \mu_A(\lambda) \leq n" />

> The geometric multiplicity of every eigenvalue is always less than or equal to its algebraic multiplicity.

The geometric multiplicity **counts independent eigenvectors** in the eigenspace, whereas the algebraic multiplicity counts how many times the root occurs in the characteristic polynomial. Jordan form shows that every independent eigenvector requires at least one Jordan block, so the geometric count cannot exceed the algebraic count.

## Computing Characteristic Polynomials

The definition is applied to concrete matrices now, and the **coefficient identities** serve as checks on the expansion. Follow each determinant step by step, then compare the roots with the trace and the determinant to confirm that the polynomial is correct.

### Third Order Matrix

This third order matrix produces a characteristic polynomial of **degree three**:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & -4 \\ 3 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{K}^{3 \times 3}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 3-t & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & -t & -4 \\ 3 & 0 & 1-t \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="= (3-t)(-t)(1-t) - 2(13-t) - 3t" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= -3t + 3t^2 + t^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}- t^3 - 2 + 2t - 24 - 3t \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="= -t^3 + 4t^2 - 4t - 26" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}" />, <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> only has the root <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 \approx -1.8003" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda_1) = 1" />.

For <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}" />, <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> has roots <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 \approx -1.8003" />, <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 \approx 2.9001 + 2.4559i" />, and <InlineMath math="\lambda_3 \approx 2.9001 - 2.4559i" /> with algebraic multiplicities <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda_1) = \mu_A(\lambda_2) = \mu_A(\lambda_3) = 1" /> respectively.

The roots pass two useful checks: their sum is the trace, and their product is the determinant.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=4=\operatorname{tr}(A)" />
<BlockMath math="\lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3=-26=\det(A)" />
</MathContainer>

### Repeated Eigenvalue in an Upper Triangular Matrix

This matrix has the **same diagonal entry twice**, namely <InlineMath math="1" />:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \chi_A(t) &amp;= \det(A - t \cdot I) \\ &amp;= \det \begin{pmatrix} 1-t &amp; 2 \\ 0 &amp; 1-t \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="= (1-t) \cdot (1-t) - 0 \cdot 2 = (1-t)^2" />
<BlockMath math="= t^2 - 2 \cdot t + 1" />
</MathContainer>

This matrix has the root <InlineMath math="\lambda = 1" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = 2" />. <InlineMath math="\lambda = 1" /> is the only eigenvalue of <InlineMath math="A" />.

<BlockMath math="(A-I)\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2y\\0\end{pmatrix}=0\iff y=0,\qquad E_A(1)=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right\}" />

The eigenspace has **dimension 1**, even though the polynomial root occurs twice.

## Characteristic Polynomials of Geometric Transformations

The calculation now runs on familiar maps of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}^2" />, so the algebraic result and its **geometric meaning** appear together. Rotation, reflection, scaling, and shearing each produce a polynomial whose roots describe what that map does to directions.

### Rotation

The rotation is represented by the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\alpha) & -\sin(\alpha) \\ \sin(\alpha) & \cos(\alpha) \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \chi_A(t) &amp;= (\cos(\alpha) - t)^2 + \sin(\alpha)^2 \\ &amp;= t^2 - 2 \cdot \cos(\alpha) \cdot t + 1 \end{aligned}" />

**Its discriminant** is <InlineMath math="-4\sin^2(\alpha)" />. The roots are real exactly when <InlineMath math="\sin(\alpha)=0" />, so, modulo a full turn, <InlineMath math="\alpha\equiv0" /> or <InlineMath math="\alpha\equiv\pi\pmod{2\pi}" />.

### Reflection

**Reflection across** the <InlineMath math="x" />-axis has the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (1-t) \cdot (-1-t) = t^2 - 1" />

The eigenvalues are <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = -1" /> with <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = \mu_A(-1) = 1" />.

### Scaling

**Scaling has** the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (s-t)^2 = t^2 - 2 \cdot s \cdot t + s^2" />

The eigenvalue is <InlineMath math="\lambda = s" /> with <InlineMath math="\mu_A(s) = 2" />.

### Shearing

**For a shear** with <InlineMath math="s \neq 0" />, the matrix is:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & s \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (1-t)^2 = t^2 - 2 \cdot t + 1" />

The eigenvalue is <InlineMath math="\lambda = 1" /> with <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = 2" />.

Compare the five parabolas below, one for each rotation angle. The lowest point of each sits at <InlineMath math="t=\cos\alpha" /> with value <InlineMath math="1-\cos^2\alpha=\sin^2\alpha" />. Only for <InlineMath math="\alpha=0" /> and <InlineMath math="\alpha=\pi" /> is that value zero, so the parabola touches the <InlineMath math="t" />-axis at <InlineMath math="t=1" /> or <InlineMath math="t=-1" />. For every other angle, the parabola floats above the axis, its roots are complex, and the rotation keeps no direction at all.

## Properties of Similar Matrices

Similar matrices describe the **same linear transformation** in different bases. They therefore have the same characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues, trace, and determinant.

Let <InlineMath math="S \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> be invertible and <InlineMath math="B = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />. Then:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\chi_B(t) \\ &amp;= \det(B - t \cdot I) \\ &amp;= \det(S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S - t \cdot S^{-1} \cdot I \cdot S) \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="= \det(S^{-1} \cdot (A - t \cdot I) \cdot S)" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= \det S^{-1} \cdot \det(A - t \cdot I) \cdot \det S \\ &amp;= \chi_A(t) \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

### Eigenvector Properties of Similar Matrices

The eigenvectors change with the basis. If <InlineMath math="v \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> is an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="A" /> for <InlineMath math="\lambda" />, then <InlineMath math="w = S^{-1}v" /> is an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="B" /> for the same eigenvalue.

Indeed, using <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" /> and <InlineMath math="w=S^{-1}v" /> gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} B \cdot w &amp;= S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S \cdot S^{-1} \cdot v \\ &amp;= S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot v \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="= S^{-1} \cdot \lambda \cdot v = \lambda \cdot S^{-1} \cdot v = \lambda \cdot w" />
</MathContainer>

Similarity preserves eigenvalues while the **inverse basis change** converts the corresponding eigenvectors.
