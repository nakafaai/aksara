## From an inconsistent system to a best fit

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}" />, <InlineMath math="x \in \mathbb{R}^n" />, and <InlineMath math="b \in \mathbb{R}^m" />. Consider the following system.

<BlockMath math="Ax=b" />

If <InlineMath math="b" /> does not belong to the column space of <InlineMath math="A" />, this system has no exact solution. This commonly happens when a model is fitted to <Highlight>noisy measurements</Highlight> or when an overdetermined system contains more equations than unknowns and those equations are inconsistent.

If no vector <InlineMath math="x" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="Ax=b" /> exactly, define the residual

<BlockMath math="e(x)=Ax-b" />

We seek the vector <InlineMath math="x" /> that produces the smallest residual. The standard linear least-squares problem is written as follows.

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}\|Ax-b\|_2^2" />

Because the residual has <InlineMath math="m" /> components, the objective expands to

<BlockMath math="\|Ax-b\|_2^2=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(Ax-b)_i^2" />

Squaring makes every contribution nonnegative and penalizes a large residual component more strongly than a small one.

## The geometric condition

The vector <InlineMath math="A\hat{x}" /> is the point in <InlineMath math="\operatorname{col}(A)" /> closest to <InlineMath math="b" /> in Euclidean distance. At the optimum, <Highlight>the residual is orthogonal to every column</Highlight> of <InlineMath math="A" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A^{\mathsf T}(A\hat{x}-b)=0" />
<BlockMath math="A^{\mathsf T}A\hat{x}=A^{\mathsf T}b" />
</MathContainer>

The second equation is called the **[normal equation](/en/subjects/ai-ds/linear-methods/normal-equation)**.

## Fitting One Constant to Three Observations

Suppose one constant <InlineMath math="x" /> must fit the three observations <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="2" />, and <InlineMath math="6" />. Use the following matrix and vector.

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\6\end{pmatrix}" />

The objective function for this example is

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} F(x) &amp;=(x-1)^2+(x-2)^2 \\ &amp;\quad{}+(x-6)^2 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} F'(x) &amp;=2(x-1)+2(x-2) \\ &amp;\quad+2(x-6) \\ &amp;=6x-18 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Setting the **derivative to zero** gives <InlineMath math="\hat{x}=3" />. The fitted vector and residual are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A\hat{x}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\3\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A\hat{x}-b=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-3\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The residual components sum to zero, so

<BlockMath math="A^{\mathsf T}(A\hat{x}-b)=2+1-3=0" />

The zero result satisfies the normal equation.

Fitting one constant <InlineMath math="x" /> to the observations <InlineMath math="1" />, <InlineMath math="2" />, and <InlineMath math="6" /> means minimizing <InlineMath math="(x-1)^2+(x-2)^2+(x-6)^2" />. The derivative vanishes when <InlineMath math="3x=9" />, so the best constant is the mean <InlineMath math="x=3" />, with squared residual <InlineMath math="4+1+9=14" />.

## Other residual norms

The norm determines what **kind of error** the fit prioritizes.

| Objective | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| <InlineMath math="\displaystyle\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2^2=\min_x\sum_{i=1}^{m}(Ax-b)_i^2" /> | Penalizes large residuals quadratically and gives the usual least-squares projection. |
| <InlineMath math="\displaystyle\min_x\|Ax-b\|_1=\min_x\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert(Ax-b)_i\rvert" /> | Uses a linear penalty, so an occasional large residual has less influence than under squared loss. |
| <InlineMath math="\displaystyle\min_x\|Ax-b\|_\infty=\min_x\max_{1\le i\le m}\lvert(Ax-b)_i\rvert" /> | Minimizes the worst absolute residual. |

The <InlineMath math="\ell_1" /> and <InlineMath math="\ell_\infty" /> formulations can be rewritten as linear programs. Their suitability still depends on the data-generating process and the error criterion that matters for the application.

For the same observations 1, 2, and 6, the norm changes the fitted constant. Least squares chooses the mean, absolute loss chooses the median, and the smallest maximum error balances distances to the two extremes.

| Criterion | Optimal constant | Minimum objective |
|---|---|---|
| <InlineMath math="\ell_2^2" /> | <InlineMath math="(1+2+6)/3=3" /> | <InlineMath math="2^2+1^2+(-3)^2=14" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\ell_1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="1+0+4=5" /> |
| <InlineMath math="\ell_\infty" /> | <InlineMath math="(1+6)/2=7/2" /> | <InlineMath math="\max(5/2,3/2,5/2)=5/2" /> |

Other norms pick other constants for the same data. The sum of absolute residuals is smallest at the median <InlineMath math="2" />, the Euclidean norm at the mean <InlineMath math="3" />, and the largest residual at the midrange <InlineMath math="\tfrac{1+6}2=3.5" />.

## Choosing a Stable Numerical Method

If <InlineMath math="A" /> has **full column rank**, the least-squares minimizer is unique. The normal equation then implies

<BlockMath math="\hat{x}=(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}b" />

This identity characterizes the unique minimizer when <InlineMath math="A" /> has full column rank. Do not compute it by explicitly forming the inverse. Choose the numerical method from the rank and conditioning:

- use a <InlineMath math="\mathrm{QR}" /> factorization for a stable full-rank least-squares solve
- use an <InlineMath math="\mathrm{SVD}" /> when rank deficiency or ill-conditioning matters
- use the pseudoinverse to select the minimum-norm solution when minimizers are not unique

There is also a precise statistical interpretation. Consider the following model.

<BlockMath math="b=Ax_*+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2I),\quad \sigma^2>0" />

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is fixed and the Gaussian errors are independent with common variance, least squares gives the maximum-likelihood estimator of <InlineMath math="x_*" />. Correlated errors or errors with unequal variance require weights that reflect the error covariance.
