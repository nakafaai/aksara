## Changing to an Eigenvector Basis

Diagonalization does not change the <Highlight>linear transformation itself</Highlight>. It changes the coordinate basis so that the transformation acts independently along eigenvector directions. In the diagonal representation, matrix multiplication reduces to scaling each eigenvector coordinate.

To diagonalize a matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />, compute its eigenvalues and eigenspaces. If there are <InlineMath math="n" /> linearly independent eigenvectors, place those vectors in a change-of-basis matrix.

In an eigenvector basis, a diagonalizable matrix only scales coordinates. For the matrix in the example below, write <InlineMath math="w=(2,3,3)" /> as <InlineMath math="v_1+v_3" /> with <InlineMath math="v_1=(1,0,1)" /> in the plane <InlineMath math="E_A(1)" /> and <InlineMath math="v_3=(1,3,2)" /> in <InlineMath math="E_A(-1)" />. Then <InlineMath math="Aw=v_1-v_3=(0,-3,-1)" />: the plane part stays, and the <InlineMath math="v_3" /> part flips.

## Diagonalization Steps

The diagonalization procedure consists of three main steps that must be performed sequentially:

1. **Compute the characteristic polynomial** to find all eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k \in \mathbb{K}" /> along with their algebraic multiplicities <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda_1), \ldots, \mu_A(\lambda_k)" />.

   Over the chosen field <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" />, the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> must split completely into linear factors:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \prod_{i=1}^k (\lambda_i - t)^{\mu_A(\lambda_i)}" />
   <BlockMath math="\sum_{i=1}^k \mu_A(\lambda_i) = n" />
   </MathContainer>

   If it does not split, then the matrix is not diagonalizable over that field.

2. **Compute the eigenspaces** for each eigenvalue by solving the homogeneous linear system:

   <BlockMath math="(A - \lambda_i \cdot I) \cdot v = 0" />

   Here we find every vector that matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> maps to a scalar multiple of itself. The factor is the eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda_i" />. A negative factor reverses the vector's direction but keeps it on the same line.

3. **Check the diagonalization conditions** by verifying whether the algebraic multiplicity equals the geometric multiplicity for all eigenvalues. Mathematically, for all <InlineMath math="i = 1, \ldots, k" /> we must have <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda_i) = \dim E_A(\lambda_i)" />.

   This condition ensures that we have <Highlight>enough independent eigenvectors to form a complete basis</Highlight>. If satisfied, the basis vectors from all eigenspaces form the columns of the transformation matrix <InlineMath math="S" />, yielding:

   <BlockMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />

Step one factors the characteristic polynomial. For the example below, <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)=(1-t)^2(-1-t)" /> crosses the axis at <InlineMath math="t=-1" /> and only touches it at <InlineMath math="t=1" />, so <InlineMath math="\mu_A(-1)=1" /> and <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1)=2" />. The procedure then needs two independent eigenvectors for <InlineMath math="1" /> and one for <InlineMath math="-1" />.

## Example Application of the Procedure

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 1 \\ -3 & -2 & 3 \\ -2 & -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" />

Apply the diagonalization procedure to this example:

1. We compute the characteristic polynomial and factor it:

   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \chi_A(t) &amp;= \det(A - t \cdot I) \\ &amp;= -t^3 + t^2 + t - 1 \end{aligned}" />

   After factoring, we obtain:

   <BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (1 - t)^2 \cdot (-1 - t)" />

   The factors give the eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = -1" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(-1) = 1" />. Their multiplicities sum to <InlineMath math="2 + 1 = 3" />, which equals the matrix dimension.

2. We determine the eigenspace for each eigenvalue:

   <MathContainer>
   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E_A(1) &amp;= \ker(A - I) \\ &amp;= \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{aligned}" />
   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} E_A(-1) &amp;= \ker(A + I) \\ &amp;= \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{aligned}" />
   </MathContainer>

   For eigenvalue <InlineMath math="1" />, every row of <InlineMath math="A-I" /> gives the same **plane equation** <InlineMath math="z=x+y" />. For eigenvalue <InlineMath math="-1" />, solve the first two equations in <InlineMath math="(A+I)v=0" />:

   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}x-y+z&=0\Rightarrow y=x+z\\-3x-y+3z&=0\Rightarrow -4x+2z=0\\z&=2x,\qquad y=3x\end{aligned}" />

   Here, the kernel or null space contains every vector that the matrix maps to the zero vector, while the span of a collection of vectors contains all of their linear combinations.

3. We check whether the diagonalization conditions are satisfied. For eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1" />, its algebraic multiplicity is <InlineMath math="2" /> and its eigenspace has dimension <InlineMath math="2" /> (two independent basis vectors). For eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = -1" />, its algebraic multiplicity is <InlineMath math="1" /> and its eigenspace has dimension <InlineMath math="1" />.

   Since <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = 2 = \dim E_A(1)" /> and <InlineMath math="\mu_A(-1) = 1 = \dim E_A(-1)" />, the diagonalization conditions are satisfied.

Now we can form the transformation matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> by arranging all eigenvectors as columns, and the diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> with eigenvalues on the main diagonal:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & & \\ & 1 & \\ & & -1 \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The columns of <InlineMath math="S" /> and the corresponding diagonal entries of <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> must remain in the **same order**. Here the construction is valid because:

<BlockMath math="\det S = -2 \neq 0, \qquad S^{-1}AS = \Lambda" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is successfully diagonalized to <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> through the transformation <InlineMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />.

The procedure therefore proves both requirements: the eigenvectors form a basis, and the matrix representation in that basis is diagonal.

Rotate the model to separate the **two eigenspaces**. Every point of the teal plane <InlineMath math="z=x+y" /> stays fixed because its eigenvalue is <InlineMath math="1" />. The orange vector <InlineMath math="v_3" /> lies outside that plane and maps to the magenta vector <InlineMath math="Av_3=-v_3" />. Together, these directions span the whole space.
