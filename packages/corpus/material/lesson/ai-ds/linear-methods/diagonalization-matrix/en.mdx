## A Basis That Separates the Coordinate Directions

Diagonalization rewrites a linear transformation in a <Highlight>basis of eigenvectors</Highlight>. In that basis, each coordinate is multiplied by its own eigenvalue, so the coordinates stop mixing with one another.

The main goal is to find a basis in which the linear transformation <InlineMath math="y = A \cdot x" /> is represented by the diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />. If the eigenbasis can be chosen orthonormally, then <InlineMath math="S^{-1} = S^T" /> in the real case and <InlineMath math="S^{-1} = S^H" /> in the complex case.

## Definition of Diagonalization

A matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> is called **diagonalizable** if it is similar to some diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />, that is, if there exists an invertible matrix <InlineMath math="S \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> such that:

<BlockMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />

Equivalently, <InlineMath math="A = S\Lambda S^{-1}" />. Repeated application then has the form:

For every nonnegative integer <InlineMath math="m" />:

<BlockMath math="A^m = S\Lambda^m S^{-1}" />

Because <InlineMath math="\Lambda^m" /> is obtained by raising each diagonal entry to the power <InlineMath math="m" />, diagonalization turns a matrix-power problem into scalar arithmetic.

For <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" />, the eigenvectors <InlineMath math="v_1=(1,1)" /> and <InlineMath math="v_2=(-1,1)" /> become the columns of <InlineMath math="S=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}" />, and the eigenvalues fill <InlineMath math="D=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)" />. In the basis <InlineMath math="v_1,v_2" />, the matrix only stretches the direction <InlineMath math="v_1" /> by three, which is exactly <InlineMath math="A=SDS^{-1}" />.

## Basic Conditions for Diagonalization

When can a matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> be diagonalized? The answer is when we can find a basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" /> that consists entirely of eigenvectors <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> of <InlineMath math="A" /> with corresponding eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K}" />.

The diagonal matrix <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> is:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \Lambda &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 &amp; &amp; \\ &amp; \ddots &amp; \\ &amp; &amp; \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;= \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n) \end{aligned}" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> has the eigenvectors as its columns:

<BlockMath math="S = (v_1 \quad \ldots \quad v_n)" />

If <InlineMath math="A" /> is diagonalizable, then the columns <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" /> of <InlineMath math="S" /> form a **basis of eigenvectors**. From <InlineMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" /> we obtain <InlineMath math="A \cdot S = S \cdot \Lambda" /> and thus <InlineMath math="A \cdot v_i = \lambda_i \cdot v_i" /> for <InlineMath math="i = 1, \ldots, n" />.

Conversely, if <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" /> is a basis of eigenvectors, then <InlineMath math="S" /> is invertible and from <InlineMath math="A \cdot v_i = \lambda_i \cdot v_i" /> for <InlineMath math="i = 1, \ldots, n" /> we obtain <InlineMath math="A \cdot S = S \cdot \Lambda" /> and thus <InlineMath math="\Lambda = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />.

## A Matrix That Cannot Be Diagonalized

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

This matrix has eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda = 1" /> with algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(1) = 2" />. Its eigenspace is the kernel of <InlineMath math="A - I" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="E_A(1) = \ker(A - I)" />
<BlockMath math="= \ker\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="= \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}" />
</MathContainer>

The span shown above contains all scalar multiples of the displayed vector and has dimension <InlineMath math="1" />. Thus the eigenspace supplies **only one independent eigenvector**, but a basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^2" /> needs two. Therefore <InlineMath math="A" /> is not diagonalizable.

| Matrix | Algebraic multiplicity of 1 | Dimension of the eigenspace | Diagonalizable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity <InlineMath math="I_2" /> | 2 | 2 | Yes |
| Matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> above | 2 | 1 | No |

The only eigendirection is <InlineMath math="E_A(1)=\operatorname{span}\{e_1\}" />, and that is not enough for a basis of the plane.

## Requirements for Matrix Diagonalization

If a matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> is diagonalizable, then the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> of <InlineMath math="A" /> over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" /> factors into linear factors:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (\lambda_1 - t) \cdots (\lambda_n - t)" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> has <InlineMath math="n" /> eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_i \in \mathbb{K}" />, counted with multiplicity, and they need not be distinct.

**The chosen field matters**. A real matrix can fail to be diagonalizable over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{R}" /> yet become diagonalizable over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" /> after its characteristic polynomial splits there.

Distinct eigenvalues remove the need to compare algebraic and geometric multiplicities. Suppose the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> of <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> factors over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" /> as follows.

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (\lambda_1 - t) \cdots (\lambda_n - t)" />

If the eigenvalues are pairwise distinct, so <InlineMath math="\lambda_i \neq \lambda_j" /> for <InlineMath math="i \neq j" /> with <InlineMath math="i, j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}" />, then <InlineMath math="A" /> is diagonalizable.

Eigenvectors for pairwise distinct eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="A" /> are linearly independent. With <InlineMath math="n" /> such eigenvalues, they form a basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" />.

Repeated eigenvalues require an additional check. Each eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity <InlineMath math="\mu_A(\lambda_i)" /> and geometric multiplicity <InlineMath math="\dim E_A(\lambda_i)" />, with the relationship:

<BlockMath math="\dim E_A(\lambda_i) \leq \mu_A(\lambda_i)" />

## Diagonalization Characterization Theorem

For a matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />, the following statements are equivalent:

1. <InlineMath math="A" /> is diagonalizable.

2. Both of the following conditions are satisfied. First, the characteristic polynomial of <InlineMath math="A" /> must factor into linear factors:

   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\chi_A(t) \\ &amp;=(\lambda_1-t)^{\mu_A(\lambda_1)}\cdots(\lambda_k-t)^{\mu_A(\lambda_k)} \end{aligned}" />

   with pairwise distinct eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k \in \mathbb{K}" /> of <InlineMath math="A" />. Second, for all eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="A" />, <Highlight>the algebraic multiplicity must equal the geometric multiplicity</Highlight>:

   <BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;\mu_A(\lambda_i) = \dim E_A(\lambda_i) \\ &amp;(i = 1, \ldots, k) \end{aligned}" />

3. The direct sum of all eigenspaces is the entire vector space:

   <BlockMath math="E_A(\lambda_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus E_A(\lambda_k) = \mathbb{K}^n" />

   This means there exists a basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" /> consisting of eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" />.

For each <InlineMath math="i = 1, \ldots, k" />, let <InlineMath math="v_1^{(i)}, \ldots, v_{d_i}^{(i)}" /> be a basis of the eigenspace <InlineMath math="E_A(\lambda_i)" />. Then:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;v_1^{(1)},\ldots,v_{d_1}^{(1)}, \\ &amp;v_1^{(2)},\ldots,v_{d_2}^{(2)},\ldots, \\ &amp;v_1^{(k)},\ldots,v_{d_k}^{(k)} \end{aligned}" />

These vectors form a basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" /> consisting of eigenvectors of <InlineMath math="A" />. Therefore, <InlineMath math="A" /> is diagonalizable.

Take <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&-1\\0&2&-1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}" /> with <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)=(2-t)^2(1-t)" />. The eigenspace <InlineMath math="E_A(2)" /> is the plane <InlineMath math="z=0" /> of dimension <InlineMath math="2" />, equal to its algebraic multiplicity, and <InlineMath math="E_A(1)" /> is the line through <InlineMath math="(1,1,1)" />. The dimensions <InlineMath math="2+1=3" /> fill the whole space, so <InlineMath math="A" /> is diagonalizable.
