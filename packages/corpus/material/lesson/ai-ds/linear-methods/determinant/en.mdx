## How a Matrix Changes Volume and Orientation

The determinant of a square matrix measures the <Highlight>oriented volume scaling</Highlight> of its linear transformation. Its absolute value gives the volume factor, while its sign records whether orientation is preserved or reversed.

Every square matrix has one determinant. A zero determinant means that the image lies in a lower-dimensional set, so the transformation is not invertible.

Determinant is a special function that takes a square matrix and produces one real number:

<BlockMath math="\det : \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \to \mathbb{R} : A \mapsto \det A" />

The sign and value of the determinant give three cases:

| Determinant | Geometric effect | Invertibility |
|---|---|---|
| <InlineMath math="\det A>0" /> | Scales volume by <InlineMath math="\lvert\det A\rvert" /> and preserves orientation | Invertible |
| <InlineMath math="\det A<0" /> | Scales volume by <InlineMath math="\lvert\det A\rvert" /> and reverses orientation | Invertible |
| <InlineMath math="\det A=0" /> | Maps the volume to zero and the image to a lower-dimensional set | Not invertible |


The teal unit cube becomes the orange parallelepiped under the following matrix. The sloping edges show shear, but volume uses **base length and perpendicular heights**.

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det A=2\cdot1\cdot1=2" />

## Three Determining Properties of Determinant

Only one determinant function satisfies the following **three properties**. Each property fixes one behavior of that function: how it reacts to a row operation, what happens when two rows coincide, and which value the identity matrix must return. Together they determine the determinant completely.

### Linear Property on Each Row

The determinant is **linear in each row while the other rows are fixed**. This property is called multilinearity.

When we add two vectors in one row:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_i + a_i' \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix} &amp;= \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_i \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;\quad{}+ \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_i' \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />

When we multiply one row by a scalar:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda \cdot a_i \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="= \lambda \cdot \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_i \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

### Alternating Property

The determinant is **alternating**: if two rows are identical, its value is zero.

<BlockMath math="\det \begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ a \\ \vdots \\ a \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} = 0" />

Equivalently, swapping two rows reverses the sign of the determinant. Identical rows would be unchanged by the swap but would also have to change sign, so the only possible value is zero.

### Normalization Property

Determinant is **normalized** so that the determinant of the identity matrix is always <InlineMath math="1" />:

<BlockMath math="\det I = 1" />

Here <InlineMath math="I" /> is the identity matrix of the relevant size. Multilinearity, alternation, and this normalization <Highlight>determine the determinant uniquely</Highlight>.

## Matrix Operations and Determinant

If we multiply the **entire matrix** by a scalar <InlineMath math="\lambda" />, the determinant gains one factor from each row:

<BlockMath math="\det(\lambda A) = \lambda^n \cdot \det A" />

This is because each row is multiplied by <InlineMath math="\lambda" />, and there are <InlineMath math="n" /> total rows.

When we swap two rows of a matrix, the determinant changes sign:

<BlockMath math="\det B = -\det A" />

Adding a multiple of one row to a different row does not change the determinant:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det \begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ a_j \\ \vdots \\ a_i + \lambda a_j \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="= \det \begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ a_j \\ \vdots \\ a_i \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

In two dimensions, <InlineMath math="\det(\lambda A)=\lambda^2\det A" />. Take <InlineMath math="A=I" />: scaling both rows by <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> turns the unit square into a square of side <InlineMath math="\lambda" />, whose area is <InlineMath math="\lambda^2" />, one factor of <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> for each row.

## Relationship with Matrix Invertibility

The determinant decides whether a square matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is invertible. The following conditions are equivalent:

1. **Matrix A is invertible**
2. **There exists an inverse matrix** <InlineMath math="A^{-1}" /> that satisfies <InlineMath math="AA^{-1} = I" />
3. **Full rank matrix**: <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A) = n" />
4. **Trivial kernel**: <InlineMath math="\ker(A) = \{0\}" />
5. **Columns are linearly independent**
6. **Rows are linearly independent**
7. **Determinant is not zero**: <InlineMath math="\det A \neq 0" />

> If the determinant is zero, the transformation maps space into a lower dimensional set, so it cannot be inverted.

If the rows are linearly dependent, one row is a linear combination of the others. Multilinearity expands the determinant into terms with repeated rows, and every such term is zero by the alternating property.

For <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\2&4\end{pmatrix}" />, the columns <InlineMath math="(1,2)" /> and <InlineMath math="(k,4)" /> span a parallelogram of area <InlineMath math="|4-2k|" />. At <InlineMath math="k=2" /> the columns point the same way, the area is <InlineMath math="0" />, and <InlineMath math="A" /> has no inverse.

## Multiplication Properties and Similar Matrices

The determinant of a matrix product equals the product of the two determinants:

<BlockMath math="\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B" />

If matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is invertible, the determinant of its inverse is:

<BlockMath math="\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A}" />

Two matrices <InlineMath math="A" /> and <InlineMath math="B" /> are called **similar** if there exists an invertible matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> such that:

<BlockMath math="B = S^{-1}AS" />

Similar matrices have the same determinant. Apply the product rule for determinants.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det B = \det(S^{-1}AS)" />
<BlockMath math="= \det(S^{-1}) \cdot \det A \cdot \det S" />
<BlockMath math="= \frac{1}{\det S} \cdot \det A \cdot \det S = \det A" />
</MathContainer>

If Gaussian elimination uses only row additions and <InlineMath math="p" /> row swaps, the determinant of the resulting upper triangular matrix <InlineMath math="R" /> is

<BlockMath math="\det R = (-1)^p \cdot \det A" />

If a row is also scaled, that scale factor must be tracked separately. With the allowed operations stated explicitly, triangularization provides a practical determinant algorithm.

## Common Mistakes About Determinant

The determinant is **not additive in general**. The following example disproves an addition rule.

As a simple example:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right)" />
<BlockMath math="= \det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 4" />
</MathContainer>

If the two determinants are calculated separately, the result is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="= 1 + 1 = 2" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="4 \neq 2" />, the determinant is not additive under matrix addition.
