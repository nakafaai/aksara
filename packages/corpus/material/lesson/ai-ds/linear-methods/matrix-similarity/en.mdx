## Different Matrices for the Same Linear Transformation

In linear algebra, matrix similarity explains how two different matrices can represent the same linear transformation in different bases. The entries change with the coordinates, but the underlying transformation does not.

Two matrices <InlineMath math="A, B \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> are said to be **similar** if there exists an invertible matrix <InlineMath math="S \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" /> such that:

<BlockMath math="B = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> is the change-of-basis matrix connecting the two representations.

## Basis Transformation and Coordinate Representation

Similar matrices represent the same linear transformation in two different bases. Let <InlineMath math="e_1, \ldots, e_n \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> be the canonical basis and <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> be another basis of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" />.

If <InlineMath math="S" /> is an invertible matrix with columns <InlineMath math="v_k" />:

<BlockMath math="S = (v_1 \quad \ldots \quad v_n) \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />

Then we have <InlineMath math="v_k = S \cdot e_k" /> or <InlineMath math="e_k = S^{-1} \cdot v_k" /> for <InlineMath math="k = 1, \ldots, n" />. The matrix <InlineMath math="S" /> represents the **basis transformation**.

A vector <InlineMath math="x \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> can be expressed in the canonical basis through coordinates <InlineMath math="x_k" /> and in the basis <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" /> through coordinates <InlineMath math="\xi_k" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="x = \sum_{k=1}^n x_k \cdot e_k" />
<BlockMath math="= \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k \cdot v_k = S \cdot \xi" />
<BlockMath math="\xi = \begin{pmatrix} \xi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \xi_n \end{pmatrix} = S^{-1} \cdot x" />
</MathContainer>

The matrix <InlineMath math="S^{-1}" /> represents the **coordinate transformation**.

## Linear Transformation in Different Bases

For the linear transformation <InlineMath math="y = A \cdot x" />, the canonical basis expresses <InlineMath math="y" /> with coordinates <InlineMath math="y_k" />. The basis <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" /> expresses the same vector with coordinates <InlineMath math="\eta_k" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="y = \sum_{k=1}^n y_k \cdot e_k" />
<BlockMath math="= \sum_{k=1}^n \eta_k \cdot v_k = S \cdot \eta" />
<BlockMath math="\eta = \begin{pmatrix} \eta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \eta_n \end{pmatrix} = S^{-1} \cdot y" />
</MathContainer>

The calculation gives:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="S \cdot \eta = y = A \cdot x" />
<BlockMath math="= A \cdot S \cdot \xi" />
</MathContainer>

The **same coordinate relation** can be written as follows.

<BlockMath math="\eta = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S \cdot \xi" />

In the basis <InlineMath math="v_1, \ldots, v_n" />, the linear transformation <InlineMath math="y = A \cdot x" /> is represented by <InlineMath math="\eta = B \cdot \xi" /> with the matrix:

<BlockMath math="B = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S" />

Similar matrices represent one linear transformation with respect to different bases of <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}^n" />.

With <InlineMath math="A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)" /> and the columns <InlineMath math="s_1=(1,0)" />, <InlineMath math="s_2=(1,1)" />, you get <InlineMath math="As_1=(2,0)=2s_1" /> and <InlineMath math="As_2=(2,3)=-s_1+3s_2" />. The coordinates of these two images in the basis <InlineMath math="s_1,s_2" /> are the columns of <InlineMath math="B=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}" />.

## Worked Basis Change Example

Let


<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} &amp;S=\begin{pmatrix}1&amp;1\\0&amp;1\end{pmatrix}, \\ &amp;S^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&amp;-1\\0&amp;1\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

The **same transformation in the basis** formed by the columns of <InlineMath math="S" /> has the matrix

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="AS=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad S^{-1}AS=\begin{pmatrix}2&2-3\\0&3\end{pmatrix}" />

<BlockMath math="B=S^{-1}AS=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Although the entries differ, both matrices have characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="(t-2)(t-3)" />, trace <InlineMath math="5" />, and determinant <InlineMath math="6" />.

| Coordinates | Input | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canonical basis | <InlineMath math="x=(2,1)^T" /> | <InlineMath math="Ax=(4,3)^T" /> |
| New basis | <InlineMath math="\xi=S^{-1}x=(1,1)^T" /> | <InlineMath math="B\xi=(1,3)^T" /> |

Converting back gives the **same output vector**:

<BlockMath math="S\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1+3\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\3\end{pmatrix}=Ax" />

The gray grid below is built from the columns <InlineMath math="s_1=(1,0)^T" /> and <InlineMath math="s_2=(1,1)^T" />, so each crossing has whole-number coordinates in the new basis. The orange segment <InlineMath math="x" /> ends at <InlineMath math="1\cdot s_1+1\cdot s_2" />, matching <InlineMath math="\xi=(1,1)^T" />. The magenta segment <InlineMath math="Ax=(4,3)^T" /> is reached by the amber path <InlineMath math="1\cdot s_1+3\cdot s_2" />, matching <InlineMath math="B\xi=(1,3)^T" />. The segments stay the same. Only the way you read their coordinates changes.

## Invariant Properties of Similar Matrices

Because similar matrices describe the same transformation in different coordinates, they <Highlight>preserve its basis-independent properties</Highlight>. They have the same characteristic polynomial, determinant, trace, rank, eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity, minimal polynomial, and Jordan block sizes.

They do **not** necessarily have the same entries, eigenvector coordinates, Euclidean norm, symmetry, or orthogonality. Those properties can depend on the chosen basis. A unitary or orthogonal change of basis preserves more geometric structure than a general similarity transformation.

| Preserved by similarity | Not generally preserved |
| --- | --- |
| Characteristic and minimal polynomials | Matrix entries |
| Eigenvalues and Jordan block sizes | Coordinate vectors of eigenvectors |
| Determinant, trace, and rank | Euclidean norm and symmetry |

### Proof of Determinant Equality

**For the determinant**, we can show:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det(B) = \det(S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S)" />
<BlockMath math="= \det(S^{-1}) \cdot \det(A) \cdot \det(S)" />
</MathContainer>

Since <InlineMath math="\det(S^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(S)}" />, then:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det(B) = \frac{1}{\det(S)} \cdot \det(A) \cdot \det(S)" />
<BlockMath math="= \det(A)" />
</MathContainer>

### Eigenvalue Correspondence

If <InlineMath math="v \in \mathbb{K}^n" /> is an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="A" /> with eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{K}" />, such that <InlineMath math="A \cdot v = \lambda \cdot v" />, then <InlineMath math="w = S^{-1} \cdot v" /> is an eigenvector of <InlineMath math="B" /> with the **same eigenvalue**:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="B \cdot w = S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot S \cdot w" />
<BlockMath math="= S^{-1} \cdot A \cdot v" />
<BlockMath math="= S^{-1} \cdot \lambda \cdot v" />
<BlockMath math="= \lambda \cdot w" />
</MathContainer>

Matrix similarity preserves the spectrum. When the basis changes, the eigenvector coordinates change from <InlineMath math="v" /> to <InlineMath math="S^{-1}v" />, but both coordinate vectors represent the same geometric direction.

<InlineMath math="A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)" /> and <InlineMath math="B=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}" /> share the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="t^2-5t+6=(t-2)(t-3)" />. This one curve carries all the invariants at once: its roots are the eigenvalues, the coefficient <InlineMath math="-5" /> is minus the trace, and the constant term <InlineMath math="6" /> is the determinant.
