## Real and Imaginary Parts in Matrix Entries

Complex matrices use the same row, column, addition, and multiplication rules as real matrices. The difference is that every entry may have both a real and an imaginary part.

A complex matrix with <InlineMath math="m \in \mathbb{N}" /> rows and <InlineMath math="n \in \mathbb{N}" /> columns can be written as

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} A &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} &amp; \cdots &amp; a_{1n} \\ \vdots &amp; &amp; \vdots \\ a_{m1} &amp; \cdots &amp; a_{mn} \end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;= (a_{ij})_{\substack{i=1,\ldots,m \\ j=1,\ldots,n}} \end{aligned}" />

Each **coefficient** <InlineMath math="a_{ij} \in \mathbb{C}" /> has indices <InlineMath math="i = 1, \ldots, m" /> and <InlineMath math="j = 1, \ldots, n" />. The set of all complex <InlineMath math="m \times n" /> matrices is written as <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}^{m \times n}" />.

For example, entries such as <InlineMath math="2+3i" /> and <InlineMath math="-1-4i" /> belong to <InlineMath math="\mathbb C" />. Because conjugation matters for lengths and angles, a plain transpose is no longer the right operation for every geometric calculation.

## Adjoint Matrix

For complex matrices, the geometric counterpart of the transpose is the **adjoint**, also called the conjugate transpose. It <Highlight>transposes the matrix and conjugates every entry</Highlight>.

Let <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}" /> be a complex <InlineMath math="m \times n" /> matrix. The **adjoint matrix** (complex conjugate transpose matrix) <InlineMath math="A^H" /> of <InlineMath math="A" /> is the complex <InlineMath math="n \times m" /> matrix obtained by swapping the rows and columns of <InlineMath math="A" /> and taking the complex conjugate of each entry:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} A^H &amp;= \overline{A^T} \\ &amp;= \overline{A}^T \\ &amp;= \begin{pmatrix} \overline{a_{11}} &amp; \cdots &amp; \overline{a_{m1}} \\ \vdots &amp; &amp; \vdots \\ \overline{a_{1n}} &amp; \cdots &amp; \overline{a_{mn}} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times m} \end{aligned}" />

For example,

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1+i&2\\-i&3-2i\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A^H=\begin{pmatrix}1-i&i\\2&3+2i\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

The entry in row <InlineMath math="j" /> and column <InlineMath math="i" /> of <InlineMath math="A^H" /> is therefore <InlineMath math="\overline{a_{ij}}" />.

Take <InlineMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}1+2i&3-i\\-2+i&2i\end{pmatrix}" />. Conjugation mirrors every entry across the real axis, then the transpose swaps the places of <InlineMath math="a_{12}" /> and <InlineMath math="a_{21}" />, so <InlineMath math="A^H=\begin{pmatrix}1-2i&-2-i\\3+i&-2i\end{pmatrix}" />.

## Properties of Adjoint Matrix

The adjoint **reverses the order of a product** and conjugates scalars. For sums and products below, the matrix dimensions must be compatible.

| Rule | Identity | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real entries | <InlineMath math="A^H=A^T" /> | Conjugation leaves real entries unchanged. |
| Taking the adjoint twice | <InlineMath math="(A^H)^H=A" /> | Both the transpose and conjugation are undone. |
| Addition | <InlineMath math="(A+B)^H=A^H+B^H" /> | Conjugation distributes over addition. |
| Scalar multiplication | <InlineMath math="(\lambda A)^H=\overline\lambda A^H" /> | The scalar is conjugated with every entry. |
| Product | <InlineMath math="(AB)^H=B^HA^H" /> | Transposing swaps the order of row-column matching. |

For the one-entry matrix <InlineMath math="A=(1+i)" />, compare the conjugate of <InlineMath math="iA" /> with multiplying the conjugate of <InlineMath math="A" /> by <InlineMath math="i" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="(iA)^H=\overline{-1+i}=-1-i" />
<BlockMath math="\overline i A^H=(-i)(1-i)=-1-i" />
<BlockMath math="iA^H=i(1-i)=1+i\ne(iA)^H" />
</MathContainer>

The <InlineMath math="1\times1" /> matrix <InlineMath math="A=(z)" /> has <InlineMath math="A^HA=(\bar zz)=(|z|^2)" />. For <InlineMath math="z=1+2i" />, the angle of <InlineMath math="\bar z" /> is minus the angle of <InlineMath math="z" />, so the product lands on the positive real axis: <InlineMath math="\bar zz=(1-2i)(1+2i)=1+4=5" />. That is why the diagonal of <InlineMath math="A^HA" /> is always real and nonnegative.

## Euclidean Norm on Complex Spaces

For <InlineMath math="v,w\in\mathbb C^n" />, the standard Hermitian inner product is

<BlockMath math="\langle v,w\rangle=v^Hw=\sum_{i=1}^n\overline{v_i}w_i" />

Conjugating the first argument ensures that the inner product of a vector with itself is real and nonnegative.

For <InlineMath math="v \in \mathbb{C}^n" />, we define:

<BlockMath math="v^H v = \sum_{i=1}^n \overline{v_i} v_i = \sum_{i=1}^n |v_i|^2 \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq0}" />

The **Euclidean norm** is calculated as:

<BlockMath math="\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{v^H v}" />

For <InlineMath math="v=(1+i,2-i)^T" />, the calculation is concrete:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} v^Hv &amp;=|1+i|^2+|2-i|^2 \\ &amp;=2+5 \\ &amp;=7 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\|v\|_2=\sqrt7" />
</MathContainer>

This is why using <InlineMath math="v^Tv" /> would be wrong in a complex space: without conjugation, the result need not be real or positive.

### Properties of Euclidean Norm

**Homogeneity**: <InlineMath math="\|\alpha v\|_2 = |\alpha| \|v\|_2" />

**Positive definite**: <InlineMath math="\|v\|_2 \geq 0" /> and <InlineMath math="\|v\|_2 = 0 \Leftrightarrow v = 0" />

**Triangle inequality**: <InlineMath math="\|v + w\|_2 \leq \|v\|_2 + \|w\|_2" />

These properties show that <InlineMath math="\|\cdot\|_2" /> satisfies the definition of a norm. Using the adjoint in the Hermitian inner product keeps complex lengths, orthogonality, and projections consistent with their real counterparts.

Draw each entry of <InlineMath math="v" /> as a point in the complex plane, as in this scene. The conjugate <InlineMath math="\overline{v_i}" /> is that point mirrored across the real axis, and <InlineMath math="\overline{v_i}v_i=|v_i|^2" /> is the squared radius of its circle. So <InlineMath math="v^Hv=2+5=7" /> adds two squared radii, not the complex entries. Without the conjugate, <InlineMath math="v^Tv=(1+i)^2+(2-i)^2=3-2i" /> is not even a real number.
