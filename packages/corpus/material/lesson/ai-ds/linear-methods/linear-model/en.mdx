## Linear in the Model Parameters

A linear model relates a known input <InlineMath math="t \in \mathbb{R}^k" /> to an observed response <InlineMath math="y \in \mathbb{R}^d" /> through unknown parameters <InlineMath math="x \in \mathbb{R}^n" />:

<BlockMath math="y=h(t)x,\qquad h(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}" />

The important word is **linear in the parameters**. For fixed <InlineMath math="t" />, the map from <InlineMath math="x" /> to <InlineMath math="y" /> is linear. The dependence on <InlineMath math="t" /> may contain powers, logarithms, sines, or other nonlinear basis functions.

## From measurements to a design matrix

Suppose we collect <InlineMath math="M" /> input-response pairs <InlineMath math="(t_i,y_i)" /> and model each observation as

<BlockMath math="y_i=h(t_i)x+\varepsilon_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,M" />

The error <InlineMath math="\varepsilon_i" /> contains measurement noise and model mismatch. Stacking all responses and model blocks gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="b=\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_M\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}" />
<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}h(t_1)\\\vdots\\h(t_M)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Md\times n}" />
<BlockMath math="b=Ax+\varepsilon" />
</MathContainer>

The matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> is the **design matrix**. When the observations do not satisfy one parameter vector exactly, estimate <InlineMath math="x" /> with least squares or another residual model. The data determine the parameters uniquely when <InlineMath math="A" /> has full column rank. The condition number of <InlineMath math="A" /> separately measures the numerical sensitivity of that estimate.

## Basis functions create many model shapes

Let <InlineMath math="\phi_1(t),\ldots,\phi_n(t)" /> be known basis functions. A scalar-response model can be written as

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y(t) &amp;=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_j\phi_j(t) \\ &amp;=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_1(t)&amp;\cdots&amp;\phi_n(t)\end{pmatrix}x \end{aligned}" />

Different choices of basis functions create different shapes while preserving linearity in <InlineMath math="x" />.

An affine line:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y&=a+bt\\&=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}" />

A polynomial of degree <InlineMath math="p" />:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y&=\sum_{j=0}^{p}a_jt^j\\&=\begin{pmatrix}1&t&\cdots&t^p\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\\\vdots\\a_p\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}" />

A Fourier basis:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y&=a_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}a_j\cos(jt)\\&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\sin(jt)\end{aligned}" />

An interaction between two inputs:

<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} y&=a+bt+cs+dts\\&=\begin{pmatrix}1&t&s&ts\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\\d\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}" />

Terms such as <InlineMath math="t^p" />, <InlineMath math="\sin(jt)" />, and <InlineMath math="ts" /> may be nonlinear in the inputs. They are known coefficients once the inputs are observed, so <Highlight>the model remains linear in its unknown parameters</Highlight>.

Take the observations <InlineMath math="(t,y)=(0,1),(1,3),(2,5)" /> and the basis <InlineMath math="1,t" />. Each observed input gives one row of the design matrix.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix}1&amp;0\\1&amp;1\\1&amp;2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\5\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="a=1,\qquad a+b=3\Rightarrow b=2" />
<BlockMath math="a+2b=1+2(2)=5" />
</MathContainer>

The third observation agrees with the first two: one parameter vector <InlineMath math="(a,b)=(1,2)" /> explains all three values.

Every violet line keeps <InlineMath math="s" /> fixed and every teal line keeps <InlineMath math="t" /> fixed, and each one is straight. The twist comes only from the term <InlineMath math="\tfrac{ts}2" />, which makes the slope in <InlineMath math="t" /> equal to <InlineMath math="\tfrac12+\tfrac s2" />. All four coefficients still enter linearly, so least squares can fit them with the design-matrix row <InlineMath math="(1,t,s,ts)" />.

## Multiple responses

If every input produces several responses, place their parameter vectors in a matrix <InlineMath math="B" />. For a row of basis values <InlineMath math="\phi(t)^{\mathsf T}" />,

<BlockMath math="y(t)^{\mathsf T}=\phi(t)^{\mathsf T}B" />

**Each column** of <InlineMath math="B" /> describes one response. This is still a linear model because every output is a linear combination of the same known basis values.

## The Ideal Gas Law

<BlockMath math="pV=nRT" />

At known temperature <InlineMath math="T" /> and volume <InlineMath math="V" />, solving for pressure gives

<BlockMath math="p=\frac{RT}{V}n" />

Pressure <InlineMath math="p" /> is linear in the **unknown amount of substance** <InlineMath math="n" />, measured in moles. The quantity <InlineMath math="n" /> is not itself the number of molecules. The number of specified entities is <InlineMath math="N=nN_A" />.

Repeated pressure measurements at several known <InlineMath math="(T_i,V_i)" /> values yield one design-matrix row <InlineMath math="RT_i/V_i" /> per observation.

## Local linearization of a nonlinear model

A genuinely nonlinear parameter model has the form

<BlockMath math="y=g(t,x)" />

In this model, parameters may occur inside exponentials, powers, or products with one another. Near a current parameter value <InlineMath math="x_0" />, a **first-order Taylor approximation** gives

<BlockMath math="g(t,x) \approx g(t,x_0) +J_xg(t,x_0)(x-x_0)" />

The Jacobian <InlineMath math="J_xg(t,x_0)" /> is known after choosing <InlineMath math="x_0" />, so the correction <InlineMath math="x-x_0" /> appears linearly. This approximation is accurate only near <InlineMath math="x_0" />. Iterative methods such as Gauss-Newton repeatedly update the linearization point.

## Checks before fitting

Before interpreting fitted parameters, verify that:

- the design matrix columns represent the intended basis functions and units
- the data contain enough **independent information** for the parameters, typically <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A)=n" />
- feature scales and the condition number do not make the solve numerically fragile
- the chosen residual loss matches the noise and outlier assumptions
- a local linearization is checked again against the original nonlinear model

A flexible basis can fit many shapes, but flexibility alone does not guarantee identifiability, numerical stability, or useful predictions.
