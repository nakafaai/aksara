## Normal Matrices

The complex spectral theorem states that a matrix can be diagonalized using an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors exactly when it is normal. In matrix form,

<BlockMath math="A\text{ is normal}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A=U\Lambda U^H" />

In this form, <InlineMath math="U" /> is unitary and <InlineMath math="\Lambda" /> is diagonal. The columns of <InlineMath math="U" /> form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Because <InlineMath math="U" /> is unitary, this change of basis preserves lengths and angles. In the real symmetric case, <InlineMath math="U" /> can be chosen real and orthogonal, so <InlineMath math="U^{-1}=U^T" />.

A complex matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}" /> is called normal if it satisfies the commutativity condition with its conjugate transpose:

<BlockMath math="A \cdot A^H = A^H \cdot A" />

This algebraic condition makes eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues orthogonal. Over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{C}" />, it also allows a unitary diagonalization.

## Special Properties of Eigenspaces of Normal Matrices

For a normal matrix, the null space of the matrix equals the null space of its conjugate transpose.

The null space (kernel) is the set of all vectors <InlineMath math="x" /> satisfying <InlineMath math="Ax=0" />. Normality gives the following norm identity for every <InlineMath math="x" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lVert Ax\rVert_2^2=x^HA^HAx" />
<BlockMath math="=x^HAA^Hx=\lVert A^Hx\rVert_2^2" />
</MathContainer>

<InlineMath math="Ax=0" /> if and only if <InlineMath math="A^Hx=0" />, so

<BlockMath math="\ker A=\ker A^H" />

Apply the same null-space identity to the normal matrix <InlineMath math="A-\lambda I" />. For every eigenvalue <InlineMath math="\lambda \in \mathbb{C}" />, this gives the corresponding eigenspace relation:

<BlockMath math="E_A(\lambda)=E_{A^H}(\overline{\lambda})" />

If <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" />, then <InlineMath math="A^H v=\overline{\lambda}v" />. The eigenvector is shared, while the eigenvalue is complex-conjugated.

This identity also proves orthogonality. If <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" /> and <InlineMath math="Aw=\mu w" /> with <InlineMath math="\lambda\neq\mu" />, then

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\overline{\lambda}v^Hw=(Av)^Hw=v^HA^Hw" />
<BlockMath math="=\overline{\mu}v^Hw\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v^Hw=0" />
</MathContainer>

## Hermitian and Unitary Matrices as Examples of Normal Matrices

Hermitian and unitary matrices are always normal. Their defining equations show why:

### Hermitian Matrices and Real Eigenvalues

For a Hermitian matrix, <InlineMath math="A^H=A" />, so both sides of the normality condition equal <InlineMath math="A^2" />.

Eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices are always real. If <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" /> with <InlineMath math="v\neq0" />, then Hermitian symmetry gives:

<BlockMath math="\lambda v^H v = v^HAv = v^HA^Hv = (Av)^Hv = \overline{\lambda}v^Hv \Rightarrow \lambda=\overline{\lambda}" />

The equality <InlineMath math="\lambda=\overline{\lambda}" /> says exactly that <InlineMath math="\lambda" /> is real.

### Worked Hermitian Example

Consider

<BlockMath math="H=\begin{pmatrix}2&i\\-i&2\end{pmatrix}" />

The conjugate transpose returns the same matrix, so <InlineMath math="H^H=H" />. Its characteristic polynomial is <InlineMath math="(2-\lambda)^2-1" />, which gives the real eigenvalues <InlineMath math="1" /> and <InlineMath math="3" />. Corresponding normalized eigenvectors are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="u_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}-i\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad u_3=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="U=(u_1\quad u_3),\qquad U^HHU=\operatorname{diag}(1,3)" />
</MathContainer>

The vectors are orthonormal because <InlineMath math="u_1^Hu_3=0" /> and both have norm <InlineMath math="1" />. This is the spectral theorem in a concrete <InlineMath math="2\times2" /> case.

### Unitary Matrices and Eigenvalues on the Unit Circle

Unitary matrices have the property <InlineMath math="A^H = A^{-1}" />. To prove that unitary matrices are also normal, we substitute into the definition and get <InlineMath math="A \cdot A^H = A \cdot A^{-1} = I = A^{-1} \cdot A = A^H \cdot A" />.

Eigenvalues of unitary matrices have magnitude <InlineMath math="1" />, so they lie on the unit circle in the complex plane. If <InlineMath math="Av=\lambda v" /> with <InlineMath math="v\neq0" />, unitarity preserves the norm:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\lVert v\rVert^2 = \lVert Av\rVert^2 = \lVert \lambda v\rVert^2 = |\lambda|^2\lVert v\rVert^2" />
<BlockMath math="v\neq0 \Rightarrow \lVert v\rVert^2>0 \Rightarrow |\lambda|^2=1 \Rightarrow |\lambda|=1" />
</MathContainer>

Every eigenvalue of a unitary matrix has modulus <InlineMath math="1" />. Geometrically, unitary transformations preserve vector lengths, and their eigenvalues lie on the unit circle.
