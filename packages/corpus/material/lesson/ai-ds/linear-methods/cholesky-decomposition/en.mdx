## LU Decomposition for Positive Definite Matrices

Let <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}" /> be symmetric positive definite, meaning

<BlockMath math="x^TAx>0\qquad\text{for every }x\neq0" />

This condition makes factorization especially well behaved. [LU decomposition](/en/subjects/ai-ds/linear-methods/lu-decomposition) can proceed without row swaps, and every pivot produced by exact Gaussian elimination is positive.

This means we obtain factorization in the form <InlineMath math="A = L \cdot U" />, where the diagonal elements of <InlineMath math="U" /> are positive pivot elements for all diagonal indices.

Since <InlineMath math="A = A^T" />, we also have:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A = A^T = (L \cdot U)^T = (L \cdot D \cdot \tilde{U})^T = \tilde{U}^T \cdot D \cdot L^T" />
</MathContainer>

where <InlineMath math="\tilde{U}" /> is a matrix whose main diagonal is normalized to <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="D" /> is a diagonal matrix:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde{U} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & u_{12}/u_{11} & \cdots & u_{1n}/u_{11} \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ & & 1 & u_{n-1,n}/u_{n-1,n-1} \\ 0 & & & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="D = \begin{pmatrix} u_{11} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & u_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

When <InlineMath math="L" /> and <InlineMath math="\tilde U" /> have unit diagonals, this LU decomposition is unique. Comparing the two factorizations gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A = L \cdot U = \tilde{U}^T \cdot (D \cdot L^T)" />
<BlockMath math="L = \tilde{U}^T \quad \text{and} \quad U = D \cdot L^T" />
</MathContainer>

If we define:

<BlockMath math="D^{\frac{1}{2}} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{u_{11}} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \sqrt{u_{nn}} \end{pmatrix}" />

then <InlineMath math="D^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot D^{\frac{1}{2}} = D" />.

## Cholesky Decomposition

Every real symmetric positive definite matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" /> has a unique Cholesky factor with positive diagonal entries:

<BlockMath math="A = L \cdot D \cdot L^T = \tilde{L} \cdot \tilde{L}^T" />

where <InlineMath math="\tilde{L} = L \cdot D^{\frac{1}{2}}" /> is nonsingular and lower triangular. The positive square roots of the pivots fix the signs of its diagonal and make the factor unique.

The computation of matrix <InlineMath math="\tilde{L}" /> is performed with:

<BlockMath math="\tilde{L} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{l}_{11} & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots \\ \tilde{l}_{n1} & \cdots & \tilde{l}_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />

based on the relationship <InlineMath math="\tilde{L} \cdot \tilde{L}^T = A" />. The following algorithm produces the Cholesky factor.

## Cholesky Algorithm

Given a positive definite matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{11} := \sqrt{a_{11}}" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{j1} := \frac{a_{j1}}{\tilde{l}_{11}}, \quad j = 2, \ldots, n" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="i = 2, \ldots, n" />:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{ii} := \sqrt{a_{ii} - \tilde{l}_{i1}^2 - \tilde{l}_{i2}^2 - \ldots - \tilde{l}_{i,i-1}^2}" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{ji} := \tilde{l}_{ii}^{-1} \cdot \left( a_{ji} - \tilde{l}_{j1}\tilde{l}_{i1} - \tilde{l}_{j2}\tilde{l}_{i2} - \ldots - \tilde{l}_{j,i-1}\tilde{l}_{i,i-1} \right)" />
</MathContainer>

for <InlineMath math="j = i + 1, \ldots, n" />.

The expression under each square root must remain positive. A zero or negative value is therefore not something to hide with an absolute value: it shows that the input is not positive definite, or that finite-precision arithmetic requires a more careful numerical treatment.

After running the algorithm, we obtain the lower triangular Cholesky factor:

<BlockMath math="\tilde{L} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{l}_{11} & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots \\ \tilde{l}_{n1} & \cdots & \tilde{l}_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />

## Worked Example

Factor the matrix

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}" />

The entries of the lower triangular factor follow directly from the algorithm:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{11}=\sqrt4=2" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{21}=\frac2{\tilde l_{11}}=1" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{22}=\sqrt{5-\tilde l_{21}^2}=2" />
</MathContainer>

Thus

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde L=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde L\tilde L^T=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}=A" />
</MathContainer>

Once the factor is known, solving <InlineMath math="Ax=b" /> needs two triangular solves rather than an inverse: first solve <InlineMath math="\tilde Ly=b" />, then solve <InlineMath math="\tilde L^Tx=y" />.

## Cholesky Algorithm Complexity

The Cholesky algorithm for computing the Cholesky factor <InlineMath math="\tilde{L}" /> from <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" /> requires:

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{3}n^3 + O(n^2)\quad\text{flops}" />

arithmetic operations.

The leading count contains about <InlineMath math="n^3/6" /> multiplications and <InlineMath math="n^3/6" /> additions. Together they give about <InlineMath math="n^3/3" /> floating-point operations, roughly half the <InlineMath math="2n^3/3" /> leading cost of LU factorization for a general dense matrix. Cholesky saves work by using symmetry and storing only one triangular half of the factorization.
