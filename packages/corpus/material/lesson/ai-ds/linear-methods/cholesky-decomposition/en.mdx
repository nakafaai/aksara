## Why No Row Swaps Are Needed

Let <InlineMath math="A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}" /> be symmetric positive definite, meaning

<BlockMath math="x^TAx>0\qquad\text{for every }x\neq0" />

For a matrix with this condition, [LU decomposition](/en/subjects/ai-ds/linear-methods/lu-decomposition) can proceed without row swaps. In exact arithmetic, **every elimination pivot is positive**.

This means we obtain factorization in the form <InlineMath math="A = L \cdot U" />, where the diagonal elements of <InlineMath math="U" /> are positive pivot elements for all diagonal indices.

Since <InlineMath math="A = A^T" />, we also have:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} A &amp;= A^T \\ &amp;= (L \cdot U)^T \\ &amp;= (L \cdot D \cdot \tilde{U})^T \\ &amp;= \tilde{U}^T \cdot D \cdot L^T \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Here, <InlineMath math="\tilde{U}" /> is a matrix whose main diagonal is normalized to <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="D" /> is a diagonal matrix:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="\tilde{U} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 &amp; \frac{u_{12}}{u_{11}} &amp; \cdots &amp; \frac{u_{1n}}{u_{11}} \\ &amp; \ddots &amp; \ddots &amp; \vdots \\ &amp; &amp; 1 &amp; \frac{u_{n-1,n}}{u_{n-1,n-1}} \\ 0 &amp; &amp; &amp; 1 \end{pmatrix}" />
  <BlockMath math="D = \begin{pmatrix} u_{11} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & u_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

When <InlineMath math="L" /> and <InlineMath math="\tilde U" /> have unit diagonals, this <InlineMath math="\mathrm{LU}" /> decomposition is unique. Comparing the two factorizations gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A = L \cdot U = \tilde{U}^T \cdot (D \cdot L^T)" />
<BlockMath math="L = \tilde{U}^T \quad \text{and} \quad U = D \cdot L^T" />
</MathContainer>

Define the diagonal square-root matrix as follows.

<BlockMath math="D^{\frac{1}{2}} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{u_{11}} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \sqrt{u_{nn}} \end{pmatrix}" />

This matrix satisfies <InlineMath math="D^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot D^{\frac{1}{2}} = D" />.

This chain explains why a positive definite matrix factors without row swaps.

    flowchart LR

## The Unique Lower Triangular Factor

Every real symmetric positive definite matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" /> has a unique Cholesky factor with positive diagonal entries:

<BlockMath math="A = L \cdot D \cdot L^T = \tilde{L} \cdot \tilde{L}^T" />

The matrix <InlineMath math="\tilde{L} = L \cdot D^{\frac{1}{2}}" /> is nonsingular and lower triangular. The positive square roots of the pivots fix the signs of its diagonal and <Highlight>make the Cholesky factor unique</Highlight>.

The computation of matrix <InlineMath math="\tilde{L}" /> is performed with:

<BlockMath math="\tilde L=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde l_{11}&amp;0&amp;\cdots&amp;0\\\tilde l_{21}&amp;\tilde l_{22}&amp;\cdots&amp;0\\\vdots&amp;\vdots&amp;\ddots&amp;\vdots\\\tilde l_{n1}&amp;\tilde l_{n2}&amp;\cdots&amp;\tilde l_{nn}\end{pmatrix}" />

This computation uses the relationship <InlineMath math="\tilde{L} \cdot \tilde{L}^T = A" />. The following algorithm produces the Cholesky factor.

## Cholesky Algorithm

Given a positive definite matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{11} := \sqrt{a_{11}}" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde{l}_{j1} := \frac{a_{j1}}{\tilde{l}_{11}}, \quad j = 2, \ldots, n" />
</MathContainer>

For <InlineMath math="i = 2, \ldots, n" />:

<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{ii}:=\sqrt{a_{ii}-\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\tilde l_{ik}^2}" />
Compute the remaining entry first:

<MathContainer>
  <BlockMath math="r_{ji}=a_{ji}-\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\tilde l_{jk}\tilde l_{ik}" />

  <BlockMath math="\tilde l_{ji}:=\tilde l_{ii}^{-1}\cdot r_{ji}" />
</MathContainer>

The second recurrence applies for <InlineMath math="j = i + 1, \ldots, n" />.

The **expression under each square root** must remain positive. A zero or negative value means that the input is not positive definite or that rounding has affected a nearly singular problem. Replacing that value with its absolute value changes the recurrence and can produce a factor that does not satisfy <InlineMath math="A=LL^T" />.

After running the algorithm, we obtain the lower triangular Cholesky factor:

<BlockMath math="\tilde L=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde l_{11}&amp;0&amp;\cdots&amp;0\\\tilde l_{21}&amp;\tilde l_{22}&amp;\cdots&amp;0\\\vdots&amp;\vdots&amp;\ddots&amp;\vdots\\\tilde l_{n1}&amp;\tilde l_{n2}&amp;\cdots&amp;\tilde l_{nn}\end{pmatrix}" />

This diagram sums up the order of calculation for one column <InlineMath math="j" />.

## Factoring a Two by Two Positive Definite Matrix

Factor the matrix

<BlockMath math="A=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}" />

The entries of the lower triangular factor follow directly from the algorithm:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{11}=\sqrt4=2" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{21}=\frac2{\tilde l_{11}}=1" />
<BlockMath math="\tilde l_{22}=\sqrt{5-\tilde l_{21}^2}=2" />
</MathContainer>

Thus

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\tilde L=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \tilde L\tilde L^T &amp;=\begin{pmatrix}2&amp;0\\1&amp;2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&amp;1\\0&amp;2\end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;=\begin{pmatrix}4&amp;2\\2&amp;5\end{pmatrix} \\ &amp;=A \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

Once the factor is known, solve <InlineMath math="Ax=b" /> through **two triangular systems**: first <InlineMath math="\tilde Ly=b" />, then <InlineMath math="\tilde L^Tx=y" />. This avoids computing a matrix inverse.

The factor <InlineMath math="L" /> from this example maps the unit square onto the parallelogram spanned by its columns. Look for the triangular shape in the scene: the first column <InlineMath math="\ell_1=(2,1)^T" /> may tilt, but the second column <InlineMath math="\ell_2=(0,2)^T" /> points straight up, because the top right entry of <InlineMath math="L" /> is zero. The area of the parallelogram is <InlineMath math="\det L=2\cdot2=4" />, and because <InlineMath math="A=LL^T" />, that area equals <InlineMath math="\sqrt{\det A}=\sqrt{16}" />.

## Cholesky Algorithm Complexity

The Cholesky algorithm for computing the Cholesky factor <InlineMath math="\tilde{L}" /> from <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}" /> requires:

<BlockMath math="\frac{1}{3}n^3 + O(n^2)\quad\text{flops}" />

This expression gives the number of arithmetic operations.

The leading count contains about <InlineMath math="n^3/6" /> multiplications and <InlineMath math="n^3/6" /> additions. Together they give about <InlineMath math="n^3/3" /> floating-point operations, roughly half the <InlineMath math="2n^3/3" /> leading cost of <InlineMath math="\mathrm{LU}" /> factorization for a general dense matrix. Cholesky saves work by **using symmetry** and storing only one triangular half of the factorization.
