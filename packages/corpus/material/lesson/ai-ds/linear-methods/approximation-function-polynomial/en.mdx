## Why Approximate a Function

A function may be expensive to evaluate or too complicated for a later calculation. We then choose a simpler family of functions and ask a precise question: which member of that family is closest to the original function?

Let <InlineMath math="C([a,b])" /> denote the real vector space of continuous functions on <InlineMath math="[a,b]" />. We measure the error across the whole interval.

## Inner Product and L2 Error

For <InlineMath math="f,g\in C([a,b])" />, define

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx" />
<BlockMath math="\|f\|_2=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}=\left(\int_a^b|f(x)|^2\,dx\right)^{1/2}" />
</MathContainer>

This inner product turns <InlineMath math="C([a,b])" /> into an inner-product space. The displayed norm is the <InlineMath math="L^2" /> norm. Dividing it by the square root of the interval length gives the root-mean-square value:

<BlockMath math="\operatorname{RMS}(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b-a}}\|f\|_2" />

The quantity <InlineMath math="\|f-g\|_2" /> combines the error across the interval. Positive and negative errors cannot cancel because the difference is squared inside the integral.

## Best Approximation as an Orthogonal Projection

Let <InlineMath math="S\subset C([a,b])" /> be a finite-dimensional subspace. The best approximation of <InlineMath math="f" /> in <InlineMath math="S" /> is the unique function <InlineMath math="g\in S" /> satisfying

<BlockMath math="\|f-g\|_2=\min_{\varphi\in S}\|f-\varphi\|_2" />

Geometrically, <InlineMath math="g" /> is the orthogonal projection of <InlineMath math="f" /> onto <InlineMath math="S" />. The residual <InlineMath math="r=f-g" /> must be orthogonal to every function in the approximation space:

<BlockMath math="\langle f-g,s\rangle=0\qquad\text{for every }s\in S" />

If <InlineMath math="\{\phi_0,\ldots,\phi_n\}" /> is a basis of <InlineMath math="S" /> and <InlineMath math="g=\sum_{j=0}^n c_j\phi_j" />, these orthogonality conditions become the normal equations

<BlockMath math="\sum_{j=0}^n\langle\phi_j,\phi_i\rangle c_j=\langle f,\phi_i\rangle,\qquad i=0,\ldots,n" />

## Approximating a Quadratic by a Line

Approximate <InlineMath math="f(x)=x^2" /> on <InlineMath math="[-1,1]" /> by a function in <InlineMath math="S=\operatorname{span}\{1,x\}" />. Write <InlineMath math="g(x)=c_0+c_1x" />. Orthogonality to the two basis functions gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\int_{-1}^{1}\left(x^2-c_0-c_1x\right)\,dx=0" />
<BlockMath math="\int_{-1}^{1}x\left(x^2-c_0-c_1x\right)\,dx=0" />
</MathContainer>

The odd terms integrate to zero on the symmetric interval. The second equation gives <InlineMath math="c_1=0" />, and the first gives <InlineMath math="c_0=1/3" />. Therefore

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="g(x)=\frac13" />
<BlockMath math="\|f-g\|_2^2=\int_{-1}^{1}\left(x^2-\frac13\right)^2\,dx=\frac8{45}" />
</MathContainer>

The best line is horizontal. That conclusion is not a guess: the symmetry of <InlineMath math="x^2" /> forces the coefficient of the odd basis function <InlineMath math="x" /> to vanish.

## Polynomial Approximation Spaces

For algebraic polynomials of degree at most <InlineMath math="n" />, use

<BlockMath math="P_n([a,b])=\operatorname{span}\{1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^n\}" />

Increasing <InlineMath math="n" /> enlarges the approximation space, so the smallest possible <InlineMath math="L^2" /> error cannot increase. In computation, orthogonal polynomial bases are often preferable to raw powers because they produce better-conditioned systems.

## Trigonometric Approximation Spaces

For a periodic function with period <InlineMath math="\omega>0" />, a useful space is

<BlockMath math="T_n(\omega)=\left\{\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^n\left[a_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi ix}{\omega}\right)+b_i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi ix}{\omega}\right)\right]:a_i,b_i\in\mathbb{R}\right\}" />

Over one full period, these sine and cosine basis functions are mutually orthogonal. Their coefficients can therefore be computed by projecting the target function onto each basis function, which is the finite-dimensional idea behind Fourier approximation.

| Choice of <InlineMath math="S" /> | Best suited for | What determines the coefficients |
|---|---|---|
| <InlineMath math="P_n([a,b])" /> | Smooth behavior on a finite interval | Orthogonality of the residual to polynomial basis functions |
| <InlineMath math="T_n(\omega)" /> | Periodic behavior | Orthogonality of the residual to sine and cosine basis functions |

> A best approximation is the orthogonal projection defined by a chosen function space, interval, and norm. Visual similarity alone does not establish it.
