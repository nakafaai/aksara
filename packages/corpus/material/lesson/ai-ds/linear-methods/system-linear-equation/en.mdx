## Overdetermined Linear Equation Systems

Suppose we want to fit a curve to measured data. We often have more observations than model parameters. Writing one equation for each observation then produces an **overdetermined linear system**.

A system is overdetermined when the number <InlineMath math="m" /> of equations is greater than the number <InlineMath math="n" /> of unknowns, so <InlineMath math="m > n" />. An exact solution can still exist. With measurement data, however, the points usually do not lie exactly on the chosen model.

## Real Example with Quadratic Polynomial Model

Consider <InlineMath math="7" /> data points that we want to fit with a quadratic polynomial.

The observations are:

| <InlineMath math="i" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> | <InlineMath math="4" /> | <InlineMath math="5" /> | <InlineMath math="6" /> | <InlineMath math="7" /> |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| <InlineMath math="t_i" /> | <InlineMath math="-3" /> | <InlineMath math="-2" /> | <InlineMath math="-1" /> | <InlineMath math="0" /> | <InlineMath math="1" /> | <InlineMath math="2" /> | <InlineMath math="3" /> |
| <InlineMath math="y_i" /> | <InlineMath math="-2.2" /> | <InlineMath math="-4.2" /> | <InlineMath math="-4.2" /> | <InlineMath math="-1.8" /> | <InlineMath math="1.8" /> | <InlineMath math="8.2" /> | <InlineMath math="15.8" /> |

We seek a parabola of the form:

<BlockMath math="y = a_2 \cdot t^2 + a_1 \cdot t + a_0" />

The unknowns are the <InlineMath math="3" /> parameters <InlineMath math="a_2" /> (quadratic coefficient), <InlineMath math="a_1" /> (linear coefficient), and <InlineMath math="a_0" /> (constant term).

## Setting Up the System of Equations

Substituting each pair <InlineMath math="(t_i,y_i)" /> into the polynomial gives one equation. The <InlineMath math="7" /> observations therefore give <InlineMath math="7" /> equations for only <InlineMath math="3" /> unknown coefficients.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot (-3)^2 + a_1 \cdot (-3) + a_0 = -2.2" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot (-2)^2 + a_1 \cdot (-2) + a_0 = -4.2" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot (-1)^2 + a_1 \cdot (-1) + a_0 = -4.2" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot 0^2 + a_1 \cdot 0 + a_0 = -1.8" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot 1^2 + a_1 \cdot 1 + a_0 = 1.8" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot 2^2 + a_1 \cdot 2 + a_0 = 8.2" />
<BlockMath math="a_2 \cdot 3^2 + a_1 \cdot 3 + a_0 = 15.8" />
</MathContainer>

Evaluating the powers of each <InlineMath math="t_i" /> turns the system into:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="9a_2 - 3a_1 + a_0 = -2.2" />
<BlockMath math="4a_2 - 2a_1 + a_0 = -4.2" />
<BlockMath math="1a_2 - 1a_1 + a_0 = -4.2" />
<BlockMath math="0a_2 + 0a_1 + a_0 = -1.8" />
<BlockMath math="1a_2 + 1a_1 + a_0 = 1.8" />
<BlockMath math="4a_2 + 2a_1 + a_0 = 8.2" />
<BlockMath math="9a_2 + 3a_1 + a_0 = 15.8" />
</MathContainer>

## Matrix Form

The system of equations above can be written in matrix form <InlineMath math="A \cdot x = b" />.

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} 9 & -3 & 1 \\ 4 & -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 9 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_2 \\ a_1 \\ a_0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2.2 \\ -4.2 \\ -4.2 \\ -1.8 \\ 1.8 \\ 8.2 \\ 15.8 \end{pmatrix}" />

For a quadratic model fitted to <InlineMath math="m" /> data points, the same construction has the general form:

<BlockMath math="\begin{pmatrix} t_1^2 & t_1 & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ t_m^2 & t_m & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_2 \\ a_1 \\ a_0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_m \end{pmatrix}" />

## Why There Is No Exact Solution

In this example, matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> has size <InlineMath math="7 \times 3" /> and the coefficient vector <InlineMath math="x" /> has size <InlineMath math="3 \times 1" />. We therefore have <InlineMath math="7" /> equations for <InlineMath math="3" /> unknowns.

The dimensions alone do not decide whether an exact solution exists. The ranks do.

The three columns of <InlineMath math="A" /> are linearly independent. Indeed, a polynomial <InlineMath math="c_2t^2+c_1t+c_0" /> of degree at most <InlineMath math="2" /> that vanishes at all seven distinct values of <InlineMath math="t_i" /> must be the zero polynomial. Therefore <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A)=3" />.

Appending <InlineMath math="b" /> forms the augmented matrix <InlineMath math="(A\mid b)" />. The first four rows already give the nonzero minor

<BlockMath math="\det\begin{pmatrix}9&-3&1&-2.2\\4&-2&1&-4.2\\1&-1&1&-4.2\\0&0&1&-1.8\end{pmatrix}=-\frac45" />

so <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A\mid b)=4" />.

Because <InlineMath math="\operatorname{rank}(A) \ne \operatorname{rank}(A\mid b)" />, the system **has no exact solution**. No single quadratic polynomial passes through all <InlineMath math="7" /> observations.

## Solution with Least Squares

When an overdetermined system has no exact solution, we can instead find the polynomial that is closest to the observations in the **least-squares** sense.

For observation <InlineMath math="i" />, the vertical residual is <InlineMath math="r_i = y_i-(a_2t_i^2+a_1t_i+a_0)" />. Least squares chooses the coefficients that minimize the sum of the squared residuals:

<BlockMath math="\min_{a_2,a_1,a_0}\sum_{i=1}^{7}\left[y_i-(a_2t_i^2+a_1t_i+a_0)\right]^2" />

This criterion penalizes large misses more strongly and produces one best-fitting parabola because <InlineMath math="A" /> has full column rank. The normal equations are

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}196&0&28\\0&28&0\\28&0&7\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A^Tb=\begin{pmatrix}136\\\frac{424}{5}\\\frac{67}{5}\end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

Solving them gives

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\hat x=\begin{pmatrix}\hat a_2\\\hat a_1\\\hat a_0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{103}{105}\\\frac{106}{35}\\-\frac{211}{105}\end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\hat y(t)=\frac{103}{105}t^2+\frac{106}{35}t-\frac{211}{105}" />
</MathContainer>

The fitted values do not equal every observation, which is expected. The residual vector is

<BlockMath math="r=b-A\hat x=\begin{pmatrix}\frac1{15}\\-\frac2{35}\\-\frac17\\\frac{22}{105}\\-\frac15\\\frac8{35}\\-\frac{11}{105}\end{pmatrix}" />

and it satisfies

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A^Tr=0" />
<BlockMath math="\lVert r\rVert_2^2=\frac{92}{525}\approx0.17524" />
</MathContainer>

The equation <InlineMath math="A^Tr=0" /> says that the remaining error is orthogonal to every column of <InlineMath math="A" />. Geometrically, <InlineMath math="A\hat x" /> is the orthogonal projection of <InlineMath math="b" /> onto the column space of <InlineMath math="A" />.

Overdetermined systems are common in engineering and science because experiments often provide many measurements for a model with relatively few parameters. Least squares uses all of those measurements without pretending that noisy data fit the model exactly.
