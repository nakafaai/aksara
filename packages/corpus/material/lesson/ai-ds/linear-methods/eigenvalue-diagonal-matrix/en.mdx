## Diagonal Matrices and Their Special Properties

For diagonal and triangular matrices, every eigenvalue can be <Highlight>read directly from the main diagonal</Highlight>, with algebraic multiplicity. The matrix may have one of these forms:

The three forms below represent a diagonal, upper triangular, and lower triangular matrix <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}" />.

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & a_{22} & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & a_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & * & \cdots & * \\ 0 & a_{22} & \cdots & * \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & a_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ * & a_{22} & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\ * & * & \cdots & a_{nn} \end{pmatrix}" />
</MathContainer>

For all three forms, the eigenvalues are the main diagonal entries:

<BlockMath math="\lambda_1 = a_{11}, \ldots, \lambda_n = a_{nn}" />

Why is this true? Since <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t) = \det(A - t \cdot I) = (a_{11} - t) \cdots (a_{nn} - t)" /> with roots <InlineMath math="a_{11}, \ldots, a_{nn}" />.

No full determinant expansion or root-finding procedure is needed for a triangular matrix.

A diagonal matrix scales each coordinate axis by its own diagonal entry. For <InlineMath math="D=\operatorname{diag}(2,\tfrac12)" />, the vectors <InlineMath math="e_1" /> and <InlineMath math="e_2" /> are eigenvectors with eigenvalues <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="\tfrac12" />, while a mixed vector such as <InlineMath math="(1,1)" /> changes direction.

## Upper and Lower Triangular Matrices

Triangular matrices have the same property as diagonal matrices. For both upper and lower triangular matrices, the eigenvalues are still the main diagonal entries.

This happens because when we calculate <InlineMath math="\det(A - tI)" />, the entries above or below the main diagonal don't affect the determinant calculation. The triangular structure allows the determinant to be computed as the **product of diagonal entries**.

For example, off-diagonal entries can change while the **same eigenvalues** remain:

<BlockMath math="T=\begin{pmatrix}2&4&1\\0&2&3\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \chi_T(t)=(2-t)^2(-1-t)" />

Thus <InlineMath math="2" /> occurs twice and <InlineMath math="-1" /> once. This gives the eigenvalues, but does not by itself prove diagonalizability.

For <InlineMath math="T" /> above, the eigenvalues <InlineMath math="2" /> and <InlineMath math="-1" /> can be read straight from the diagonal. Rotate this three-dimensional scene: the vector <InlineMath math="e_1" /> is only stretched by two along its own axis, because the first column of <InlineMath math="T" /> is <InlineMath math="(2,0,0)^T" />. The vector <InlineMath math="v=(1,-1,1)^T" /> satisfies <InlineMath math="Tv=-v" />, so its image points exactly the other way. The entries <InlineMath math="4" />, <InlineMath math="1" />, and <InlineMath math="3" /> above the diagonal change the eigenvectors, but they never change the eigenvalues.

## Computing Eigenvalues of Nontriangular Matrices

The diagonal rule is special to triangular matrices. Once a matrix is no longer triangular, the eigenvalues stay hidden in the characteristic polynomial, so the work moves from reading diagonal entries to **solving for roots**.

### Complex Eigenvalues

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1-t & -1 \\ 1 & 1-t \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= (1-t) \cdot (1-t) - 1 \cdot (-1) \\ &amp;= t^2 - 2t + 2 \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

<BlockMath math="t^2-2t+2=0\iff(t-1)^2=-1\iff t=1\pm i" />

**The roots of this characteristic polynomial** are <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1 + i" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 1 - i" />.

### Zero Eigenvalues

Consider the matrix:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

Its characteristic polynomial is:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1-t & -i \\ i & 1-t \end{pmatrix}" />
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned}  &amp;= (1-t) \cdot (1-t) - i \cdot (-i) \\ &amp;= t^2 - 2t \end{aligned}" />
</MathContainer>

**The roots of this characteristic polynomial** are <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 0" />.

The eigenvalues <InlineMath math="1\pm i=\sqrt2\,e^{\pm i\pi/4}" /> have modulus <InlineMath math="\sqrt2" /> and angle <InlineMath math="45^\circ" />. Apply <InlineMath math="A" /> to <InlineMath math="e_1" /> again and again, and every step rotates the vector by that angle and stretches it by that modulus.

## Characteristic Polynomial Factorization

When <InlineMath math="A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}" /> has eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n" /> **counted with algebraic multiplicity**, its characteristic polynomial factors as:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = (\lambda_1 - t) \cdots (\lambda_n - t)" />

Let <InlineMath math="\sigma(A)" /> denote the finite set of distinct eigenvalues of <InlineMath math="A" />. Their algebraic multiplicities sum to <InlineMath math="n" />:

<BlockMath math="\sum_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} \mu_A(\lambda) = n" />

In a more compact form:

<BlockMath math="\chi_A(t) = \prod_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} (\lambda - t)^{\mu_A(\lambda)}" />

This property holds naturally for complex eigenvalues of matrices with real entries. Eigenvalues can be real numbers or complex conjugate pairs.

## Relationship Between Determinant and Trace

If the characteristic polynomial <InlineMath math="\chi_A(t)" /> splits into linear factors over <InlineMath math="\mathbb{K}" />, the matrix <InlineMath math="A" /> has <InlineMath math="n" /> eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n" /> there, counted with multiplicity. Their sum and product satisfy:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\det A = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i" />
<BlockMath math="\text{tr } A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i" />
</MathContainer>

**The determinant is the product** of all eigenvalues, and **the trace is the sum** of all eigenvalues.

Verify with our previous examples:

For the following matrix with eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 1 + i" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 1 - i" />:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

We can check the eigenvalues using the determinant and trace:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det A &amp;= 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot (-1) \\ &amp;= 2 \\ &amp;= \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{tr } A = 1 + 1 = 2 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2" />
</MathContainer>

For the following matrix with eigenvalues <InlineMath math="\lambda_1 = 2" /> and <InlineMath math="\lambda_2 = 0" />:

<BlockMath math="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}" />

We can check the eigenvalues using the determinant and trace:

<MathContainer>
<BlockMath math="\begin{aligned} \det A &amp;= 1 \cdot 1 - i \cdot (-i) \\ &amp;= 0 \\ &amp;= \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \end{aligned}" />
<BlockMath math="\text{tr } A = 1 + 1 = 2 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2" />
</MathContainer>

These identities are useful checks, but they do not determine every eigenvalue of a general matrix when <InlineMath math="n > 2" />. One immediate consequence is:

<BlockMath math="\det A = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 0 \in \sigma(A)" />

So a square matrix is singular exactly when zero is one of its eigenvalues.
